\section{Bounded \texorpdfstring{$C^1$}{C1} domains and why the definition in the script is so terrible}
\label{sec:bounded_c1_domains}

% Source: Corsin Nick/Class Notes/Week 10.pdf, p. 6

\begin{definition}[Bounded $C^k$ domain]
\label{def:bounded_ck_domain}
Given $k \geq 1$, a bounded open set $\Omega \subset \mathbb{R}^n$ is called a
\newterm{bounded $C^k$ domain} if
\begin{enumerate}[label=\textbf{(\alph*)}]
  \item $M := \partial\Omega$ is an $(n-1)$-dimensional submanifold of class $C^k$, and
  \item $\Omega$ lies locally on one side of $M$: every $p \in M$ has an open neighbourhood $V$
    with a function $g \in C^k(V)$ such that $\nabla g \neq 0$ on $V$ and
    $\Omega \cap V = \{x \in V : g(x) < 0\}$.
\end{enumerate}
\end{definition}

Citing the 2024 lecture notes, Corsin builds up the machinery needed to actually \emph{use}
this definition. To define \newterm{flux} we need the concept of interior and exterior
normal vectors. For $\Omega$ as above, a normal vector $v \in N_p(\partial\Omega)$ is
\newterm{interior} if
\[p + hv \in \Omega \quad \text{for all } h>0 \text{ small},\]
and \newterm{exterior} if
\[p + hv \in \mathbb{R}^n\setminus\Omega \quad \text{for all } h>0 \text{ small}.\]

A \newterm{Gauss map} is a continuous map of unit normal vectors on $\partial\Omega$. There
exists a unique interior and exterior Gauss map; write
\[\nu \in C^0(\partial\Omega, \mathbb{S}^{n-1}) \text{ such that } \nu(p) \text{ is exterior
  normal}\]
for the exterior unit normal map.

% Generator: Claude (xhigh)
Condition \textbf{(b)} is what makes this true. With $V$ and $g$ as there,
$\partial\Omega \cap V = \{x \in V : g(x) = 0\}$, so $\nabla g(p)$ spans the normal line
$N_p(\partial\Omega)$, and the unit normal $\nabla g(p)/\lvert\nabla g(p)\rvert$ is exterior:
$g$ increases in that direction, so $g(p+hv) > 0$ and $p + hv \notin \Omega$ for small $h > 0$.
Its negative is interior for the same reason. A normal line holds only these two unit vectors,
so $\nu = \nabla g/\lvert\nabla g\rvert$ near $p$, whichever $g$ was chosen, and this is
continuous.

\begin{remark}[Why a smooth boundary is not enough]
\label{rem:c1_domain_needs_one_side}
Condition \textbf{(a)} alone admits sets on which none of the above makes sense. Take
\[\Omega := B_1(0) \setminus \partial B_{1/2}(0) \subseteq \mathbb{R}^3,\]
the open unit ball with the sphere of radius $\tfrac12$ removed. It is bounded and open, and
$\partial\Omega = \partial B_1(0) \cup \partial B_{1/2}(0)$ is a smooth surface, so
\textbf{(a)} holds. But $\Omega$ lies on \emph{both} sides of the inner sphere: at a point $p$
of it, both unit normals $\pm 2p$ are interior. There is no exterior normal at $p$, hence no
exterior Gauss map, and the flux of \cref{def:flux} is undefined.

No choice of normal on the inner sphere repairs this. For $F(x) := x$, with $\divg F = 3$, the
volume side of the divergence theorem (\cref{thm:gauss_divergence}) is
$\int_\Omega 3\,d\vol_3 = 4\pi$, the removed sphere being a null set. The outer sphere alone
already contributes $\int_{\partial B_1(0)}\langle x,x\rangle\,d\vol_2 = 4\pi$ to the boundary
side, and the inner sphere adds $\pm\tfrac12\vol_2\big(\partial B_{1/2}(0)\big) = \pm\tfrac{\pi}{2}
\neq 0$, whichever unit normal it is given.

Condition \textbf{(b)} excludes the example. Since $\nabla g(p) \neq 0$, it puts points with
$g > 0$, which lie outside $\overline\Omega$, arbitrarily close to every boundary point, and
near the inner sphere there are none.
\end{remark}

\begin{ainote}
Corsin's notes state \cref{def:bounded_ck_domain} with condition \textbf{(a)} alone and then
assert that a unique interior and exterior Gauss map exists, which
\cref{rem:c1_domain_needs_one_side} refutes. Condition \textbf{(b)} has been added here so that
the assertion, and with it the flux and the divergence theorem built on it, holds. The domains to
which this document applies those results all satisfy it.
\end{ainote}

\begin{theorem}[Jordan--Brouwer separation theorem]
\label{thm:jordan_brouwer}
If $M \subseteq \mathbb{R}^{n+1}$ is an $n$-dimensional compact and connected submanifold,
then $\mathbb{R}^{n+1}\setminus M$ has exactly two connected components, an
\newterm{inside} and an \newterm{outside}.
\end{theorem}
