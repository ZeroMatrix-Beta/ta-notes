\begin{theorem}[Mean value theorem]
\label{thm:mean_value_theorem}
Let $f : U \to \mathbb{R}$ be differentiable, $x \in U$ and $h \in \mathbb{R}^n$ with
$x+th \in U$ for all $t \in [0,1]$. Then there is $t_0 \in (0,1)$ such that, writing
$\xi := x+t_0h$,
\[f(x+h)-f(x) = \partial_hf(\xi).\]
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
Apply the one-variable mean value theorem to $\varphi(t) := f(x+th)$ on $[0,1]$, which is
differentiable with $\varphi'(t) = \partial_hf(x+th)$ by the chain rule along a path
(\cref{sec:chain_rule_along_path}). It gives $t_0 \in (0,1)$ with
$\varphi(1)-\varphi(0) = \varphi'(t_0)$, which is the claim.
\end{proof}

\begin{importantremark}[It fails for vector-valued $f$]
\label{rem:mvt_fails_vector_valued}
The hypothesis \qt{$f : U \to \mathbb{R}$} is essential. \textbf{The theorem is false the moment
the target is $\mathbb{R}^m$ with $m \geq 2$}, and this is exactly what \cref{ex:4.5} below
probes.
% Corrected: this pointed at \cref{ex:4.6}, which applies the *scalar* theorem to $u(x+t\nu)$
% and never leaves $\mathbb{R}$. The problem that probes the vector-valued failure is 4.5 ---
% which was not in the built document when the sentence was written.

Applying the scalar result to each component $f_1,\dots,f_m$ separately does produce points
$\xi_1,\dots,\xi_m$ with
\[f(x+h)-f(x) = \begin{pmatrix}\partial_hf_1(\xi_1) \\ \vdots \\ \partial_hf_m(\xi_m)\end{pmatrix},\]
but the $\xi_i$ are \emph{different points} in general, and no single $\xi$ need work for all
components at once. The standard witness is the \emph{circle}
$\gamma(t) := (\cos t, \sin t)$ on $[0,2\pi]$: here $\gamma(2\pi)-\gamma(0) = 0$, while
$\gamma'(t) = (-\sin t,\cos t)$ has $|\gamma'(t)| = 1$ and so never vanishes. There is no such
$\xi$ at all. Geometrically this is entirely reasonable: a walker who returns to the starting
point must have had zero \emph{average} velocity, but need never have stood still. In one
dimension \qt{returning} forces a turning point; in two it does not.

What survives for vector-valued $f$ is the \newterm{mean value inequality} \germanfor{Mittelwertsatz-Ungleichung}, which replaces exact equality by a sharp upper bound controlling the displacement in terms of the maximum derivative along the line segment.
\end{importantremark}

\begin{theorem}[Mean value inequality]
\label{thm:mean_value_inequality}
Let $U \subseteq \mathbb{R}^n$ be open, $x \in U$, and $h \in \mathbb{R}^n$ such that the line segment $[x, x+h] := \{x + th \mid t \in [0,1]\}$ lies entirely in $U$. If $F : U \to \mathbb{R}^m$ is differentiable at every point of $[x, x+h]$, then
\[
|F(x+h) - F(x)| \leq \left( \sup_{t \in [0,1]} \|DF_{x+th}\|_{\mathrm{op}} \right) |h|,
\]
where $\|DF_z\|_{\mathrm{op}} := \sup_{|v|\leq 1} |DF_z(v)|$ is the operator norm of the linear map $DF_z$.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
Set $y := F(x+h) - F(x) \in \mathbb{R}^m$. If $y = 0$, the inequality holds trivially. Assuming $y \neq 0$, let $u := \frac{y}{|y|}$, so that $|u| = 1$ and $\langle u, y \rangle = |y|$.

Define the auxiliary scalar function $\varphi : [0,1] \to \mathbb{R}$ by
\[
\varphi(t) := \langle u, F(x+th) \rangle.
\]
By the chain rule along a path (\cref{sec:chain_rule_along_path}), $\varphi$ is continuous on $[0,1]$ and differentiable on $(0,1)$ with derivative $\varphi'(t) = \langle u, DF_{x+th}(h) \rangle$. Applying the scalar Mean Value Theorem (\cref{thm:mean_value_theorem}) to $\varphi$ on $[0,1]$, there exists some $t_0 \in (0,1)$ such that $\varphi(1) - \varphi(0) = \varphi'(t_0)$, which expands to
\[
|F(x+h) - F(x)| = \langle u, F(x+h) - F(x) \rangle = \langle u, DF_{x+t_0 h}(h) \rangle.
\]
Applying the Cauchy--Schwarz inequality and the definition of the operator norm, we obtain
\[
|F(x+h) - F(x)| \leq |u| \, |DF_{x+t_0 h}(h)| \leq 1 \cdot \|DF_{x+t_0 h}\|_{\mathrm{op}} \, |h| \leq \left( \sup_{t \in [0,1]} \|DF_{x+th}\|_{\mathrm{op}} \right) |h|,
\]
\end{proof}

\begin{remark}[Global Lipschitz bound on convex domains]
\label{rem:mvi_lipschitz_bound}
As Diego Torres Tejeda emphasizes in his notes, \Cref{thm:mean_value_inequality} is the principal multivariable tool for establishing Lipschitz continuity. If $U \subseteq \mathbb{R}^n$ is \emph{convex} and $F : U \to \mathbb{R}^m$ is differentiable with $\|DF_z\|_{\mathrm{op}} \leq M$ for all $z \in U$, then for any pair $x, y \in U$, the line segment $[x, y]$ lies in $U$, so
\[
|F(y) - F(x)| \leq M |y - x|.
\]
Thus $F$ is $M$-Lipschitz continuous on $U$.
\end{remark}

\begin{corollary}[Every $C^1$ function is locally Lipschitz]
\label{cor:c1_locally_lipschitz}
Let $U \subseteq \mathbb{R}^n$ be open, not necessarily convex, and let
$F \in C^1(U,\mathbb{R}^m)$. Then every point of $U$ has a ball around it on which $F$ is
Lipschitz, with a constant that may depend on the ball.
\end{corollary}

\begin{proof}
Given $z \in U$, choose $r > 0$ with $\overline{B_r(z)} \subseteq U$. This closed ball is closed
and bounded, hence compact (\cref{thm:heine_borel}), and $x \mapsto \|\Jac F(x)\|_2$ is
continuous because $F$ is $C^1$ and the Hilbert--Schmidt norm is a polynomial in the entries. So
it attains a finite maximum $M$ there (\cref{lem:sequential_compactness_extreme_values}). By
\cref{lem:hilbert_schmidt_bounds_lengths} that bounds the operator norm too,
$\|DF_x\|_{\mathrm{op}} \leq \|\Jac F(x)\|_2 \leq M$ on $\overline{B_r(z)}$. The open ball
$B_r(z)$ is convex, so \cref{rem:mvi_lipschitz_bound} applies on it with that $M$.
\end{proof}

\begin{importantremark}[Local, and not more than local]
\label{rem:locally_lipschitz_not_global}
The word \emph{locally} is not decoration. On $U = \mathbb{R}$ the function $F(x) := x^2$ is
$C^1$, hence Lipschitz on every bounded interval, and Lipschitz on no unbounded one: the constant
$M = \max|F'|$ produced by the proof grows with the ball and has no finite supremum. What
\cref{rem:mvi_lipschitz_bound} needs, and what fails here, is a bound on the derivative that is
uniform over the whole domain.

Locally Lipschitz is nevertheless the right hypothesis surprisingly often, because it is what
Picard--Lindelöf asks of the right-hand side of an ODE
(\cref{thm:picard_lindeloef_local}), and \cref{ex:c1_implies_locally_lipschitz} is this corollary
again, proved from scratch in the form that theorem wants.
\end{importantremark}

\begin{theorem}[Vanishing differential on a connected set]
\label{thm:zero_differential_constant}
Let $U \subseteq \mathbb{R}^n$ be open and \emph{connected}, and let $F : U \to \mathbb{R}^m$ be
differentiable with $DF_x = 0$ for every $x \in U$. Then $F$ is constant.

Without connectedness the conclusion weakens to: $F$ is constant on each connected component of
$U$.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
Assume $U \neq \emptyset$, fix $x_0 \in U$ and put
\[G := \{x \in U : F(x) = F(x_0)\}.\]
$G$ is non-empty, since $x_0 \in G$, and closed in $U$, being the preimage of the closed set
$\{F(x_0)\}$ under the continuous map $F$ (\cref{prop:differentiable_implies_continuous}).

$G$ is also \emph{open}. Given $x \in G$, choose $r > 0$ with $B_r(x) \subseteq U$. A ball is
convex, so \cref{rem:mvi_lipschitz_bound} applies on it with $M = 0$, giving
$|F(y) - F(x)| \leq 0$, that is $F(y) = F(x) = F(x_0)$, for every $y \in B_r(x)$. Hence
$B_r(x) \subseteq G$.

So $G$ is a non-empty subset of $U$ that is both open and closed in $U$. Since $U$ is connected,
$G = U$ (\cref{def:connectedness}), which is the claim. If $U$ is not connected, run the argument
inside a single component, which is itself open and connected.
\end{proof}

\begin{remark}[Why connectedness cannot be dropped]
\label{rem:zero_differential_needs_connected}
On $U := (0,1) \cup (2,3) \subseteq \mathbb{R}$ the function that is $0$ on the first interval and
$1$ on the second is differentiable with vanishing derivative everywhere, and is not constant.
Nothing in the local argument can see this: the derivative is a purely local object, and
\qt{constant} is not. Connectedness is precisely the hypothesis that lets a local statement be
propagated to a global one, which is why the proof above is a connectedness argument
(\qt{non-empty, open and closed, hence everything}) with a one-line differential calculus input
rather than the other way round.
\end{remark}

\begin{example}[Computing a Lipschitz constant via the Mean Value Inequality]
\label{ex:mvi_lipschitz_example}
Consider the vector field $F : \mathbb{R}^2 \to \mathbb{R}^2$ defined by $F(x,y) := (\sin(x+y), \cos(x-y))\transp$. We use the Mean Value Inequality to find an explicit Lipschitz constant for $F$ on $\mathbb{R}^2$.

The Jacobi matrix of $F$ at $(x,y)$ is
\[
\Jac F(x,y) = \begin{pmatrix} \cos(x+y) & \cos(x+y) \\ -\sin(x-y) & \sin(x-y) \end{pmatrix}.
\]
For any vector $v = (v_1, v_2)\transp \in \mathbb{R}^2$, the squared norm of $\Jac F(x,y) v$ is
\[
|\Jac F(x,y) v|^2 = \cos^2(x+y)(v_1+v_2)^2 + \sin^2(x-y)(v_1-v_2)^2.
\]
Since $\cos^2(x+y) \leq 1$ and $\sin^2(x-y) \leq 1$, we estimate:
\[
|\Jac F(x,y) v|^2 \leq (v_1+v_2)^2 + (v_1-v_2)^2 = (v_1^2 + 2v_1v_2 + v_2^2) + (v_1^2 - 2v_1v_2 + v_2^2) = 2(v_1^2 + v_2^2) = 2|v|^2.
\]
Taking square roots gives $|\Jac F(x,y) v| \leq \sqrt{2}|v|$, so $\|DF_{(x,y)}\|_{\mathrm{op}} \leq \sqrt{2}$ for all $(x,y) \in \mathbb{R}^2$. Since $\mathbb{R}^2$ is convex, \Cref{rem:mvi_lipschitz_bound} implies that $F$ is $\sqrt{2}$-Lipschitz continuous on $\mathbb{R}^2$.
\end{example}

\begin{exercise}[Differential of $A \mapsto A\transp A$]
\label{ex:transpose_diff}
We identify the inner product space of real-valued matrices $\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}$ with inner
product
\[\langle A, B\rangle = \sum_{i,j=1}^{n} A_{ij}B_{ij} = \Tr(A\transp B)\]
for $A, B \in \mathbb{R}^{n\times n}$, with the euclidean space
$\mathbb{R}^{n^2}\cong\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}$, with the standard inner product. Then, consider
the function
\[F : \mathbb{R}^{n\times n}\to\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}, \qquad A \mapsto A\transp A.\]
Compute the differential at the identity matrix $\id \in \mathbb{R}^{n\times n}$ applied to
$X \in \mathbb{R}^{n\times n}$:
\[DF_{\id}(X) = \partial_X F(\id).\]
\end{exercise}

% Kept inline: solved directly in Corsin Nick/Class Notes/Week 4.pdf, p. 7.
\begin{exercisesolution}[\cref{ex:transpose_diff}]
We use $(*)$:
\[
\begin{aligned}
DF_{\id}(X) &= \left.\frac{d}{ds}\right|_{s=0} F(\id+sX) \\
&= \left.\frac{d}{ds}\,(\id+sX)\transp(\id+sX)\right|_{s=0} \\
&= \left.\frac{d}{ds}\left[\id + s(X\transp+X) + s^2X\transp X\right]\right|_{s=0} \\
&= X\transp + X
\end{aligned}
\]
\end{exercisesolution}

\begin{exercise}[4.5 --- Mean value for vector-valued functions \textnormal{(optional)}]
\label{ex:4.5}
Let $f \in C^1(\mathbb{R},\mathbb{R}^m)$ for $m > 1$. Is it true that there is $t \in [0,1]$ such
that
$$f(1) - f(0) = Df_t(1) = \begin{pmatrix} f_1'(t) \\ \vdots \\ f_m'(t) \end{pmatrix}?$$
Prove it or provide a counterexample.

\textit{Official hint:} try $f(t) = (\sin(2\pi t), \cos(2\pi t))$.
\end{exercise}
