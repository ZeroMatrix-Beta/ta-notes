\begin{exercisesolution}[Solution to \cref{ex:schwarz_application}]
\label{sol:schwarz_application}
% Relocated from 02-taylor-expansions.tex by Claude Opus 5 (Extra), 2026-08-13.
\begin{enumerate}[label=\textbf{(\alph*)}]
  \item Every term in $f$ is a composition of $C^2$ functions (polynomials, exponentials, sine),
    and the denominator $2+\sin(x)$ is bounded away from zero. Thus $f$ is $C^2$.
  \item Since $f \in C^2$, by Schwarz's theorem (\cref{thm:schwarz_mixed_partials}) we have
    $\partial_2 \partial_1 f = \partial_1 \partial_2 f$. Computing $\partial_1$ of the second
    term would be tedious. However, notice that the second term depends only on $x$, so its
    derivative with respect to $y$ is $0$. Thus, we first compute $\partial_2 f$:
    \[\partial_2 f(x,y) = x^2 (2y e^{y^2}) + 0 = 2x^2 y e^{y^2}.\]
    Then we compute $\partial_1$ of this result:
    \[\partial_1 \partial_2 f(x,y) = \partial_1 (2x^2 y e^{y^2}) = 4xy e^{y^2}.\]
    By Schwarz's theorem, $\partial_2 \partial_1 f(x,y) = 4xy e^{y^2}$.
\end{enumerate}
\end{exercisesolution}

\begin{remark}[The monstrous term is the exercise]
The second term of $f$ was not chosen to be differentiated. It was chosen so that differentiating
it is out of the question, which leaves the reader with only two options: give up, or notice that
it does not depend on $y$ and let \cref{thm:schwarz_mixed_partials} take the derivatives in the
other order. An exercise of this shape has a single decision in it, and taking the decision is the
entire content.
\end{remark}

\begin{exercisesolution}[Solution to \cref{ex:reading_off_expansions}]
\label{sol:reading_off_expansions}
% Generator: Claude Opus 5 (High)
Throughout, the tool is \cref{cor:taylor_expansion_unique}: an $o(\lvert h\rvert^{k})$ fit by a
polynomial of degree $\leq k$ pins down every derivative of order $\leq k$, and nothing beyond.
\begin{enumerate}[label=\textbf{(\alph*)}]
  \item \textbf{Only $f(0)=0$.} The hypothesis is an $O$, not an $o$, so the corollary does not
    apply at any order, and that is the entire point of the item. Setting $x=0$ in
    $\lvert f(x)-x_1x_2\rvert \leq C\lvert x\rvert$ gives $\lvert f(0)\rvert \leq 0$, so
    $f(0)=0$. Nothing else is determined: both
    \[f(x) := x_1x_2 \qquad\text{and}\qquad g(x) := x_1x_2 + 5x_1\]
    satisfy the hypothesis, yet $\nabla f(0) = (0,0)$ while $\nabla g(0) = (5,0)$. An
    $O(\lvert x\rvert)$ remainder is free to hide a whole linear term, so not even the first
    derivatives survive.

    Note also that the visible $x_1x_2$ is a red herring: it is $O(\lvert x\rvert^2)$, hence
    already absorbed by the stated error, and contributes nothing.
  \item \textbf{Value and first derivatives; nothing further.} Put $x_0 := (1,-1)$ and
    $h := (x_1,x_2)$. The hypothesis reads $f(x_0+h) = 2 + h_1 + o(\lvert h\rvert)$, and
    $P(h) := 2 + h_1$ has degree $1$, so by \cref{cor:taylor_expansion_unique} with $k=1$ it
    \emph{is} the first-order Taylor polynomial:
    \[f(1,-1) = 2, \qquad \partial_1f(1,-1) = 1, \qquad \partial_2f(1,-1) = 0.\]
    Equivalently $\nabla f(1,-1) = (1,0)\transp$. Second derivatives are not determined: adding
    any quadratic to $f$ preserves the hypothesis.
  \item \textbf{Everything up to third order.} Put $x_0 := (-1,0)$. Rearranging,
    \[f(x_0+h) = \underbrace{-2 + 2h_1h_2 - 2h_2^2 + 3h_2^3}_{=:\,P(h)} + o\big(\lvert h\rvert^3\big),\]
    with $\deg P = 3$, so $P$ is the third-order Taylor polynomial and every derivative of order
    $\leq 3$ can be read off.

    \textbf{Order $0$ and $1$.} $f(-1,0) = -2$, and $P$ has no linear part, so
    $\nabla f(-1,0) = (0,0)\transp$, so the point is critical.

    \textbf{Order $2$.} The quadratic part of a Taylor expansion is
    $\tfrac12 h\transp\Hess f(x_0)h$, so
    $\tfrac12h\transp \Hess f(-1,0)\,h = 2h_1h_2 - 2h_2^2$. Comparing coefficients in
    $h\transp Hh = H_{11}h_1^2 + 2H_{12}h_1h_2 + H_{22}h_2^2 = 4h_1h_2 - 4h_2^2$ gives
    \[\Hess f(-1,0) = \begin{pmatrix} 0 & 2 \\ 2 & -4\end{pmatrix}.\]
    Its determinant is $-4 < 0$, so the Hessian is indefinite and, by \cref{thm:hessian_test},
    the critical point $(-1,0)$ is a \textbf{saddle}. Note that this was settled without ever
    differentiating $f$.

    \textbf{Order $3$.} A third-order term is $\partial^\alpha f(x_0)\,h^\alpha/\alpha!$. The only
    cubic monomial present is $h_2^3$, with $\alpha = (0,3)$ and $\alpha! = 6$, so
    $\partial_2^3f(-1,0)/6 = 3$, giving $\partial_2^3f(-1,0) = 18$; every other third derivative
    vanishes. Fourth-order derivatives are not determined.
\end{enumerate}
\end{exercisesolution}

\begin{exercisesolution}[Solution to \cref{ex:mean_value_vector_valued}]
\label{sol:mean_value_vector_valued}
% Generator: Claude Opus 5 (High)
\textbf{No}, and the official hint's function is the cleanest witness. Take
\[f(t) := \big(\sin(2\pi t),\ \cos(2\pi t)\big), \qquad f \in C^1(\mathbb{R},\mathbb{R}^2).\]
Then $f(1) = (0,1) = f(0)$, so the left-hand side is
\[f(1) - f(0) = (0,0).\]
On the other hand $f'(t) = 2\pi\big(\cos(2\pi t),\ -\sin(2\pi t)\big)$, whose norm is
\[|f'(t)| = 2\pi\sqrt{\cos^2(2\pi t) + \sin^2(2\pi t)} = 2\pi \neq 0
  \qquad \text{for every } t.\]
So $f'(t)$ never equals $(0,0)$, and no $t \in [0,1]$ can satisfy the claimed identity.

The reason is the one given in \cref{rem:mvt_fails_vector_valued}: applying the scalar mean value
theorem componentwise produces one intermediate point \emph{per component},
\[f_1(1)-f_1(0) = f_1'(\xi_1), \qquad f_2(1)-f_2(0) = f_2'(\xi_2),\]
and nothing forces $\xi_1 = \xi_2$. Here $\xi_1 = \tfrac14$ and $\xi_2 = \tfrac12$ work
componentwise. Both components do vanish, just not at the same time.

\begin{ainote}
Note what does \emph{not} fail. The \emph{mean value inequality}
$|f(1)-f(0)| \leq \sup_{t \in [0,1]}|f'(t)|$ holds here as
$0 \leq 2\pi$, comfortably. That inequality is what survives into $\mathbb{R}^m$ and what every
later estimate in this document actually uses; the \emph{equality} is a one-dimensional accident.
Geometrically the counterexample is a walker going once round a circle: they return to the start,
so the total displacement is zero, but they never once stand still.
\end{ainote}
\end{exercisesolution}

\begin{exercisesolution}[Solution to \cref{ex:landau_notation_multiple_choice}]
\label{sol:landau_notation_multiple_choice}
% Generator: Claude Sonnet 5 (Medium)
Throughout, recall the hint: \textit{as $x \to 0$} and \textit{as $|x| \to 0$} describe the same
limit, so both phrasings are treated identically below. The recurring subtlety is instead between
a statement holding \textbf{at} the single point $x=0$ (which a remainder estimate as $x \to 0$
can establish) versus \textbf{around}/in a whole neighbourhood of $x=0$ (which it cannot).
\begin{enumerate}[label=\textbf{(\alph*)}]
  \item \textbf{True.} Since $|x_2x_1^2| \leq |x|\cdot|x_1|^2 \leq |x|^3 = O(|x|^2)$ (as
    $|x|^3/|x|^2 = |x| \to 0$) and $O(x_1^2) = O(|x|^2)$ (since $x_1^2 \leq |x|^2$), the sum
    $f(x) = x_2x_1^2 + O(x_1^2)$ is a sum of two $O(|x|^2)$ terms, hence $f(x) = O(|x|^2)$.
  \item \textbf{False.} It suffices to show $x_2x_1^2$ itself is not $O(x_1^3)$. Along the path
    $x_1 = t^2$, $x_2 = t$ (so $x \to 0$ as $t \to 0$), $\dfrac{|x_2x_1^2|}{|x_1|^3} =
    \dfrac{t\cdot t^4}{t^6} = \dfrac{1}{t} \to \infty$, so no bound $|x_2x_1^2| \leq C|x_1|^3$ can
    hold near $0$. Adding an $O(x_1^3)$ term cannot repair this, so $f(x) \neq O(x_1^3)$ in
    general.
  \item \textbf{True.} Since $x_2x_1 = O(|x|^2)$ and the hypothesis adds a further $O(|x|^2)$
    term, $f(x) = O(|x|^2) = o(|x|)$ as $|x| \to 0$. Hence $f(x) = 0\cdot x + o(|x|)$, i.e.\ $f$ is
    differentiable \textbf{at} $x=0$ with $Df(0) = 0$.
  \item \textbf{False.} The hypothesis only constrains the behaviour of $f$ as $x \to 0$, i.e.\ at
    the single point $0$; it says nothing about $f$ at nearby points $x_0 \neq 0$, so twice
    differentiability \textbf{around} (in a neighbourhood of) $x=0$ does not follow. Concretely,
    take $f(x) := \max\{0,x_1\}^2$: this satisfies $f(x) = x_2x_1 + O(|x|^2)$ as $x \to 0$ (indeed
    even $f(x) = O(|x|^2)$, since $x_2x_1$ itself is $O(|x|^2)$ and so contributes nothing extra
    to the hypothesis), yet along $x_1$ alone, $t \mapsto \max\{0,t\}^2$ has derivative $2\max\{0,t\}$,
    which is not differentiable at $t=0$ (its one-sided difference quotients at $0$ are $2$ from
    the right and $0$ from the left). So $f$ is not twice differentiable at $x=0$, let alone
    around it.
  \item \textbf{False}, for the same reason as \textbf{(d)}: even the sharper estimate
    $f(x) = x_2x_1 + O(|x|^3)$ only pins down the second-order Taylor data of $f$ \textbf{at} the
    % Correction: Claude Opus 5 (Ultracode) --- read "by Corollary 10.45", unitalicised, which reads as
    % an internal reference to a number this document does not have: the corollary meant is the
    % script's (printed p. 56, "Polynomial expansions determine higher derivatives"), and our own
    % form of it is cor:taylor_expansion_unique, printed 11.b.20. Elsewhere the file cites the
    % script in italics for exactly this reason. Pointed at our statement instead, which is what
    % the top of sol:reading_off_expansions already does.
    point $x=0$ (by \cref{cor:taylor_expansion_unique}, this does give $f$ twice differentiable
    \emph{at} $0$ with $\partial_{11}f(0) = 0$), but it does not force twice differentiability in a whole
    neighbourhood \textbf{around} $x=0$. Without an a priori assumption such as $f \in C^2$ near
    $0$, one can cook up genuinely pathological examples, e.g.\
    $f(x) := x_1x_2 + |x|^4\sin(|x|^{-100})$, for which $\partial_1 f(x) \to \infty$ as
    $x \to 0$, so $\partial_1 f$ is not even continuous at $0$.
\end{enumerate}
\end{exercisesolution}

\begin{exercisesolution}[Solution to \cref{ex:taylor_expansion_cost_multiple_choice}]
\label{sol:taylor_expansion_cost_multiple_choice}
% Generator: Claude Opus 5 (High)
\textbf{The answer is (a): degree 3, at a cost of 20 CHF.}

The hypothesis $f(x) = x_1 + x_2x_1 + O(|x|^4)$ says two things at once: $f(0) = 0$, and $f$ is
pinned down exactly up to order $3$ (its degree-$3$ part happens to be zero). Expanding $g$ about
$f(0) = 0$,
\[(g\circ f)(x) = \sum_{k \geq 0} \frac{g^{(k)}(0)}{k!}\,f(x)^k.\]
Since $f = O(|x|)$, we have $f^k = O(|x|^k)$, so every term with $k \geq 4$ is already
$O(|x|^4)$ and invisible at order $3$. The unknown $O(|x|^4)$ inside $f$ likewise only perturbs
the answer at order $4$. So the degree-$3$ Taylor polynomial of $g \circ f$ is determined, and no
more than that is: nothing in the data constrains the degree-$4$ coefficients of $f$.

Concretely, truncating $f = x_1 + x_1x_2$ at order $3$ gives $f^2 = x_1^2 + 2x_1^2x_2 + O(|x|^4)$
and $f^3 = x_1^3 + O(|x|^4)$, so
\[(g\circ f)(x) = g(0) + g'(0)\big(x_1 + x_1x_2\big)
  + \frac{g''(0)}{2}\big(x_1^2 + 2x_1^2x_2\big) + \frac{g'''(0)}{6}\,x_1^3 + O(|x|^4).\]
That uses the four values $g(0), g'(0), g''(0), g'''(0)$, so four queries at 5 CHF each, giving
\textbf{20 CHF}. Options (b) and (c) under-pay and cannot reach degree 3; (d) claims degree 3 for
three queries, which is one derivative short.
\end{exercisesolution}

\begin{exercisesolution}[Solution to \cref{ex:polynomials_multivariate_expansion}]
\label{sol:polynomials_multivariate_expansion}
% Generator: Claude Opus 5 (High)
Put $R := P - Q = \sum_\alpha c_\alpha X^\alpha$ with $c_\alpha := a_\alpha - b_\alpha$, so the
hypothesis reads $|R(x)| \leq M|x|^\sigma$ for $|x| \leq 1$ and the claim is that
$c_\alpha = 0$ whenever $|\alpha| < \sigma$.

Suppose not, and let
\[d := \min\{\,|\alpha| \ :\ c_\alpha \neq 0\,\} < \sigma.\]
Split $R = R_d + \widetilde R$, where $R_d := \sum_{|\alpha| = d}c_\alpha X^\alpha$ is the
homogeneous part of degree $d$, non-zero by the choice of $d$, and $\widetilde R$ collects
the terms of degree $> d$, so that $\widetilde R(tu) = O(t^{d+1})$ uniformly over unit vectors
$u$.

Fix a unit vector $u$ and evaluate at $x := tu$ for $t \in (0,1]$. Homogeneity gives
$R_d(tu) = t^dR_d(u)$, so
\[\big|t^dR_d(u) + O(t^{d+1})\big| \ =\ |R(tu)| \ \leq\ M\,t^{\sigma},\]
and dividing by $t^d > 0$,
\[\big|R_d(u) + O(t)\big| \ \leq\ M\,t^{\sigma - d}.\]
Here is where the hypothesis $d < \sigma$ is spent: the exponent $\sigma - d$ is \emph{strictly
positive}, so the right-hand side tends to $0$ as $t \to 0^+$, and the left-hand side tends to
$|R_d(u)|$. Hence $R_d(u) = 0$ for every unit vector $u$, and by homogeneity $R_d \equiv 0$ on
all of $\mathbb{R}^n$.

A polynomial vanishing identically on $\mathbb{R}^n$ has all coefficients zero, so $c_\alpha = 0$
for every $|\alpha| = d$ --- contradicting the choice of $d$. Therefore no such $d$ exists, i.e.\
$a_\alpha = b_\alpha$ for all $|\alpha| < \sigma$.

\begin{ainote}
The statement is sharp in the obvious way: coefficients of order exactly $\sigma$ need not agree.
With $n=1$, $\sigma = 2$, $P(x) := x^2$ and $Q := 0$ we get $|P-Q| = |x|^2 \leq 1\cdot|x|^2$, and
the order-$2$ coefficients differ. The argument above breaks at precisely the right place: for
$d = \sigma$ the bound becomes $|R_d(u) + O(t)| \leq M$, which says nothing.
\end{ainote}
\end{exercisesolution}

\begin{exercisesolution}[Solution to \cref{ex:taylor_expansion_sqrt_1_plus_x_plus_y2}]
\label{sol:taylor_expansion_sqrt_1_plus_x_plus_y2}
% Generator: Gemini 3.6 Flash (High)
Using the 1D expansion $\sqrt{1+t} = 1 + \frac{1}{2}t - \frac{1}{8}t^2 + O(t^3)$ with $t = x+y^2$, we obtain:
\begin{align*}
\sqrt{1+x+y^2} &= 1 + \frac{1}{2}(x+y^2) - \frac{1}{8}(x+y^2)^2 + O(|(x,y)|^3) \\
&= 1 + \frac{1}{2}x + \frac{1}{2}y^2 - \frac{1}{8}(x^2+2xy^2+y^4) + o(|(x,y)|^2) \\
&= 1 + \frac{1}{2}x - \frac{1}{8}x^2 + \frac{1}{2}y^2 + o(|(x,y)|^2).
\end{align*}
Note that $t = x+y^2$ is of order $|h|$ and not of order $|h|^2$, because of its $x$. That is why
the $t^2$ term has to be carried and expanded, and why $2xy^2$ and $y^4$ are then dropped from it.
\end{exercisesolution}

\begin{exercisesolution}[Solution to \cref{ex:taylor_expansion_exp_arctan}]
\label{sol:taylor_expansion_exp_arctan}
% Generator: Claude Sonnet 5 (High)
Write $s := x-y$, itself of order $|h|$. Since $\arctan$ is odd, $\arctan(t) = t + O(t^3)$, so
\[\arctan(s) = s + O(|h|^3) = s + o(|h|^2).\]
Since $\exp$ is smooth and bounded near $0$, an $o(|h|^2)$ correction inside $\exp$ contributes
only an $o(|h|^2)$ correction to the output. Consequently, to order $2$, $\exp(\arctan(s))$ and
$\exp(s)$ agree:
\[\exp(\arctan(x-y)) = \exp(x-y) + o(|(x,y)|^2).\]
Now $\exp(s) = 1+s+\tfrac12s^2+O(s^3)$ with $s^2 = x^2-2xy+y^2$, so
\[\exp(\arctan(x-y)) = 1 + x - y + \tfrac12x^2 - xy + \tfrac12y^2 + o(|(x,y)|^2).\]
\end{exercisesolution}

\begin{exercisesolution}[Solution to \cref{ex:taylor_expansion_one_over_1_minus_x2_y2}]
\label{sol:taylor_expansion_one_over_1_minus_x2_y2}
% Generator: Claude Sonnet 5 (High)
Using $\tfrac{1}{1-t} = 1+t+O(t^2)$ with $t := x^2+y^2$, itself already $O(|h|^2)$, so
$t^2 = O(|h|^4) = o(|h|^2)$:
\[\frac{1}{1-x^2-y^2} = 1 + (x^2+y^2) + o(|(x,y)|^2).\]
\end{exercisesolution}

\begin{remark}[The substitution's own order decides how far to expand]
Read the three together and one number governs all of them, namely the order of the substituted
quantity $t$ itself. For $1/(1-x^2-y^2)$ we have $t = x^2+y^2 = O(|h|^2)$, so a single term of the
geometric series already exhausts order $2$ and $t^2$ is invisible. For $\sqrt{1+x+y^2}$ the same
$t$ carries an $x$, drops to order $|h|$, and consequently the quadratic term of the series has to
be expanded in full. The habit worth taking away is to ask what order $t$ is \emph{before}
deciding how many terms of the one-variable series to write down.
\end{remark}

\begin{exercisesolution}[Proof of \cref{ex:taylor_parametric_integral}]
\label{sol:taylor_parametric_integral}
% Generator: Gemini 3.6 Flash (High)
% Correction: Claude Opus 5 (High) --- parts (c) and (d) written; Flash's (c) only observed that
% x = 0 is critical, which answers neither the source's question nor its own weakened one.
\begin{enumerate}[label=\textbf{(\alph*)}]
  \item The integrand is continuous and both limits are differentiable in $x$, so by the Leibniz
    rule together with the fundamental theorem of calculus,
\[f'(x) = \frac{d}{dx} \int_{x-1}^{x+1} \cos(\pi t^2) \, dt = \cos\big(\pi (x+1)^2\big) - \cos\big(\pi (x-1)^2\big).\]
Since $(x\pm1)^2 = x^2 \pm 2x + 1$ and $\cos(\theta+\pi) = -\cos\theta$, each term simplifies:
\[\cos\big(\pi(x^2+1) \pm 2\pi x\big) = -\cos(\pi x^2 \pm 2\pi x).\]
Therefore $f'(x) = \cos(\pi x^2 - 2\pi x) - \cos(\pi x^2 + 2\pi x)$, and the identity
$\cos A - \cos B = -2\sin\frac{A+B}{2}\sin\frac{A-B}{2}$ collapses this to
\[f'(x) = 2 \sin(\pi x^2) \sin(2\pi x).\]

  \item Expand each factor as $x \to 0$: $\sin(2\pi x) = 2\pi x - \frac{4\pi^3}{3} x^3 + O(x^5)$ and
    $\sin(\pi x^2) = \pi x^2 + O(x^6)$. Multiplying,
\[f'(x) = 2 \big(\pi x^2 + O(x^6)\big)\left(2\pi x - \frac{4\pi^3}{3}x^3 + O(x^5)\right) = 4\pi^2 x^3 + O(x^5).\]
Integrating from $0$,
\[f(x) = f(0) + \int_0^x \big(4\pi^2 t^3 + O(t^5)\big)\,dt = f(0) + \pi^2 x^4 + O(x^6),\]
where $f(0) = \int_{-1}^1 \cos(\pi t^2)\,dt = 2\int_0^1 \cos(\pi t^2)\,dt$. Hence
$a_1 = a_2 = a_3 = 0$ and $a_4 = \pi^2$.

  \item By part \textbf{(a)}, $f'(x) = 0$ if and only if one of the two sine factors vanishes.
    Now $\sin(\pi x^2) = 0$ exactly when $x^2$ is a non-negative integer, and $\sin(2\pi x) = 0$
    exactly when $2x$ is an integer. The set of critical points is therefore
\[\left\{\pm\sqrt{n} \;\middle|\; n \in \mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}\right\} \cup \tfrac{1}{2}\mathbb{Z}.\]
    The two families overlap precisely at the integers whose square roots are integers, so the
    critical points of the form $x = \pm 1, \pm 2, \dots$ are \newterm{double} zeros of $f'$, and
    those are the ones where the classification changes character.

  \item \textbf{At $x = 0$:} by part \textbf{(b)}, $f(x) - f(0) = \pi^2 x^4 + O(x^6) > 0$ for all
    small $x \neq 0$. Equivalently, $f'(x) = 4\pi^2 x^3 + O(x^5)$ changes sign from negative to
    positive. Either way, $x = 0$ is a strict local \textbf{minimum} (and $f^{(4)}(0) = 4!\,a_4 =
    24\pi^2 > 0$).

    \textbf{At $x = 1$:} write $x = 1 + h$. Then $\sin(\pi x^2) = \sin\big(\pi + \pi(2h+h^2)\big) =
    -\sin\big(\pi(2h+h^2)\big) = -2\pi h + O(h^2)$ and $\sin(2\pi x) = \sin(2\pi h) = 2\pi h +
    O(h^3)$, so
\[f'(1+h) = 2(-2\pi h + O(h^2))(2\pi h + O(h^3)) = -8\pi^2 h^2 + O(h^3).\]
    This is strictly negative for all small $h \neq 0$, on \emph{both} sides. So $f$ is decreasing
    through $x = 1$ with a horizontal tangent: the point is \textbf{neither} a maximum nor a
    minimum. Reading the coefficients off the expansion gives $f''(1) = 0$ and
    $f'''(1) = -16\pi^2$, which is the value the official solution reports.
\end{enumerate}
\begin{remark}
The contrast between the two points is the whole content of part \textbf{(d)}. Both are critical,
and at both the second derivative vanishes, so the Hessian test says nothing at either. What decides
the question is the \emph{order} of the first non-vanishing derivative: odd order at $x=1$ (no
extremum), even order at $x=0$ (extremum, with the sign of the coefficient choosing which).
\end{remark}
\begin{remark}[A shortcut for the odd coefficients]
The vanishing of $a_1$ and $a_3$ needs no computation at all. Substituting $s = -t$ shows
$f(-x) = f(x)$, so $f$ is even, and the Taylor coefficients of an even function at $0$ vanish in
every odd degree. This is the route the official solution takes, and it is worth preferring: it
settles two of the four coefficients before a single derivative is computed.
\end{remark}
\begin{ainote}
Cross-checked against the official solution, which agrees on every value: $a_1 = a_2 = a_3 = 0$,
$a_4 = \pi^2$, $f^{(4)}(0) = 24\pi^2$, the two families $\pm\sqrt{n}$ and $\tfrac12\mathbb{Z}$ of
critical points, and $f'''(1) = -16\pi^2$. One divergence is presentational, and is flagged here so
that the two can be read side by side: the official solution displays the product formula as
$2\sin(\pi(x^2+1))\sin(2\pi x)$, which is $-f'(x)$ rather than $f'(x)$, since
$\sin(\pi(x^2+1)) = -\sin(\pi x^2)$. It comes from applying $2\sin a\sin b = \cos(a-b) - \cos(a+b)$
to $\cos(\pi(x-1)^2) - \cos(\pi(x+1)^2)$, which is $f'$ with its two terms interchanged. The sign
never surfaces in that solution's conclusions, because it uses the formula only to locate zeros and
then classifies $x=0$ and $x=1$ from $f^{(4)}(0)$ and $f'''(1)$ instead. It would matter to anyone
running a sign argument off the displayed identity, as the classification of $x=1$ above does.
\end{ainote}
