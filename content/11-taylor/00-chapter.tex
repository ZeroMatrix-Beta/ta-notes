\chapter{The Mean Value Theorem and Taylor Expansions}
\label{ch:taylor}

% Transition: Claude Opus 5 (High)
Having linearized a map once, we ask two further questions. How good is that linear approximation,
and how is it improved order by order? The answer is the several-variable analogue of the Taylor
series from \emph{Analysis I}, and it is what supplies every quantitative estimate in the
optimization theory of the next part.

The mean value theorem comes first, for two reasons. It is the instrument the error terms are
estimated with, and it is the first result in this course that survives the move to
$\mathbb{R}^m$ only as an \emph{inequality} rather than an equality once $m > 1$ (\cref{ex:mean_value_vector_valued}).
That degradation is not a defect of the proof. There is simply no single intermediate point at
which all $m$ components can be caught at once.
