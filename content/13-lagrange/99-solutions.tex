\begin{exercisesolution}[Proof of \cref{ex:antipodal_max_distance}]
% Generator: Claude Sonnet 5 (High)
Since $|x|=|y|=1$ on $S^{n-1}$,
\[f(x,y) := |x-y|^2 = |x|^2 - 2\langle x,y\rangle + |y|^2 = 2 - 2\langle x,y\rangle,\]
so maximising $|x-y|$ is the same as maximising $f$, i.e.\ minimising $\langle x,y\rangle$.
The domain $S^{n-1}\times S^{n-1}$ is compact (a product of compact sets), so $f$ attains a
maximum somewhere by the extreme value theorem; we locate it with Lagrange multipliers under the
two constraints $g_1(x,y) := |x|^2-1 = 0$ and $g_2(x,y) := |y|^2-1=0$.

Computing gradients in $\mathbb{R}^{2n} = \mathbb{R}^n_x \times \mathbb{R}^n_y$:
\[\nabla f = (-2y,\,-2x), \qquad \nabla g_1 = (2x,\,0), \qquad \nabla g_2 = (0,\,2y).\]
At a critical point, $\nabla g_1,\nabla g_2$ are linearly independent (they are non-zero and
supported on disjoint blocks of coordinates), so \cref{prop:constrained_optimization} gives
$\lambda_1,\lambda_2$ with $\nabla f = \lambda_1\nabla g_1 + \lambda_2\nabla g_2$, i.e.\
\[-2y = 2\lambda_1x, \qquad -2x = 2\lambda_2y \qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad
  y = -\lambda_1x, \quad x=-\lambda_2y.\]
So, $x$ and $y$ are parallel. Combined with $|x|=|y|=1$, this forces $y = x$ or $y=-x$.
These are the only two candidates, and $f(x,x) = 0$ while $f(x,-x) = |2x|^2 = 4$. The maximum
of $f$ over the compact domain is therefore $4$, attained exactly when $y=-x$ (i.e.\ exactly at
antipodal pairs, where $|x-y| = 2$).
\end{exercisesolution}

\begin{remark}[Squaring first is not a cosmetic step]
% Generator: Claude Opus 5 (Extra)
The problem is posed for $|x-y|$ and solved for $|x-y|^2$, and the swap is what makes the
multiplier rule applicable at all. The Euclidean norm fails to be differentiable exactly where its
argument vanishes, which here is the diagonal $x=y$, and that is one of the two candidate
configurations the argument has to compare. Squaring removes the singularity without moving the
maximiser, since $t \mapsto t^2$ is strictly increasing on $[0,\infty)$. The same manoeuvre is
worth reaching for whenever a distance is being optimised.
\end{remark}

\begin{exercisesolution}[Solution to \cref{ex:5.2}]
% Generator: Claude Sonnet 5 (Medium)
\begin{enumerate}[label=\textbf{(\alph*)}]
  \item $\overline{U}$ is closed and bounded in $\mathbb{R}^3$, hence compact by
    \Cref{thm:heine_borel}, and $f$ is a polynomial, hence continuous. By the extreme value
    theorem (\cref{item:extreme_value_theorem}), $f$ attains its absolute maximum and minimum on
    $\overline{U}$.
  \item $\nabla f(x,y,z) = (-2x+4,\ -2y,\ 0)$ vanishes only at $x=2$, $y=0$ (any $z$), and no such
    point lies in $U$ (since $x=2$ already violates $x^2+y^2+z^2<1$). So, $f$ has \textbf{no
    critical point in the interior of $U$}; consequently, by
    \Cref{prop:extremum_implies_critical}, $\sup_U f$ and $\inf_U f$ are \textbf{not attained} at
    any interior point.
  \item $\partial U$ is compact (a sphere) and $f$ is continuous, so $\max_{\partial U} f$ and
    $\min_{\partial U} f$ \textbf{both exist}, again by the extreme value theorem
    (\cref{item:extreme_value_theorem}).
  \item With $g(x,y,z) := x^2+y^2+z^2-1$, the Lagrange condition $\nabla f = \lambda \nabla g$
    reads $(-2x+4,-2y,0) = \lambda(2x,2y,2z)$. The last coordinate gives $\lambda z = 0$.
    \textbf{If $z \neq 0$:} then $\lambda = 0$, so $-2x+4=0 \Rightarrow x=2$, impossible on
    $\partial U$. So, $z = 0$. Then $x^2+y^2=1$, and the remaining equations are
    $4-2x = 2\lambda x$ and $-2y = 2\lambda y$, i.e.\ $-2y(1+\lambda) = 0$.
    \textbf{If $\lambda = -1$:} the first equation becomes $4-2x=-2x$, i.e.\ $4=0$, impossible.
    So $y = 0$, and $x^2=1$ gives $x = \pm 1$. The Lagrange candidates are
    $(1,0,0)$ (with $\lambda = 1$) and $(-1,0,0)$ (with $\lambda = -3$).
  \item Evaluating, $f(1,0,0) = 1-1+4 = 4$ and $f(-1,0,0) = 1-1-4 = -4$. Since there are no
    interior critical points, these are the global extrema on $\overline{U}$: the
    \textbf{maximum} $4$ is attained at $(1,0,0)$, and the \textbf{minimum} $-4$ at $(-1,0,0)$.
\end{enumerate}
\end{exercisesolution}

\begin{exercisesolution}[Proof of \cref{ex:ai_lagrange_circle}]
% Generator: Gemini 3.6 Flash (Medium)
The constraint set $S := \{(x,y) \in \mathbb{R}^2 \mid x^2 + y^2 = 5\}$ is compact and $\nabla g(x,y) = (2x, 2y)\transp \neq (0,0)\transp$ on $S$. By Lagrange Multipliers, candidates satisfy $\nabla f = \lambda \nabla g$, i.e.:
\[
1 = 2\lambda x \quad \text{and} \quad 2 = 2\lambda y \implies y = 2x.
\]
Substituting $y = 2x$ into $x^2 + y^2 = 5$ gives $5x^2 = 5 \implies x = \pm 1$. The candidate points are $(1,2)$ and $(-1,-2)$. Evaluating $f$:
\[
f(1,2) = 1 + 4 = 5 \quad (\text{maximum}), \qquad f(-1,-2) = -1 - 4 = -5 \quad (\text{minimum}).
\]
\end{exercisesolution}

\begin{exercisesolution}[Solution to \cref{ex:6.10}]
% Generator: Claude Sonnet 5 (Medium)
With $g_1(x,y,z) := x^2+y^2+z^2-1$ and $g_2(x,y,z) := x+y$, the Lagrangian
$L := f - \lambda_1 g_1 - \lambda_2 g_2$ gives the system
\[3 - 2\lambda_1 x - \lambda_2 = 0, \qquad -1-2\lambda_1 y - \lambda_2 = 0, \qquad 2 - 2\lambda_1 z = 0, \qquad x+y=0, \qquad x^2+y^2+z^2=1.\]
From $x+y=0$, $y=-x$. Subtracting the second equation from the first eliminates $\lambda_2$:
\[4 - 2\lambda_1 x - 2\lambda_1(-x) \cdot(-1)= 4-2\lambda_1x-2\lambda_1x=4-4\lambda_1 x = 0 \implies \lambda_1 x = 1.\]
From $2-2\lambda_1 z=0$, $\lambda_1 = 1/z$ (so $z \neq 0$), hence $x = z$ (from $\lambda_1 x = 1 =
\lambda_1 z$). With $y=-x=-z$, the constraint becomes $3x^2 = 1$, i.e.\ $x = \pm\tfrac{1}{\sqrt3}$,
giving the two points $\tfrac{1}{\sqrt3}(1,-1,1)$ and $-\tfrac{1}{\sqrt3}(1,-1,1)$. Evaluating,
\[f\Big(\tfrac{1}{\sqrt3}(1,-1,1)\Big) = \frac{3+1+2}{\sqrt3} = 2\sqrt3, \qquad f\Big({-\tfrac{1}{\sqrt3}}(1,-1,1)\Big) = -2\sqrt3.\]
Since $M$ is compact (closed and bounded in $\mathbb{R}^3$) and $f$ is continuous, these are the
only Lagrange candidates, so $f$ attains its \textbf{maximum} $2\sqrt3$ at $\tfrac{1}{\sqrt3}(1,-1,1)$
and its \textbf{minimum} $-2\sqrt3$ at $-\tfrac{1}{\sqrt3}(1,-1,1)$.
\end{exercisesolution}

\begin{exercisesolution}[Solution to \cref{ex:6.11}]
% Generator: Claude Sonnet 5 (Medium)
Minimizing the distance to $(0,0,1)$ is the same as minimizing
$f(x,y,z) := x^2+y^2+(z-1)^2$ subject to $g(x,y,z) := z-x^2+y^2 = 0$. Since $f$ is a distance
squared to a fixed point, its minimum over the (closed) constraint set is attained. Lagrange's
condition $\nabla f = \lambda \nabla g$ reads
\[2x = -2\lambda x, \qquad 2y = 2\lambda y, \qquad 2(z-1) = \lambda,\]
i.e.\ $x(1+\lambda) = 0$ and $y(1-\lambda) = 0$.

\textbf{Case $x=y=0$:} the constraint gives $z=0$, so $(0,0,0)$ with $d^2 = 1$.

\textbf{Case $x \neq 0$ (so $\lambda=-1$):} then $2(z-1)=-1 \implies z = \tfrac12$, and $y(1-\lambda)=2y=0
\implies y=0$. The constraint $z=x^2-y^2$ gives $x^2=\tfrac12$, i.e.\ $x=\pm\tfrac{1}{\sqrt2}$,
giving $\big(\pm\tfrac{1}{\sqrt2},0,\tfrac12\big)$ with $d^2 = \tfrac12+0+\tfrac14 = \tfrac34$.

\textbf{Case $y \neq 0$ (so $\lambda=1$):} then $2(z-1)=1 \implies z=\tfrac32$, and $x(1+\lambda)=2x=0
\implies x=0$; the constraint becomes $-y^2 = \tfrac32$, which has no real solution.

Comparing $d^2=1$ against $d^2=\tfrac34$, the \textbf{closest points} are
$\big(\pm\tfrac{1}{\sqrt2},0,\tfrac12\big)$, at minimal distance $\tfrac{\sqrt3}{2}$.
\end{exercisesolution}

\begin{exercisesolution}[Proof of \cref{ex:lagrange_ellipsoid_linear}]
% Generator: Gemini 3.6 Flash (High)
% Correction: Claude Opus 5 (High) --- ^T -> \transp; the \implies signs inside displays replaced by
% prose per the logical-arrows rule; and "g := ... - 1 = 0" split, since it was naming g and
% asserting a constraint in one breath.
Write $S := a_1^2 b_1^2 + a_2^2 b_2^2 + a_3^2 b_3^2$, and define the constraint function
\[g(x_1,x_2,x_3) := \frac{x_1^2}{a_1^2} + \frac{x_2^2}{a_2^2} + \frac{x_3^2}{a_3^2} - 1,\]
so that $E = g^{-1}(0)$. This set is closed and bounded, hence compact, and $f$ is continuous, so
$f$ does attain a maximum and a minimum on $E$; it remains to find them.

The gradients are $\nabla f(x) = (b_1, b_2, b_3)\transp$ and $\nabla g(x) = \left(\frac{2x_1}{a_1^2}, \frac{2x_2}{a_2^2}, \frac{2x_3}{a_3^2}\right)\transp$.
Since $0 \notin E$, we have $\nabla g \neq 0$ on $E$, so every extremum satisfies Lagrange's
condition $\nabla f = \lambda \nabla g$ for some $\lambda$. Componentwise this reads
$b_i = 2\lambda x_i / a_i^2$, and therefore
\[x_i = \frac{a_i^2 b_i}{2\lambda}, \qquad i = 1,2,3.\]
Here $\lambda \neq 0$, since $\lambda = 0$ would force $b = 0$. Substituting into the constraint,
\[\sum_{i=1}^3 \frac{1}{a_i^2} \left(\frac{a_i^2 b_i}{2\lambda}\right)^2 = \frac{S}{4\lambda^2} = 1,\]
which gives $2\lambda = \pm\sqrt{S}$. The two candidate points are therefore
\[x^{\pm} = \pm \frac{\big(a_1^2 b_1,\; a_2^2 b_2,\; a_3^2 b_3\big)\transp}{\sqrt{S}},\]
and evaluating $f$ at them,
\[f(x^{\pm}) = \pm \frac{1}{\sqrt{S}}\sum_{i=1}^3 a_i^2 b_i^2 = \pm \frac{S}{\sqrt{S}} = \pm\sqrt{S}.\]
Since the maximum and minimum both exist and must occur among these two points, the maximum value
is $\sqrt{S}$, attained at $x^{+}$, and the minimum value is $-\sqrt{S}$, attained at $x^{-}$.
\begin{remark}
Substituting $u_i := x_i/a_i$ turns $f$ into $\langle u, (a_ib_i)_i\rangle$ on the unit sphere, where
the answer is Cauchy--Schwarz: the extreme values of a linear form on the unit sphere are
$\pm$ the norm of its coefficient vector, attained at $\pm$ the unit vector in that direction. The
Lagrange computation above is that fact in disguise, with $\sqrt{S}$ the norm of $(a_ib_i)_i$.
\end{remark}
\end{exercisesolution}

\begin{exercisesolution}[Proof of \cref{ex:entropy_simplex_minimum}]
% Generator: Claude Opus 5 (High)
Throughout, write $g(x) := x_1 + \dots + x_n - 1$ for the constraint function, so that
$A = g^{-1}(0) \cap (0,1]^n$, and note $\nabla g \equiv (1,\dots,1)\transp \neq 0$, so the Lagrange
condition is available at every point.
\begin{enumerate}[label=\textbf{(\alph*)}]
  \item On the interior, where every $x_i > 0$, the function $t \mapsto t\log t$ is differentiable
    and $\nabla f(x) = \big(\log(x_i) + 1\big)_{i=1}^n$. The Lagrange condition
    $\nabla f(x) = \lambda \nabla g(x)$ reads
\[\log(x_i) + 1 = \lambda \qquad \text{for every } i,\]
    so all the $x_i$ share the common value $e^{\lambda - 1}$; call it $c$. The constraint
    $\sum_i x_i = 1$ becomes $nc = 1$, forcing $c = 1/n$. Hence there is exactly one critical point,
\[p = \left(\tfrac1n, \dots, \tfrac1n\right), \qquad
  f(p) = \sum_{i=1}^n \tfrac1n \log\tfrac1n = \log\tfrac1n = -\log n .\]

  \item Existence needs a compact set, and $A$ is not closed: points with some $x_i = 0$ are missing.
    So pass to the closed standard simplex
\[\Delta := \left\{x \in \mathbb{R}^n \;\middle|\; x_i \ge 0 \text{ for all } i, \ \textstyle\sum_i x_i = 1\right\},\]
    which is closed and bounded, hence compact, and extend $f$ to it by setting $0\log 0 := 0$. This
    extension is continuous, because $t \log t \to 0$ as $t \to 0^+$. Therefore $f$ attains a minimum
    somewhere on $\Delta$.

    Where? Suppose the minimum were attained at a point $x \in \Delta \setminus A$, so that exactly
    $k$ of the coordinates are positive, with $1 \le k \le n-1$. On that face, $f$ is the same
    expression in $k$ variables summing to $1$, and by part \textbf{(a)} applied with $k$ in place of
    $n$ its smallest value there is $-\log k$. Since $k < n$ gives $-\log k > -\log n = f(p)$, and
    $p \in \Delta$, such a point cannot be a minimiser. The minimum is therefore attained in the
    interior, where by \textbf{(a)} the only candidate is $p$. Hence $\min_A f = f(p) = -\log n$.
\end{enumerate}
\begin{remark}
The result is the discrete Gibbs inequality: among all probability vectors on $n$ outcomes, the
uniform one has the least $\sum x_i\log x_i$, that is, the \emph{greatest} entropy $-\sum x_i \log
x_i$, namely $\log n$. The mechanism is convexity: $\Hess f(x) = \diag(1/x_1,\dots,1/x_n)$ is
positive definite on the interior, so $f$ is strictly convex there, and a strictly convex function on
a convex set has at most one critical point --- which is why part \textbf{(a)} finds exactly one and
part \textbf{(b)} can then simply rule out the boundary.
\end{remark}
\end{exercisesolution}

\begin{exercisesolution}[Solution of \cref{ex:kobel_lagrange_single_choice}]
% Generator: Claude Opus 5 (High)
The correct answer is \textbf{(a)}, and one line of the Lagrange system settles it.

We have $\nabla g(x,y,z) = (0,\,2ay,\,2bz)\transp$, which vanishes only where $y = z = 0$; that
point is not on the constraint set, since $g(x,0,0) = -\pi \neq 0$. So the constraint
qualification holds everywhere on $g^{-1}\{0\}$ and \cref{prop:constrained_optimization} applies
without reservation: at any constrained extremum there is a $\lambda$ with
$\nabla f = \lambda\nabla g$, that is
\[e^x = \lambda \cdot 0, \qquad 2y = 2\lambda a y, \qquad 2z = 2\lambda b z.\]
The first equation reads $e^x = 0$, which has no real solution. The system is therefore
inconsistent, there is no critical point of the constrained problem for any $a$ and $b$, and by the
theorem there is no constrained extremum either.
\begin{remark}[Why the other three are wrong, and what they are testing]
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{(b)} fails on the geometry, and the phrase to distrust is \emph{independently of
    the values of $a$ and $b$}. The set $\{ay^2+bz^2 = \pi\}$ in $\mathbb{R}^3$ is never compact,
    whatever the signs: the variable $x$ is entirely unconstrained, so the set is a cylinder over a
    conic in the $(y,z)$-plane and is unbounded along the $x$-axis. When $a,b>0$ that conic is an
    ellipse and the set is an elliptic cylinder; when $a$ and $b$ have opposite signs it is a
    hyperbola and the set is unbounded in the $(y,z)$-directions as well.
  \item \textbf{(c)} fails for the reason \textbf{(a)} succeeds. Setting $a=b$ makes the cross
    section a circle and does nothing to the first Lagrange equation, which is where the
    obstruction lives.
  \item \textbf{(d)} is the answer of a reader who notices that $f$ itself is unknown. But
    \cref{prop:constrained_optimization} is a statement about $\nabla f$, and $\nabla f$ \emph{is}
    given. Knowing $f$ only up to an additive constant is exactly enough.
\end{itemize}
The transferable point is that the constrained problem can be settled without ever writing down
$f$, and that an inconsistent Lagrange system is a legitimate --- and here decisive --- outcome.
\end{remark}
\end{exercisesolution}

\begin{exercisesolution}[Solution of \cref{ex:kobel_sphere_meets_cylinder}]
% Generator: Claude Opus 5 (High)
The maximum is $\boxed{5/4}$.

Subtract the second constraint from the first: $z^2 = 1 - 1/2 = 1/2$ on the whole constraint set.
So $z$ is not a free variable at all, and the objective collapses to
\[f = x^2 + y + z^2 = x^2 + y + \tfrac12 .\]
The second constraint gives $x^2 = 1/2 - y^2$, leaving a function of the single variable $y$:
\[\varphi(y) := \left(\tfrac12 - y^2\right) + y + \tfrac12 = 1 + y - y^2,
  \qquad y \in \left[-\tfrac{1}{\sqrt2},\,\tfrac{1}{\sqrt2}\right],\]
the interval being forced by $x^2 = 1/2-y^2 \ge 0$. Now $\varphi'(y) = 1-2y$ vanishes at
$y = 1/2$, which lies in the interval since $1/2 < 1/\sqrt2$, and $\varphi'' = -2 < 0$, so this is
the maximum. Its value is $\varphi(1/2) = 1 + 1/2 - 1/4 = 5/4$, attained where $y = 1/2$,
$x^2 = 1/2-1/4 = 1/4$ and $z^2 = 1/2$, that is at the four points
$\left(\pm\tfrac12,\ \tfrac12,\ \pm\tfrac{1}{\sqrt2}\right)$.

The constraint set is compact --- it is the intersection of a sphere with a cylinder, two circles
of radius $1/\sqrt{2}$ at heights $z = \pm 1/\sqrt2$ --- so a maximum exists and the computation
above finds it.
\begin{remark}[Reach for Lagrange second]
\label{rem:constraints_before_multipliers}
Two constraints in three variables leave a one-dimensional set, and \cref{prop:constrained_optimization}
with two multipliers will find the answer. It is more work than the problem needs. The constraints
here are algebraically compatible in a way that eliminates two of the three variables outright, and
noticing that turns a $4 \times 4$ system into a one-variable parabola.

The habit worth forming: before setting up multipliers, ask whether the constraints can be
\emph{solved}. Multipliers exist for the case where they cannot --- where the constraint set has no
convenient parametrisation --- and applying them to a constraint set you could have parametrised is
a common way to lose marks to arithmetic.
\end{remark}
\end{exercisesolution}
