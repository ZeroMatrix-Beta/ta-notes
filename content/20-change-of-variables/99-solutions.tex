\begin{exercisesolution}[Solution to \cref{ex:change_of_variables_computations}]
\label{sol:change_of_variables_computations}
% Generator: Claude Opus 5 (High)
In each part the work is the same two steps: find $\lvert\det\Jac\rvert$, then translate every
constraint. As the exercise says, no integral is actually evaluated.
\begin{enumerate}[label=\textbf{(\alph*)}]
  \item \textbf{Polar coordinates.} Here $\Phi(r,\theta) := (r\cos\theta, r\sin\theta)$ has
    $\det\Jac\Phi = r > 0$ (\cref{ex:polar_spherical_coordinates}), so
    $dx_1dx_2 = r\,dr\,d\theta$. The three constraints translate one at a time:
    \begin{itemize}
      \item $1 < x_1^2+x_2^2 < 4$ becomes $1 < r < 2$;
      \item $x_1 > 0$ becomes $\cos\theta > 0$, i.e.\ $\theta \in (-\tfrac\pi2, \tfrac\pi2)$;
      \item $x_2 < x_1$ becomes $r\sin\theta < r\cos\theta$; since $r > 0$ and $\cos\theta > 0$
        this is $\tan\theta < 1$, i.e.\ $\theta < \tfrac\pi4$.
    \end{itemize}
    So the transformed domain is the rectangle
    $(r,\theta) \in (1,2)\times\big(-\tfrac\pi2,\tfrac\pi4\big)$ and
    \[\int_A x_1^2\sin(x_2)\,dx_1dx_2
      = \int_{1}^{2}\!\!\int_{-\pi/2}^{\pi/4} r^3\cos^2\theta\,\sin(r\sin\theta)\,d\theta\,dr,\]
    the extra power of $r$ coming from $x_1^2 = r^2\cos^2\theta$ times the Jacobian factor $r$.
  \item \textbf{$u := xy$, $v := x^2$.} Follow Sascha Brack's hint and fix the signs first. On $B$
    the constraint $x^2 < y$ forces $y > 0$, and then $xy > 1 > 0$ forces $x > 0$; so
    $u > 0$ and $v > 0$ throughout, and $x = \sqrt v$, $y = u/\sqrt v$.

    Differentiating in the convenient direction,
    \[\frac{\partial(u,v)}{\partial(x,y)}
      = \det\begin{pmatrix} y & x \\ 2x & 0\end{pmatrix} = -2x^2 = -2v,
      \qquad\text{so}\qquad dx\,dy = \frac{du\,dv}{2v}.\]
    Now the constraints. $1 < xy < 2$ becomes $1 < u < 2$ at once. The second is the interesting
    one: $x^2 < y < 2x^2$ reads $v < u/\sqrt v < 2v$, i.e.
    \[v^{3/2} < u < 2\,v^{3/2}, \qquad\text{equivalently}\qquad
      \Big(\frac{u}{2}\Big)^{2/3} < v < u^{2/3}.\]
    The integrand becomes $y^2e^{-xy} = (u^2/v)\,e^{-u}$, so
    \[\int_B y^2e^{-xy}\,dx\,dy
      = \int_{1}^{2}\!\!\int_{(u/2)^{2/3}}^{u^{2/3}} \frac{u^2e^{-u}}{2v^2}\,dv\,du.\]
    Note what did \emph{not} happen: straightening $1 < xy < 2$ into a pair of constant bounds
    did nothing for the other constraint, which stays curved. Straightening one constraint does
    not oblige the rest to cooperate.
  \item \textbf{A linear change of variables.} With $u := z$, $v := z-2y$, $w := 3x+y+z$,
    \[\frac{\partial(u,v,w)}{\partial(x,y,z)}
      = \det\begin{pmatrix} 0 & 0 & 1 \\ 0 & -2 & 1 \\ 3 & 1 & 1\end{pmatrix} = 6,
      \qquad\text{so}\qquad dx\,dy\,dz = \frac{du\,dv\,dw}{6},\]
    a constant, since the map is linear. Each constraint already \emph{is} one of the new
    coordinates, so the transformed domain is literally the box
    \[(u,v,w) \in (0,1)\times(1,2)\times(-2,0).\]
    Inverting the substitution gives $z = u$, $y = \tfrac{u-v}{2}$ and
    $x = \tfrac{1}{6}\big(2w - 3u + v\big)$, so $xyz = \tfrac{1}{12}\,u\,(u-v)\,(2w-3u+v)$ and
    \[\int_C xyz\,dx\,dy\,dz
      = \int_{0}^{1}\!\!\int_{1}^{2}\!\!\int_{-2}^{0}
        \frac{u\,(u-v)\,(2w-3u+v)}{72}\,dw\,dv\,du.\]
\end{enumerate}

\begin{ainote}
The three parts are graded deliberately, and comparing them is the lesson. In part \textbf{(c)}
the change of variables was \emph{chosen} to be the constraints, so the domain is a box and the
Jacobian a constant, and nothing is left to do. In part \textbf{(a)} the domain is a box in the
new coordinates but the Jacobian is not constant. In part \textbf{(b)} neither holds: the Jacobian
varies and one of the two constraints stays curved. Reading the substitution off the constraints,
as in part \textbf{(c)}, is the move
worth remembering, and it is available far more often than it looks.
\end{ainote}
\end{exercisesolution}

\begin{exercisesolution}[Solution to \cref{ex:ai_triangle_substitution}]
\label{sol:ai_triangle_substitution}
% Generator: Claude Opus 5 (High)
\begin{enumerate}[label=\textbf{(\alph*)}]
  \item \textbf{The new domain and the Jacobian factor.} The map $\Psi(x,y) := (y-x,\ y+x)$ is
    linear and invertible, with
    \[\Psi^{-1}(u,v) = (x,y) = \left(\frac{v-u}{2},\ \frac{u+v}{2}\right),
      \qquad
      \det\Jac\Psi^{-1} = \det\begin{pmatrix} -1/2 & 1/2 \\ 1/2 & 1/2\end{pmatrix} = -\frac12,\]
    so $dx\,dy = \tfrac12\,du\,dv$. The three sides of $T$ translate one at a time: $x \geq 0$
    reads $u \leq v$, the condition $y \geq 0$ reads $u \geq -v$, and $x+y \leq 1$ reads
    $v \leq 1$. Taking strict inequalities for the interior gives exactly $0 < v < 1$ and
    $-v < u < v$, a triangle again, but one whose horizontal slices run in the direction the
    integrand likes.
  \item \textbf{The value.} On that region the integrand is $e^{u/v}$, and $v$ is constant along
    the inner integration, so the inner integral is an exponential in $u$:
    \[I = \frac12\int_0^1\!\!\int_{-v}^{v} e^{u/v}\,du\,dv
        = \frac12\int_0^1 \Big[v\,e^{u/v}\Big]_{u=-v}^{u=v}\,dv
        = \frac{e - e^{-1}}{2}\int_0^1 v\,dv
        = \frac{e - e^{-1}}{4}.\]
  \item \textbf{The origin.} A single point is a Jordan null set, so removing it changes neither
    the Jordan measurability of $T$ nor the value of the integral. What does need checking is
    that the integral exists at all, and it does, because the integrand is \emph{bounded}: on $T$
    one has $\lvert y-x\rvert \leq y+x$, so the exponent $u/v$ lies in $[-1,1]$ and the integrand
    lies between $e^{-1}$ and $e$. It is the boundedness, and not the smallness of the bad set on
    its own, that rescues the computation.
\end{enumerate}

\begin{remark}[Two ways to choose a substitution]
\label{rem:substitution_from_the_integrand}
\Cref{ex:change_of_variables_domain} chose $\Phi$ to straighten the \emph{domain}. Here the
choice comes from the \emph{integrand}, which is a function of $y-x$ and $y+x$ and of nothing
else. That the domain also becomes pleasant is a bonus, and a mild one: the new region is still
a triangle. What made the integral collapse is that the inner integration now runs along the
direction in which the integrand is a plain exponential.
\end{remark}
\end{exercisesolution}

\begin{exercisesolution}[Solution to \cref{ex:ai_order_swap_gaussian}]
\label{sol:ai_order_swap_gaussian}
% Generator: Claude Opus 5 (High)
\begin{enumerate}[label=\textbf{(\alph*)}]
  \item The inner integral asks for an antiderivative of $y \mapsto e^{y^2}$, and there is
    none in elementary terms. Nothing about the outer integral repairs this, so the order as
    written is a dead end.
  \item The region is the triangle below the diagonal, and it admits two descriptions:
    \[R = \{(x,y) : 0 \leq x \leq 1,\ x \leq y \leq 1\}
        = \{(x,y) : 0 \leq y \leq 1,\ 0 \leq x \leq y\}.\]
    The first reads it by vertical slices, the second by horizontal ones. Taking the second and
    applying \cref{thm:fubini},
    \[I = \int_0^1\!\!\int_0^{y} e^{y^2}\,dx\,dy.\]
  \item Now $e^{y^2}$ is constant in $x$, so the inner integral contributes a factor of $y$,
    and that factor is exactly what the substitution $s := y^2$ needs:
    \[I = \int_0^1 y\,e^{y^2}\,dy = \left[\frac{e^{y^2}}{2}\right]_0^1 = \frac{e-1}{2}.\]
\end{enumerate}

\begin{remark}[What the swap actually bought]
\label{rem:order_swap_supplies_the_factor}
The reversal did not find a cleverer antiderivative. It produced a factor of $y$ that was not
there before, and $y\,e^{y^2}$ integrates where $e^{y^2}$ does not. That is the pattern worth
recognising: when the length of a slice depends on the variable that is causing the trouble,
swapping the order hands you that length as a factor in the integrand.
\end{remark}
\end{exercisesolution}

\begin{exercisesolution}[Solution to \cref{ex:ai_frullani}]
\label{sol:ai_frullani}
% Generator: Claude Opus 5 (High)
\begin{enumerate}[label=\textbf{(\alph*)}]
  \item Both exponentials equal $1$ at $x = 0$, so the quotient is a difference quotient in
    disguise. Expanding, $e^{-ax} - e^{-bx} = (b-a)x + O(x^2)$, and therefore
    \[\lim_{x\to0^+}\frac{e^{-ax} - e^{-bx}}{x} = b - a.\]
    Assigning that value at $x = 0$ makes the integrand continuous on $[0,\infty)$, and it decays
    exponentially, so the improper integral converges.
  \item Only the second exponential depends on $t$, and the $x$ that the chain rule produces
    cancels the one in the denominator:
    \[I'(t) = \int_0^\infty \frac{\partial}{\partial t}\left(\frac{e^{-ax} - e^{-tx}}{x}\right)dx
            = \int_0^\infty \frac{x\,e^{-tx}}{x}\,dx
            = \int_0^\infty e^{-tx}\,dx
            = \frac{1}{t}.\]
    That cancellation is the whole trick. Differentiating in the parameter removes the $1/x$
    which made the integrand hard, and leaves an integral of one line.
  \item At $t = a$ the two exponentials coincide and the integrand vanishes identically, so
    $I(a) = 0$. Consequently, by the fundamental theorem of calculus,
    \[\int_0^\infty \frac{e^{-ax} - e^{-bx}}{x}\,dx = I(b) = I(b) - I(a) = \int_a^b \frac{dt}{t}
      = \log\!\left(\frac{b}{a}\right).\]
    That the answer sees only the ratio $b/a$ is now visible in the derivation rather than a
    surprise, since $I'(t) = 1/t$ is scale-invariant.
\end{enumerate}

\begin{remark}[The hypotheses of \cref{thm:feynmans_trick} do not literally cover this]
\label{rem:feynman_on_a_half_line}
As stated, \cref{thm:feynmans_trick} differentiates under an integral over a \emph{compact} set
$K$, and here the domain is $[0,\infty)$. The step is still legitimate, by the same reasoning
\cref{ex:feynman_dirichlet_integral} appeals to. Fix $0 < t_0 < t_1$. For $t \in [t_0,t_1]$ the
$t$-derivative of the integrand is $e^{-tx} \leq e^{-t_0x}$, a bound that is independent of $t$
and integrable on $[0,\infty)$, so the differentiation may be carried out on $[0,R]$ and the
limit $R \to \infty$ taken afterwards. The compact statement is the one the course proves; the
dominated version is what the applications actually use.
\end{remark}
\end{exercisesolution}

\begin{exercisesolution}[Proof of \cref{ex:volume_ellipsoid_change_variables}]
\label{sol:volume_ellipsoid_change_variables}
% Generator: Gemini 3.6 Flash (High)
% Correction: Claude Opus 5 (High) --- \operatorname{vol} -> \vol, J\Phi -> \Jac\Phi, ^T ->
% \transp, and \iiint -> \int as elsewhere in this chapter.
\begin{enumerate}[label=\textbf{(\alph*)}]
  \item Define $\Phi(u,v,w) := (au, bv, cw)\transp$, and write $(x,y,z) = \Phi(u,v,w)$. Then
    $(x/a)^2 + (y/b)^2 + (z/c)^2 = u^2+v^2+w^2$, so $(u,v,w)$ lies in the closed unit ball if and
    only if $(x,y,z) \in U$. Since $a,b,c > 0$, the map $\Phi$ is a linear bijection, and therefore
    $\Phi(\overline{B_1(0)}) = U$.

  \item The Jacobian matrix of $\Phi$ is the diagonal matrix
\[\Jac\Phi = \begin{pmatrix} a & 0 & 0 \\ 0 & b & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & c \end{pmatrix},\]
    constant in $(u,v,w)$, with determinant $\det \Jac\Phi = abc > 0$.

  \item By the change of variables theorem (\cref{thm:change_of_variables}),
\[\vol_3(U) = \int_U 1 \, d\vol_3 = \int_{\overline{B_1(0)}} \lvert\det \Jac\Phi\rvert \, d\vol_3 = abc \int_{\overline{B_1(0)}} 1 \, d\vol_3 .\]
The unit ball in $\mathbb{R}^3$ has volume $\tfrac{4}{3}\pi$, and therefore
\[\vol_3(U) = \frac{4}{3} \pi abc.\]
\end{enumerate}
\end{exercisesolution}

\begin{exercisesolution}[Proof of \cref{ex:cubic_shear_area}]
\label{sol:cubic_shear_area}
% Generator: Claude Opus 5 (High)
\begin{enumerate}[label=\textbf{(\alph*)}]
  \item Differentiating each component,
\[D_x\varphi = \Jac\varphi(x) = \begin{pmatrix} 3x_1^2 & -1 \\ 1 & 1 \end{pmatrix},
  \qquad \det \Jac\varphi(x) = 3x_1^2 + 1 \ge 1 > 0 .\]

  \item The determinant never vanishes, so by the inverse function theorem
    (\cref{thm:inverse_function_theorem}) $\varphi$ is a local diffeomorphism at every point; in
    particular $\varphi(\mathbb{R}^2)$ is open. It remains to prove global injectivity, and then
    $\varphi$ is a bijection onto its image which is locally a diffeomorphism, hence a
    diffeomorphism onto its image.

    Suppose $\varphi(x) = \varphi(y)$. The second components give $x_2 + x_1 = y_2 + y_1$, that is,
    $x_2 - y_2 = -(x_1 - y_1)$. Substituting this into the equality of first components,
    $x_1^3 - x_2 = y_1^3 - y_2$, yields
\[x_1^3 - y_1^3 = x_2 - y_2 = -(x_1 - y_1),
  \qquad\text{that is,}\qquad (x_1 - y_1)\big(x_1^2 + x_1y_1 + y_1^2 + 1\big) = 0 .\]
    The second factor is at least $1$, because $x_1^2 + x_1y_1 + y_1^2 = \big(x_1 +
    \tfrac{y_1}{2}\big)^2 + \tfrac34 y_1^2 \ge 0$. Hence $x_1 = y_1$, and then $x_2 = y_2$.

  \item By part \textbf{(a)} the Jacobian determinant is positive, so the change of variables
    theorem (\cref{thm:change_of_variables}) gives
\[\vol_2(\varphi(Q)) = \int_Q \lvert\det \Jac\varphi\rvert \, dx
  = \int_0^1\!\!\int_0^1 \big(3x_1^2 + 1\big) \, dx_2\,dx_1
  = \int_0^1 \big(3x_1^2+1\big)\,dx_1 = 1 + 1 = 2 .\]
\end{enumerate}
\begin{remark}
The map is not linear, so the image of the square is not a parallelogram and there is no single
factor by which areas scale. The change of variables formula handles that by scaling
\emph{pointwise} and integrating the result, which is the whole reason the determinant appears
inside the integral rather than outside it.
\end{remark}
\end{exercisesolution}

\begin{exercisesolution}[Solution of \cref{ex:gaussian_second_moment_polar}]
\label{sol:gaussian_second_moment_polar}
% Generator: Claude Opus 5 (High)
Following the hint, the substitution $(x,y) \mapsto (y,x)$ is a linear change of variables with
Jacobian determinant $-1$, and it carries the integrand $x^2e^{-\alpha(x^2+y^2)}$ to
$y^2e^{-\alpha(x^2+y^2)}$. Hence the two integrals agree, and adding them gives
\[2I_\alpha = \int_{\mathbb{R}^2} (x^2+y^2)\,e^{-\alpha(x^2+y^2)} \, dx\,dy .\]
The integrand is now radial, which is what makes polar coordinates pay. Writing $x = r\cos\theta$,
$y = r\sin\theta$, with Jacobian determinant $r$,
\[2I_\alpha = \int_0^{2\pi}\!\!\int_0^\infty r^2 e^{-\alpha r^2}\, r\,dr\,d\theta
  = 2\pi \int_0^\infty r^3 e^{-\alpha r^2}\,dr .\]
Substituting $u := r^2$, so that $du = 2r\,dr$, turns the remaining integral into one that can be
done by parts:
\[\int_0^\infty r^3 e^{-\alpha r^2}\,dr = \frac12 \int_0^\infty u e^{-\alpha u}\,du
  = \frac{1}{2\alpha^2}.\]
Therefore $2I_\alpha = 2\pi \cdot \frac{1}{2\alpha^2} = \frac{\pi}{\alpha^2}$, and
\[I_\alpha = \frac{\pi}{2\alpha^2}.\]
\begin{remark}
% Correction: Claude Opus 5 (Ultracode) --- the second check read
% "$\tfrac12\cdot\tfrac{1}{2\alpha}\cdot\tfrac{\pi}{\alpha}$", which is $\pi/(4\alpha^2)$: half
% the value it was offered as confirming, and the sentence asserted "which it does". The leading
% $\tfrac12$ is spurious. The integrand already factorises as $x^2e^{-\alpha x^2}$ times
% $e^{-\alpha y^2}$, so the two one-dimensional moments multiply with nothing in front. Written
% out, since a check that has to be re-derived to be trusted is not doing its job.
Two sanity checks come free. Scaling $(x,y) \mapsto (x,y)/\sqrt\alpha$ predicts $I_\alpha =
\alpha^{-2} I_1$, which the answer satisfies; and the integrand factorises as
$x^2e^{-\alpha x^2}\cdot e^{-\alpha y^2}$, so $I_\alpha$ is the product of the two
one-dimensional moments,
\[\left(\int_{\mathbb{R}} x^2e^{-\alpha x^2}dx\right)\left(\int_{\mathbb{R}} e^{-\alpha y^2}dy\right)
  = \frac{1}{2\alpha}\sqrt{\frac{\pi}{\alpha}}\cdot\sqrt{\frac{\pi}{\alpha}}
  = \frac{1}{2\alpha}\cdot\frac{\pi}{\alpha} = \frac{\pi}{2\alpha^2},\]
as found. The symmetry trick in the first line is the same one that
evaluates $\int_{\mathbb{R}} e^{-x^2}dx$ by squaring it: an integral that resists in Cartesian
coordinates becomes radial once it is paired with its own mirror image.
\end{remark}
\end{exercisesolution}

\begin{exercisesolution}[Proof of \cref{ex:examHS24_TF_image_of_small_sets}]
\label{sol:image_of_small_sets_tf}
% Generator: Claude Opus 5 (High)
Everything turns on a single number:
\[\det\Jac\Phi(0,0) = 2\cdot 1 - 1\cdot(-1) = 3 .\]
In particular $\Jac\Phi(0,0)$ is invertible, so the inverse function theorem
(\cref{thm:inverse_function_theorem}) supplies an open ball $V$ around the origin on which $\Phi$
is a $C^1$-diffeomorphism onto an open set. Since $\Phi$ is $C^1$, the map
$x \mapsto \lvert\det\Jac\Phi(x)\rvert$ is continuous, and therefore
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:examHS24_A6_determinant_continuity}
\lvert\det\Jac\Phi(x)\rvert = 3 + \varepsilon(x), \qquad \text{where } \varepsilon(x) \to 0
\text{ as } x \to 0 .
\end{equation}
For a Jordan measurable $E$ with $\overline{E} \subseteq V$, the change of variables formula
(\cref{thm:change_of_variables}), taken in the multiplied form $\int_{\Phi(E)} f = \int_E
(f\circ\Phi)\,\lvert\det\Jac\Phi\rvert$ recorded just below it and applied to $f \equiv 1$, reads
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:examHS24_A6_volume_of_image}
\vol_2\big(\Phi(E)\big) = \int_E \lvert\det\Jac\Phi(x)\rvert \, dx .
\end{equation}
Parts \textbf{(b)} and \textbf{(c)} are both this identity, read against
\eqref{eq:examHS24_A6_determinant_continuity}.
\begin{enumerate}[label=\textbf{(\alph*)}]
  \item \textbf{True.} For $r$ small enough we have $B_r(0,0) \subseteq V$, and a diffeomorphism is
    in particular an open map. Hence $\Phi(B_r(0,0))$ is open.
  \item \textbf{False.} Applying \eqref{eq:examHS24_A6_volume_of_image} to $E = B_r(0,0)$ and
    substituting \eqref{eq:examHS24_A6_determinant_continuity},
    \[\vol_2\big(\Phi(B_r(0,0))\big) = \int_{B_r(0,0)} \big(3 + \varepsilon(x)\big)\,dx
      = 3\pi r^2 + \int_{B_r(0,0)} \varepsilon(x)\,dx .\]
    The remaining integral is bounded in absolute value by $\pi r^2 \sup_{B_r(0,0)}
    \lvert\varepsilon\rvert$, and that supremum tends to $0$ with $r$, so the term is $o(r^2)$. The
    correct expansion is $3\pi r^2 + o(r^2)$. The stated coefficient $2$ is the top-left entry of
    the Jacobian rather than its determinant.
  \item \textbf{True.} Write $R_r := [0,r] \times [0, ar^{1/2}]$, a rectangle of area $ar^{3/2}$.
    Every point of $R_r$ has norm at most $\sqrt{r^2 + a^2 r}$, which tends to $0$, so
    $R_r \subseteq V$ for $r$ small enough and \eqref{eq:examHS24_A6_volume_of_image} applies:
    \[\vol_2\big(\Phi(R_r)\big) = \int_{R_r} \big(3 + \varepsilon(x)\big)\,dx
      = 3ar^{3/2} + \int_{R_r} \varepsilon(x)\,dx ,\]
    and the error is bounded by $ar^{3/2}\sup_{R_r}\lvert\varepsilon\rvert = o(r^{3/2})$.
\end{enumerate}
\begin{remark}[Why part \textbf{(c)} is not simply part \textbf{(b)} again]
Under \eqref{eq:examHS24_A6_volume_of_image} the two parts really are the same computation, and
that is the point of the pair. The tempting alternative route is not. One is inclined to replace
$\Phi$ by its linearisation, $\Phi(x) = \Jac\Phi(0,0)x + o(\lvert x\rvert)$, and read the volume
off the linear map. For the ball that argument can be made to work, since the error
$o(\lvert x\rvert) = o(r)$ is small against the radius $r$ in every direction. For $R_r$ it breaks:
the rectangle has sides $r$ and $ar^{1/2}$, so its diameter is of order $r^{1/2}$ while its short
side is of order $r$, and the error term $o(r^{1/2})$ is then far \emph{larger} than the side it
would have to be weighed against. The change of variables formula never approximates $\Phi$ at all.
It integrates the exact local scaling factor, and the only approximation left is to that factor's
value, uniformly over a set shrinking to a point.
\end{remark}
\begin{ainote}
The set $X := \Phi^{-1}((0,0)\transp)$ is introduced in the exam's statement and then used by none
of the three items. It is reproduced in \cref{ex:examHS24_TF_image_of_small_sets} for fidelity to
the source, and nothing above depends on it. A little can be said about it in passing: the inverse
function theorem makes $\Phi$ injective near the origin, so $X$ meets a small ball around $(0,0)$
in that single point, while away from the origin the hypotheses constrain $X$ not at all.
\end{ainote}
\end{exercisesolution}

\begin{exercisesolution}[Proof of \cref{ex:examHS24_TF_affine_volume_scaling}]
\label{sol:affine_volume_scaling_tf}
% Generator: Claude Opus 5 (High)
The map is affine, $\Psi(v) = Mv + b$, with
\[M = \begin{pmatrix} -1 & 0 & 0 \\ 0 & 1 & \alpha \\ 0 & \alpha & 1 \end{pmatrix},
  \qquad b = \begin{pmatrix} e^\pi \\ 0 \\ 0 \end{pmatrix} .\]
Expanding along the first row gives $\det M = -(1 - \alpha^2) = \alpha^2 - 1$. A translation does
not change volume, so for every Jordan measurable $E$,
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:examHS24_A7_scaling}
\vol_3\big(\Psi(E)\big) = \lvert\det M\rvert \, \vol_3(E)
  = \lvert\alpha^2 - 1\rvert \, \vol_3(E) .
\end{equation}
Iterating $\Psi$ iterates its linear part, so $\Psi^k$ is affine with linear part $M^k$ and
\eqref{eq:examHS24_A7_scaling} gives $\vol_3(\Psi^k(E)) = \lvert\det M\rvert^k \vol_3(E)$. Every
item is now a matter of evaluating $\lvert\alpha^2-1\rvert$.
\begin{enumerate}[label=\textbf{(\alph*)}]
  \item \textbf{False.} By \eqref{eq:examHS24_A7_scaling}, $\vol_3(\Psi(E))$ vanishes for every $E$
    precisely when $\det M = 0$, that is when $\alpha^2 = 1$. That holds for $\alpha = 1$
    \emph{and} for $\alpha = -1$, so the \qt{only if} direction fails. The correct criterion is
    $\alpha = \pm 1$.
  \item \textbf{True.} For $\alpha = -2$ we get $\det M = 4 - 1 = 3$, and therefore
    $\vol_3(\Psi^k(E)) = 3^k \vol_3(E)$ for every $k \ge 0$.
  \item \textbf{True.} For $\alpha = 0$ we get $\det M = -1$, so $\lvert\det M\rvert = 1$ and every
    iterate preserves volume, the $2025$th included.
  \item \textbf{False.} The inverse of an affine map with invertible linear part $M$ is affine with
    linear part $M^{-1}$, so for $\alpha \in (0,1)$, where $1 - \alpha^2 > 0$,
    \[\vol_3\big(\Psi^{-1}([0,1]^3)\big) = \frac{\vol_3([0,1]^3)}{\lvert\det M\rvert}
      = \frac{1}{1-\alpha^2} .\]
    The stated value is the negative of this, and no volume is negative.
\end{enumerate}
\begin{remark}
Item \textbf{(d)} can be dismissed before any computation: the left-hand side is a volume and the
right-hand side is negative throughout $(0,1)$. Item \textbf{(a)} is the one that rewards care.
Writing the scaling factor as $\lvert\alpha^2-1\rvert$ puts both roots in view at once, and a
criterion of the form \qt{if and only if $\alpha = 1$} then fails for the cheapest of reasons: it
forgets that squaring loses a sign.
\end{remark}
\end{exercisesolution}

\label{sol:spherical_coordinates_integral_tf}
% Generator: Gemini 3.1 Pro (High)
\begin{enumerate}[label=\textbf{(\alph*)}]
  \item \textbf{True:} On the restricted domain $(0, 2\pi) \times (0, \pi) \times (0, \infty)$, the map $\Psi$ is injective. The angles $\phi \in (0, 2\pi)$ and $\theta \in (0, \pi)$ uniquely parameterise points away from the half-plane $\{(x,0,z) \mid x \ge 0\}$ and the $z$-axis.
  \item \textbf{False:} The image of $\Psi$ omits the set where $\phi = 0$ (which corresponds to the half-plane $y = 0, x \ge 0$) and the $z$-axis (where $\theta = 0$ or $\theta = \pi$, or $r=0$). Therefore, it does not cover all of $\mathbb{R}^3$.

Computing the Jacobian matrix for this specific ordering gives a determinant of $-r^2 \sin \theta$, not $+r^2 \sin \theta$. While the \emph{absolute value} of the determinant (which appears in the change of variables formula) is $r^2 \sin \theta$, the determinant itself is negative because $(\phi, \theta, r)$ is not an orientation-preserving ordering of the standard spherical coordinates.
  \item \textbf{False:} The volume of a ball of radius $R$ in $\mathbb{R}^3$ is $\frac{4}{3}\pi R^3$. Therefore, $\vol_3(B_R \setminus B_r) = \frac{4}{3}\pi(R^3 - r^3)$, not $\pi(R^3 - r^3)$.

  \item \textbf{True:} This limit represents the derivative of the volume $V(R) = \frac{4}{3}\pi R^3$ with respect to $R$, evaluated at $R=1$. We have $\frac{d}{dR} \big(\frac{4}{3}\pi R^3\big) = 4\pi R^2$, which at $R=1$ evaluates to $4\pi$ (this is exactly the surface area of the unit sphere).

\begin{exercisesolution}[Solution to \cref{ex:examHS24_box_linear_vol}]
\label{sol:linear_map_volume_scaling_box}
% Generator: Gemini 3.1 Pro (High)
For any linear map $\Phi \colon \mathbb{R}^2 \to \mathbb{R}^2$, the change of variables formula
implies that the volume of the image of a Jordan measurable set $E$ is given by
\[ \vol_2(\Phi(E)) = \lvert\det \Phi\rvert \cdot \vol_2(E). \]
To satisfy the condition $\vol_2(\Phi(E)) = 2 \vol_2(E)$ for all $E$, we must have
$\lvert\det \Phi\rvert = 2$. Any matrix with determinant $\pm 2$ will work. An explicit and simple
choice is the diagonal matrix
\[ \Phi(x,y) = (2x, y) \quad \text{or equivalently, the matrix } \begin{pmatrix} 2 & 0 \\ 0 & 1 \end{pmatrix}. \]
Other valid choices include $\Phi(x,y) = (x, 2y)$ or $\Phi(x,y) = (\sqrt{2}x, \sqrt{2}y)$.
\end{exercisesolution}

\begin{exercisesolution}[Proof of \cref{ex:ai_spanned_ellipsoid_volume}]
\label{sol:ai_spanned_ellipsoid_volume}
% Generator: Gemini 3.1 Pro (High)
Let $T \colon \mathbb{R}^3 \to \mathbb{R}^3$ be the linear map $T(t_1, t_2, t_3) := t_1 v_1 +
t_2 v_2 + t_3 v_3$. Its matrix in the standard basis has columns $v_1, v_2, v_3$, so
$\det T = \det(v_1, v_2, v_3) = 2$, and $A$ is precisely the image under $T$ of the closed unit ball
$B := \{ t \in \mathbb{R}^3 \mid t_1^2 + t_2^2 + t_3^2 \le 1 \}$.
\begin{enumerate}[label=\textbf{(\alph*)}]
  \item \textbf{True:} By the change of variables formula (\cref{thm:change_of_variables}), or
    equally by the geometric reading of the determinant,
    \[ \vol_3(A) = \vol_3(T(B)) = \lvert\det T\rvert \cdot \vol_3(B)
      = 2 \cdot \frac{4}{3}\pi = \frac{8\pi}{3}. \]
  \item \textbf{True:} The unit ball $B$ is Jordan measurable, being bounded with boundary the unit
    sphere, a smooth surface and hence of volume zero. The image of a Jordan measurable set under
    an invertible linear map is again Jordan measurable.
  \item \textbf{False:} By part \textbf{(a)} the volume depends on the $v_i$ only through
    $\det(v_1,v_2,v_3)$, which the hypothesis fixes at $2$. Every admissible choice therefore
    yields the same volume $\frac{8\pi}{3}$.
\end{enumerate}
\end{exercisesolution}

\begin{exercisesolution}[Solution of \cref{ex:radial_exponential_integral}]
\label{sol:radial_exponential_integral}
% Generator: Claude Opus 5 (High)
The integrand is continuous and positive on $\mathbb{R}^2$, so the improper integral of
\cref{def:improper_integral} is well defined, and by \cref{lem:improper_integral_as_limit} it may
be computed along the exhaustion by closed disks. In polar coordinates the integrand depends only
on $r = \sqrt{x^2+y^2}$, so the angular integral contributes $2\pi$ and
\[I_\alpha = 2\pi\int_0^\infty e^{-\alpha r}\,r\,dr .\]
Integrating by parts with $u := r$ and $v' := e^{-\alpha r}$,
\[\int_0^R r\,e^{-\alpha r}\,dr
  = \left[-\frac{r}{\alpha}e^{-\alpha r}\right]_0^R + \frac{1}{\alpha}\int_0^R e^{-\alpha r}\,dr
  = -\frac{R}{\alpha}e^{-\alpha R} + \frac{1}{\alpha^2}\big(1 - e^{-\alpha R}\big).\]
Both exponential terms vanish as $R \to \infty$ because $\alpha > 0$, so
$\int_0^\infty r e^{-\alpha r}\,dr = 1/\alpha^2$ and
\[\boxed{\;I_\alpha = \frac{2\pi}{\alpha^2}\;}\]
The three statements now follow.
\begin{enumerate}[label=\textbf{(\alph*)}]
  \item \textbf{$I_1 = \pi$: false.} $I_1 = 2\pi$.
  \item \textbf{$I_2 = \pi/2$: true.} $I_2 = 2\pi/4 = \pi/2$.
  \item \textbf{$\lim_{\alpha\to+\infty}\alpha I_\alpha = 0$: true.} $\alpha I_\alpha = 2\pi/\alpha
    \to 0$.
\end{enumerate}
\begin{remark}[Why the $r$ changes the answer so much]
Without the Jacobian factor $r$ the radial integral would be $\int_0^\infty e^{-\alpha r}dr =
1/\alpha$, and the decay in $\alpha$ would be one power slower. The factor $r$ suppresses the
region near the origin, where the integrand is largest, and it is exactly what turns $1/\alpha$
into $1/\alpha^2$. Item \textbf{(c)} is a check on that power and nothing else: it is
$\alpha I_\alpha$ rather than $I_\alpha$ precisely so that the answer $0$ distinguishes
$\alpha^{-2}$ from $\alpha^{-1}$.
\end{remark}
\end{exercisesolution}

\begin{exercisesolution}[Solution of \cref{ex:box_linear_volume_preserving}]
\label{sol:box_linear_volume_preserving}
% Generator: Claude Opus 5 (High)
By \cref{thm:change_of_variables}, a linear $\Phi$ scales every Jordan measurable set's volume by
$\lvert\det\Phi\rvert$, so the requirement is exactly $\lvert\det\Phi\rvert = 1$ together with
$\Phi \neq \id$. The shear
\[\Phi(x,y) := (x+y,\ y), \qquad
  \Phi = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 1 \\ 0 & 1\end{pmatrix}, \qquad \det\Phi = 1,\]
does it. So do the reflection $(x,y)\mapsto(y,x)$ and the point reflection
$(x,y)\mapsto(-x,-y)$, both of determinant $-1$, and every rotation.
\begin{remark}[What the extra clause is for]
Dropping \qt{different from the identity} would make the problem vacuous, and adding it makes the
answer informative: the volume-preserving linear maps of the plane form a large group, namely
$\{A \in \GL_2(\mathbb{R}) \mid \lvert\det A\rvert = 1\}$, and the identity is one point of it. The
shear above is the example worth remembering, because it is the one that visibly distorts a square
into a parallelogram while changing nothing about its area. Length, angle and shape are all
destroyed; only area survives, and area is all the change of variables formula ever sees.
\end{remark}
\end{exercisesolution}

\begin{exercisesolution}[\cref{ex:linear_substitution_elementary_matrices}]
\label{sol:linear_substitution_elementary_matrices}
\begin{enumerate}[label=\textbf{(\alph*)}]
  \item Let $A = \operatorname{diag}(\lambda_1,\dots,\lambda_n)$ with all $\lambda_i \neq 0$, so
    that $\det A = \lambda_1\cdots\lambda_n$. For a box
    $Q = [a_1,b_1]\times\dots\times[a_n,b_n]$ the image $A(Q)$ is again a box, the $i$-th edge
    running between $\lambda_ia_i$ and $\lambda_ib_i$, of length
    $\lvert\lambda_i\rvert(b_i-a_i)$. Hence
    \[\vol\big(A(Q)\big) = \prod_{i=1}^n \lvert\lambda_i\rvert(b_i-a_i)
      = \lvert\det A\rvert\,\vol(Q).\]
    For general Jordan measurable $B$, approximate from inside and outside by dyadic sets
    $F \subseteq B \subseteq G$ with $\vol(G)-\vol(F) < \varepsilon$
    (\cref{def:dyadic_set}). Since $A$ is a bijection, $A(F) \subseteq A(B) \subseteq A(G)$, and
    both $A(F)$ and $A(G)$ are finite disjoint unions of boxes, to which the computation above
    applies cube by cube. Therefore
    \[\lvert\det A\rvert\,\vol(F) \le \mu_{\mathrm{in}}\big(A(B)\big)
      \le \mu_{\mathrm{out}}\big(A(B)\big) \le \lvert\det A\rvert\,\vol(G),\]
    and the two outer quantities differ by less than $\lvert\det A\rvert\,\varepsilon$. Letting
    $\varepsilon \to 0$ shows $A(B)$ is Jordan measurable with
    $\vol(A(B)) = \lvert\det A\rvert\,\vol(B)$.

  \item Two observations combine. First, \eqref{eq:linear_substitution_rule} is multiplicative: if
    it holds for $A_1$ and for $A_2$, then applying it twice gives
    \[\vol\big(A_1A_2(B)\big) = \lvert\det A_1\rvert\,\vol\big(A_2(B)\big)
      = \lvert\det A_1\rvert\,\lvert\det A_2\rvert\,\vol(B)
      = \lvert\det(A_1A_2)\rvert\,\vol(B),\]
    using $\det(A_1A_2) = \det A_1 \det A_2$. So the set of matrices satisfying
    \eqref{eq:linear_substitution_rule} is closed under products. Second, Gaussian elimination
    writes any invertible $A$ as a product of elementary matrices, and each elementary matrix is a
    diagonal matrix, a permutation matrix or a shear. Part \textbf{(a)} handles the diagonal
    factors, so proving the permutation and shear cases finishes the proof for every invertible
    $A$.

  \item A permutation matrix $P_\pi$ sends a box to the box with its edges relabelled, so it
    preserves volume, and $\lvert\det P_\pi\rvert = 1$: the case is immediate.

    For the shear, take $A$ to be the identity with one off-diagonal entry $c$, say
    $A e_j = e_j$ for $j \neq 2$ and $Ae_2 = e_2 + c\,e_1$; every shear is of this form after a
    permutation. Then $A$ is triangular with unit diagonal, so $\det A = 1$, and the claim is that
    $A$ preserves volume. Let $Q = [a_1,b_1]\times\dots\times[a_n,b_n]$. Writing points as
    $(x_1,x_2,x')$ with $x' \in \mathbb{R}^{n-2}$, we have
    $A(x_1,x_2,x') = (x_1 + cx_2,\; x_2,\; x')$. Compute $\vol(A(Q))$ by Fubini
    (\cref{thm:fubini}), integrating in $x_1$ first with $x_2$ and $x'$ held fixed. For fixed
    $x_2 \in [a_2,b_2]$ and $x' $, the slice of $A(Q)$ is the interval
    $[a_1+cx_2,\ b_1+cx_2]$, a \emph{translate} of $[a_1,b_1]$, of the same length $b_1-a_1$.
    Since every slice has the length of the corresponding slice of $Q$, integrating over the
    remaining variables gives $\vol(A(Q)) = \vol(Q) = \lvert\det A\rvert\,\vol(Q)$.
\end{enumerate}
\begin{remark}[Where the determinant actually enters]
Only part \textbf{(a)} ever multiplies anything by a determinant, and there it is nothing but the
product of the edge-scaling factors, the determinant of a diagonal matrix being visible to the
naked eye. The shear contributes the factor $1$, and the permutation contributes $1$: neither case
requires knowing what a determinant is. So the whole content of
\eqref{eq:linear_substitution_rule} is the trivial diagonal computation, plus the fact that
$\det$ is multiplicative and elementary matrices generate $\GL_n(\mathbb{R})$. That is a good
answer to the question of why $\lvert\det A\rvert$, of all functions of $A$, is the volume
distortion factor: it is the only multiplicative function that scales correctly on diagonal
matrices and is blind to shears.

Part \textbf{(c)} is worth reading once more for the shape of the argument. A shear preserves
volume because it slides each line parallel to $e_1$ along itself, and a translated interval has
the same length. Cavalieri's principle in one sentence, and the reason a leaning stack of coins has
the volume of a straight one.
\end{remark}
\end{exercisesolution}

\begin{exercisesolution}[Solution to \cref{ex:gaussian_slab_alpha_asymptotics}]
\label{sol:gaussian_slab_alpha_asymptotics}
% Generator: Claude Opus 5 (High) --- the three statements are the examiner's; the working is
% written for these notes. Verdicts checked afterwards against exercises_2024/mocksol.pdf and
% agree on all three.
The integrand factors as $e^{-x^2}\cdot e^{-y^2}\cdot e^{-\alpha\lvert z\rvert}$, one factor per
variable. It is continuous and non-negative, so Fubini's theorem splits the integral into a
product of three one-dimensional ones, and the value in $[0,+\infty]$ is the same whichever
exhaustion of $\mathbb{R}^3$ computes it. Two of the three factors are Gaussians, so
\[I_\alpha = \left(\int_{\mathbb{R}} e^{-x^2}dx\right)
  \left(\int_{\mathbb{R}} e^{-y^2}dy\right)
  \int_{\mathbb{R}} e^{-\alpha\lvert z\rvert}dz
  = \pi \int_{\mathbb{R}} e^{-\alpha\lvert z\rvert}dz .\]
The remaining factor is where $\alpha$ finally matters. For $\alpha > 0$ the integrand is even
and integrable,
\[\int_{\mathbb{R}} e^{-\alpha\lvert z\rvert}dz = 2\int_0^\infty e^{-\alpha z}dz = \frac{2}{\alpha},
  \qquad\text{so}\qquad I_\alpha = \frac{2\pi}{\alpha};\]
for $\alpha = 0$ the integrand is the constant $1$ and the integral is $+\infty$.
\begin{enumerate}[label=\textbf{(\alph*)}]
  \item \textbf{False.} $I_0 = +\infty$: the $z$-direction is not damped at all, so the integrand
    is constant along every vertical line. The value $\sqrt{\pi}$ is what a \emph{single} Gaussian
    factor contributes, which is the trap.
  \item \textbf{True} for every $\alpha > 0$, since $\alpha I_\alpha = \alpha\cdot 2\pi/\alpha =
    2\pi$ and $I_1 = 2\pi$. Note that the product is independent of $\alpha$, which is the
    content of the statement.
  \item \textbf{True.} Substituting the closed form,
    \[\big(\alpha+\sqrt{\alpha}\big)\frac{2\pi}{\alpha}
      = 2\pi\left(1 + \frac{1}{\sqrt\alpha}\right) \xrightarrow[\alpha\to+\infty]{} 2\pi .\]
    The $\sqrt\alpha$ is there only to be negligible; the statement is that $I_\alpha$ decays
    exactly like $2\pi/\alpha$ and not faster.
\end{enumerate}

\begin{ainote}
Part \textbf{(b)} is stated for the whole range $\alpha \geq 0$ the problem opens with, and at
$\alpha = 0$ it reads $0\cdot(+\infty)$, which is not a real number. The identity is to be read
for $\alpha > 0$, where it holds; the official key marks it true without comment.
\end{ainote}
\end{exercisesolution}

\begin{exercisesolution}[Proof of \cref{ex:centroid_additivity_and_triangle}]
\label{sol:centroid_additivity_and_triangle}
% Generator: Claude Opus 5 (High) --- the statement is the examiner's; the proof and the remark
% are written for these notes. Probeprfg3_Lsg.pdf gives the same two results.
\begin{enumerate}[label=\textbf{(\alph*)}]
  \item Jordan content is additive on pairwise disjoint Jordan measurable sets, so
    $\vol_2(B) = \sum_i \vol_2(B_i)$, and the integral is additive over the same decomposition.
    Applying the latter to the coordinate function $x$ and then reading each summand through
    \cref{def:centroid},
    \[\int_B x \,d\vol_2 = \sum_{i=1}^n \int_{B_i} x \,d\vol_2
      = \sum_{i=1}^n \vol_2(B_i)\,x_{B_i} .\]
    Dividing by $\vol_2(B)$ gives the first coordinate of the claim, and $y$ gives the second.
  \item Parametrise the triangle by the affine map that carries the standard triangle
    $T := \{(u,v) \mid u,v \geq 0,\ u+v \leq 1\}$ onto $B$, namely
    \[\Phi(u,v) := u\,p + v\,q = (ur+vs,\ vt), \qquad
      \Jac\Phi = \begin{pmatrix} r & s \\ 0 & t \end{pmatrix}, \qquad
      \det\Jac\Phi = rt > 0 .\]
    It is injective, since $\det\Jac\Phi \neq 0$ and $\Phi$ is affine, so
    \cref{thm:change_of_variables} applies. First the area,
    \[\vol_2(B) = \int_T rt \,du\,dv = rt\,\vol_2(T) = \tfrac{rt}{2},\]
    and then the two coordinate integrals at once, writing them as the single
    $\mathbb{R}^2$-valued integral $\int_B (x,y)\,d\vol_2$:
    \[\int_B (x,y)\,d\vol_2 = \int_T \Phi(u,v)\,rt \,du\,dv
      = rt\left(p\int_T u \,du\,dv + q\int_T v \,du\,dv\right).\]
    Both remaining integrals equal $\int_0^1 u(1-u)\,du = \tfrac12-\tfrac13 = \tfrac16$, the
    second by the symmetry of $T$ in $u$ and $v$. Hence
    \[z_B = \frac{rt\cdot\tfrac16(p+q)}{\tfrac{rt}{2}} = \tfrac13(p+q) = \tfrac13(o+p+q),\]
    the last step because $o = 0$.
\end{enumerate}

\begin{remark}[Part \textbf{(a)} is what makes part \textbf{(b)} useful]
On its own, the triangle formula is a curiosity. Together with the additivity it computes the
centroid of \emph{any} polygon, since every polygon can be cut into triangles: take the
area-weighted average of the vertex averages. That is the whole algorithm, and it needs no
integral once part \textbf{(b)} is known.

Note also that nothing in the computation used the particular placement of the vertices. Any
triangle is the image of $T$ under some invertible affine map, and $z_B = \tfrac13(o+p+q)$ says
that the centroid is carried along by that map --- an affine map sends the centroid of a region
to the centroid of the image, because it commutes with averaging. The same is not true of the
circumcentre or of any other centre a triangle has.
\end{remark}
\end{exercisesolution}

\begin{exercisesolution}[Solution to \cref{ex:centroid_minimises_second_moment}]
\label{sol:centroid_minimises_second_moment}
% Generator: Claude Opus 5 (High) --- part (a) is the examiner's; part (b), the completing-the-
% square proof and the remark are written for these notes. The official key does part (a) by
% differentiating under the integral sign and does not observe that the answer is the centroid;
% see the AI-Note.
One expansion settles both parts, so it is done first, for a general Jordan measurable $B$ with
$V := \vol_2(B) > 0$. Expanding the integrand and integrating term by term,
\begin{align*}
  I(s,t) &= \int_B (x^2+y^2)\,d\vol_2 - 2s\int_B x\,d\vol_2 - 2t\int_B y\,d\vol_2
    + (s^2+t^2)V \\
  &= \int_B (x^2+y^2)\,d\vol_2 - 2sVx_B - 2tVy_B + (s^2+t^2)V ,
\end{align*}
using \cref{def:centroid} in the second line. Completing the square in $s$ and in $t$,
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:second_moment_completed_square}
  I(s,t) = V\Big[(s-x_B)^2 + (t-y_B)^2\Big]
    + \underbrace{\int_B (x^2+y^2)\,d\vol_2 - V\big(x_B^2+y_B^2\big)}_{\text{independent of }(s,t)} .
\end{equation}

\begin{enumerate}[label=\textbf{(\alph*)}]
  \item For the quarter disc the area is $V = \pi/4$, and in polar coordinates, with $\theta$
    running over $[0,\pi/2]$,
    \[\int_B x \,d\vol_2 = \int_0^{\pi/2}\!\!\int_0^1 (r\cos\theta)\,r\,dr\,d\theta
      = \left(\int_0^1 r^2\,dr\right)\left(\int_0^{\pi/2}\cos\theta\,d\theta\right)
      = \frac13 .\]
    Hence $x_B = (1/3)/(\pi/4) = 4/(3\pi)$, and $y_B = 4/(3\pi)$ by the symmetry of $B$ in the
    diagonal. By part \textbf{(b)} the minimising pair is
    \[(s,t) = z_B = \left(\frac{4}{3\pi},\ \frac{4}{3\pi}\right) \approx (0.4244,\ 0.4244).\]
  \item In \eqref{eq:second_moment_completed_square} the bracket is non-negative, vanishes
    exactly when $(s,t) = z_B$, and is multiplied by $V > 0$; the remaining term does not depend
    on $(s,t)$ at all. So $I$ is minimised at $z_B$ and at no other point.
\end{enumerate}

\begin{remark}[The leftover constant is the parallel axis theorem]
\Cref{eq:second_moment_completed_square} says more than where the minimum is. Setting
$z := (s,t)$ and evaluating the constant term by putting $z = z_B$, it reads
\[I(z) = \vol_2(B)\,\lvert z - z_B\rvert^2 + I(z_B),\]
so the second moment about \emph{any} point is the second moment about the centroid plus the
area times the squared distance between the two. That is Steiner's parallel axis theorem, and it
is the reason the centroid is the point mechanics cares about: it is the unique point about which
a body is easiest to spin, and the penalty for choosing any other grows quadratically with how
far off you are.
\end{remark}

\begin{ainote}
The official key answers part \textbf{(a)} by differentiating under the integral sign to get
$\int_B -2(x-s)\,d\vol_2 = 0$, then solving. That is correct and shorter, but it produces only a
critical point, so convexity still has to be invoked to know it is a minimum, and it never
records that the answer is the centroid of $B$. Completing the square gives the location, the
uniqueness, the general statement and Steiner's theorem in one line, which is why it is used
above.
\end{ainote}
\end{exercisesolution}
