\begin{exercisesolution}[Proof of \cref{ex:path_concatenation_work}]
\label{sol:path_concatenation_work}
% Generator: Claude Opus 5 (High)
\textbf{The concatenation is an admissible path.} First check that $\gamma * \sigma$ is defined at
all. The two formulas overlap at $t = \tfrac12$, where they give $\sigma(1)$ and $\gamma(0)$; these
agree by hypothesis, so $\gamma*\sigma$ is a well-defined continuous map $[0,1] \to U$. It is
piecewise $C^1$ but in general \emph{not} $C^1$: at $t=\tfrac12$ the one-sided derivatives are
$2\sigma'(1)$ and $2\gamma'(0)$, and nothing forces those to agree. This is exactly why
\cref{def:line_integral} is stated for piecewise $C^1$ paths, and the single exceptional point does
not affect any of the integrals below.

\textbf{The computation.} Split the defining integral at $t = \tfrac12$:
\[\int_{\gamma*\sigma} F \cdot d(\gamma*\sigma)
  = \int_0^{1} F\big((\gamma*\sigma)(t)\big)\cdot(\gamma*\sigma)'(t)\,dt
  = \underbrace{\int_0^{1/2}\!\!\cdots}_{=:A} \ +\ \underbrace{\int_{1/2}^{1}\!\!\cdots}_{=:B}.\]
On $(0,\tfrac12)$ we have $(\gamma*\sigma)(t) = \sigma(2t)$, so by the chain rule
$(\gamma*\sigma)'(t) = 2\sigma'(2t)$ and
\[A = \int_0^{1/2} F\big(\sigma(2t)\big)\cdot 2\sigma'(2t)\,dt
    = \int_0^1 F\big(\sigma(s)\big)\cdot\sigma'(s)\,ds = \int_\sigma F\cdot d\sigma,\]
substituting $s := 2t$, $ds = 2\,dt$, which carries $t \in [0,\tfrac12]$ onto $s \in [0,1]$. On
$(\tfrac12,1)$ we have $(\gamma*\sigma)(t) = \gamma(2t-1)$ and $(\gamma*\sigma)'(t) =
2\gamma'(2t-1)$, so with $s := 2t-1$, again $ds = 2\,dt$ and $t \in [\tfrac12,1]$ onto
$s \in [0,1]$,
\[B = \int_{1/2}^{1} F\big(\gamma(2t-1)\big)\cdot 2\gamma'(2t-1)\,dt
    = \int_0^1 F\big(\gamma(s)\big)\cdot\gamma'(s)\,ds = \int_\gamma F\cdot d\gamma.\]
Adding $A$ and $B$ gives the claim. Note that $F$ enters only through its continuity; the
hypothesis $F \in C^1$ is more than the statement needs.

\textbf{In terms of work.} The work done against a force field over a journey is the sum of the
work done over its legs. Nothing about the field is required for this --- not conservativity, not
even path independence --- because it is a statement about the \emph{path}, not the field: the
integral does not know that the journey was made in two stages.
\begin{remark}[The factor $2$ is the whole proof, and it is not a coincidence]
\label{rem:line_integral_reparametrisation_invariance}
Each leg is traversed at double speed, which multiplies $\gamma'$ by $2$; and each leg occupies
half the parameter interval, which divides $dt$ by $2$. The two cancel exactly.

That cancellation is not special to this substitution. It is
\newterm{reparametrisation invariance}: for any $C^1$ increasing bijection
$\varphi : [a,b] \to [0,1]$, the substitution $s = \varphi(t)$ gives
$\int_{\gamma\circ\varphi} F\cdot d(\gamma\circ\varphi) = \int_\gamma F \cdot d\gamma$, by the same
one-line chain rule and change of variable. The line integral depends on the curve and the
direction of travel, not on the schedule --- which is what licenses writing $\int_\gamma F$ for a
\emph{curve} at all, as the rest of this chapter does. Reverse the direction and the sign flips,
since $\varphi$ is then decreasing.
\end{remark}
\end{exercisesolution}

\begin{exercisesolution}[Solution of \cref{ex:stokes_work_along_paths}]
\label{sol:stokes_work_along_paths}
\begin{enumerate}[label=\textbf{(\alph*)}]
  \item The domain is $\{(x,y,z) \in \mathbb{R}^3 : (x,y) \neq (0,0)\}$, i.e.\
    $\mathbb{R}^3$ with the $z$-axis removed. It is connected (path-connected: any two points can
    be joined by a path avoiding the axis), but not simply connected: $\gamma_1$, for instance,
    which winds once around the axis, cannot be contracted to a point without crossing it. (It is
    also not convex: the segment from $(-1,0,0)$ to $(1,0,0)$ leaves the domain.)

  \item Writing $F = (F^1,F^2,F^3)$ with $F^1 = 2(xz+y)/(x^2+y^2)$, $F^2 =
    2(yz-x)/(x^2+y^2)$, $F^3 = \log(x^2+y^2)$, a direct computation of each pair of mixed
    partials gives
    \begin{align*}
      \partial_y F^3 = \partial_z F^2 &= \frac{2y}{x^2+y^2}, &
      \partial_z F^1 = \partial_x F^3 &= \frac{2x}{x^2+y^2}, \\
      \partial_x F^2 = \partial_y F^1 &= \frac{2(x^2-y^2-2xyz)}{(x^2+y^2)^2},
    \end{align*}
    so $\curl F = (\partial_yF^3-\partial_zF^2,\ \partial_zF^1-\partial_xF^3,\ \partial_xF^2-
    \partial_yF^1) = (0,0,0)$: $F$ is curl-free. On $\gamma_1$, $x=\cos t$, $y=\sin t$, $z=0$,
    $x^2+y^2=1$, so $F(\gamma_1(t)) = (2\sin t,\,-2\cos t,\,0)$ and $\gamma_1'(t) = (-\sin t,
    \cos t,0)$, giving
    \[\int_{\gamma_1} F\cdot d\gamma_1 = \int_0^{2\pi} \big(2\sin t\cdot(-\sin t) + (-2\cos t)
      \cdot\cos t\big)\,dt = \int_0^{2\pi} -2\,dt = -4\pi.\]
    Since this is nonzero on a closed loop, $F$ is \textbf{not} conservative. It is curl-free, but
    its domain is not simply connected (part \textbf{(a)}), so curl-free does not force
    conservative here.

  \item Let $S := \{(x,y,z) : \sqrt{x^2+y^2} = 1+z/2,\ 0\leq z\leq2\}$, the cone swept out
    by linearly interpolating the radius from $\gamma_1$ ($z=0$, radius $1$) to $\gamma_2$ ($z=2$,
    radius $2$), parametrized by $H(s,t) := \big((1+s)\cos t,(1+s)\sin t,2s\big)$ for $(s,t) \in
    [0,1]\times[0,2\pi]$. Since the radius $1+s \geq 1 > 0$ throughout, $S$ never meets the
    $z$-axis and lies entirely in the domain of $F$; its oriented boundary (outward normal) is
    $\gamma_2$ minus $\gamma_1$. Since $\curl F = 0$ on $S$ by part \textbf{(b)},
    \cref{thm:stokes_classical} gives
    \[\int_{\gamma_2} F\cdot d\gamma_2 - \int_{\gamma_1} F\cdot d\gamma_1 = \int_{\partial S}
      F\cdot ds = \int_S \curl F\cdot d\vec n = 0,\]
    i.e.\ $\int_{\gamma_1} F\cdot d\gamma_1 = \int_{\gamma_2} F\cdot d\gamma_2$.

  \item No. The disk $D := \{(x,y,z) : (x-6)^2+y^2 \leq 9,\ z=3\}$, oriented upward, has
    oriented boundary exactly $\gamma_3$, and $D$ lies entirely in the domain of $F$ (it does not
    meet the $z$-axis, since its center $(6,0,3)$ is at distance $6$ from the axis and its radius
    is only $3$). By \cref{thm:stokes_classical} with $\curl F=0$,
    \[\int_{\gamma_3} F\cdot d\gamma_3 = \int_{\partial D} F\cdot ds = \int_D \curl F\cdot d\vec n
      = 0.\]
    Since $\int_{\gamma_1} F\cdot d\gamma_1 = -4\pi \neq 0 = \int_{\gamma_3} F\cdot d\gamma_3$,
    the two do \emph{not} agree, and Stokes' theorem gives no reason to expect they would: unlike
    the pair $(\gamma_1,\gamma_2)$, there is no surface joining $\gamma_1$ and $\gamma_3$ that
    stays inside the domain, since $\gamma_1$ winds around the excluded axis and $\gamma_3$ does
    not.
\end{enumerate}
\end{exercisesolution}

\label{sol:flux_on_punctured_domain}
\textbf{(a) False.} The Newton potential's gradient $F(x) = \nabla(1/\lvert x\rvert) =
-x\lvert x\rvert^{-3}$ is divergence-free on $U$ (it is $V_{-3}$ up to sign, from

% factor $1/r^2$: on $\partial B_r$ the integrand is $\langle F,n\rangle = -|x|^2/r^4 = -1/r^2$,
% not $-1$. The printed value $-4\pi r^2$ also contradicts part (b) directly, which proves $I_r$

% "false" was unaffected, since both values are non-zero.
\cref{ex:radial_vector_fields_flux}, with $n=3$), yet on $\partial B_r$ the normal is $n = x/r$
and $\langle F,n\rangle = -\lvert x\rvert^2/r^4 = -1/r^2$, so
\[I_r = \int_{\partial B_r} F\cdot n\,dS = -\frac{1}{r^2}\vol_2(\partial B_r) = -4\pi \neq 0,\]
a constant, exactly as part \textbf{(b)} predicts.

\textbf{(b) True.} Applying \cref{thm:gauss_divergence} on the shell $B_{r_2}\setminus
B_{r_1} \Subset U$ for $0<r_1<r_2$, whose boundary is $\partial B_{r_2}$ (outward) together
with $\partial B_{r_1}$ with reversed orientation,
\[0 = \int_{B_{r_2}\setminus B_{r_1}} \divg F\,dx = I_{r_2} - I_{r_1},\]
so $I_r$ is independent of $r$.

\textbf{(c) False.} Take $F(x) = x = \tfrac12\nabla\lvert x\rvert^2$, which is curl-free
(being a gradient). Then $\divg F \equiv 3$, so by \cref{thm:gauss_divergence}, $I_r =
\int_{B_r} 3\,dx = 3\vol_3(B_r) = 4\pi r^3$, which depends on $r$.

\textbf{(d) True.} If $F = \curl G$ then $\divg F = \divg(\curl G) = 0$ (a standard vector
identity), so part (b) applies and $I_r$ is independent of $r$. On the other hand,
\[\lvert I_r\rvert \leq \int_{\partial B_r} \lvert\langle F,n\rangle\rvert\,dS \leq
  \frac{C}{r}\,\vol_2(\partial B_r) = \frac{C}{r}\cdot4\pi r^2 = 4\pi C r \longrightarrow 0
  \quad\text{as } r\to0^+.\]
Since $I_r$ is constant in $r$ and this constant is a limit of values tending to $0$, $I_r=0$
for all $r>0$.
\end{exercisesolution}

\begin{exercisesolution}[Solution of \cref{ex:conservative_vector_field_potential}]
\label{sol:conservative_vector_field_potential}
Writing $F = (F^1,F^2)$ with $F^1 = \lambda xe^y$, $F^2 = (y+1+x^2)e^y$, the integrability
condition is $\partial_yF^1 = \partial_xF^2$:
\[\partial_yF^1 = \lambda xe^y, \qquad \partial_xF^2 = 2xe^y.\]
Since $\mathbb{R}^2$ is convex (so integrability is equivalent to conservativity here), $F$
is conservative if and only if $\lambda=2$.

For $\lambda=2$, seek $f$ with $\partial_xf = F^1 = 2xe^y$: integrating in $x$,
$f(x,y) = x^2e^y + g(y)$ for some function $g$. Then $\partial_yf = x^2e^y+g'(y)$ must equal
$F^2 = (y+1+x^2)e^y$, so $g'(y) = (y+1)e^y$; integrating by parts, $\int(y+1)e^y\,dy =
ye^y - e^y + e^y = ye^y$ (up to an additive constant). Hence
\[f(x,y) = \boxed{\ (x^2+y)e^y\ } \qquad (\text{unique up to an additive constant}),\]
which one checks directly satisfies $\nabla f = F$.
\end{exercisesolution}

\begin{exercisesolution}[Proof of \cref{ex:curl_identity_greens_formula}]
\label{sol:curl_identity_greens_formula}
% Generator: Gemini 3.6 Flash (High)
% Correction: Claude Opus 5 (High) --- \operatorname{rot} -> \curl, J\Phi -> \Jac\Phi, and the line
% integrals rewritten in this chapter's own notation (\int_{\partial\Omega} F\cdot\tau\,dL, as in
% thm:green_theorem) rather than the physics d\mathbf{r}. Part (b) also justified properly: Flash
% asserted "d\Phi_2 = d\Psi_2 on \partial\Omega", which is false for the full differentials --- only
% the tangential derivative agrees, and that is all the line integral sees.
\begin{enumerate}[label=\textbf{(\alph*)}]
  \item Write $V = (V_1, V_2)\transp = (\Phi_1 \partial_1 \Phi_2, \Phi_1 \partial_2 \Phi_2)\transp$.
The scalar curl in the plane is $\curl V = \partial_1 V_2 - \partial_2 V_1$, so the product rule
gives
\begin{align*}
\curl V &= \partial_1 (\Phi_1 \partial_2 \Phi_2) - \partial_2 (\Phi_1 \partial_1 \Phi_2) \\
&= (\partial_1 \Phi_1 \partial_2 \Phi_2 + \Phi_1 \partial_1 \partial_2 \Phi_2) - (\partial_2 \Phi_1 \partial_1 \Phi_2 + \Phi_1 \partial_2 \partial_1 \Phi_2).
\end{align*}
Since $\Phi \in C^2(\overline{\Omega}, \mathbb{R}^2)$, Schwarz's lemma
(\cref{thm:schwarz_mixed_partials}) gives $\partial_1 \partial_2 \Phi_2 = \partial_2 \partial_1
\Phi_2$, and the two second-derivative terms cancel. Consequently,
\[\curl V = \partial_1 \Phi_1 \partial_2 \Phi_2 - \partial_2 \Phi_1 \partial_1 \Phi_2 = \det \begin{pmatrix} \partial_1 \Phi_1 & \partial_2 \Phi_1 \\ \partial_1 \Phi_2 & \partial_2 \Phi_2 \end{pmatrix} = \det \Jac\Phi.\]
Note that the hypothesis $\Phi \in C^2$ is doing real work here: without equality of mixed partials
the identity is simply false.

  \item Write $V_\Phi := \Phi_1 \nabla \Phi_2$ and $V_\Psi := \Psi_1 \nabla \Psi_2$. By part
\textbf{(a)}, $\curl V_\Phi = \det \Jac\Phi$ and $\curl V_\Psi = \det \Jac\Psi$, so Green's theorem
(\cref{thm:green_theorem}) turns both integrals into boundary integrals:
\[\int_\Omega \det \Jac\Phi \, dx = \int_{\partial \Omega} V_\Phi \cdot \tau \, dL,
\qquad
\int_\Omega \det \Jac\Psi \, dx = \int_{\partial \Omega} V_\Psi \cdot \tau \, dL.\]
It remains to see that the two boundary integrands agree. Fix a point of $\partial\Omega$ and let
$\tau$ be the unit tangent there. Since $\Phi = \Psi$ on all of $\partial\Omega$, the two maps agree
along the boundary curve, and therefore so do their derivatives \emph{in the direction $\tau$}:
\[\nabla \Phi_2 \cdot \tau = \partial_\tau \Phi_2 = \partial_\tau \Psi_2 = \nabla \Psi_2 \cdot \tau .\]
(The full gradients need not agree, since nothing constrains the normal derivative; but the line
integral only ever sees the tangential part.) Together with $\Phi_1 = \Psi_1$ on $\partial\Omega$
this gives $V_\Phi \cdot \tau = V_\Psi \cdot \tau$ pointwise on $\partial\Omega$, and hence
\[\int_\Omega \det \Jac\Phi \, dx = \int_\Omega \det \Jac\Psi \, dx.\]
\end{enumerate}
\begin{remark}
The statement is a genuinely striking one: $\int_\Omega \det\Jac\Phi$ is an interior quantity, the
signed area swept out by $\Phi$, yet it depends on nothing but the boundary values of $\Phi$. That
is the same phenomenon as a conservative field having path-independent work, one dimension up.
\end{remark}

% Generator: Claude Opus 5 (High) --- parts (c) to (f), added 2026-08-11 together with the
% corresponding parts of the statement. Parts (a) and (b) above are Gemini 3.6 Flash's, corrected.
From here on $\Omega = B_1$ and $\Phi(x) = x$ on $\partial B_1$. The four remaining parts form a
chain, each one feeding the next, and the single computation they all run on is the one carried
out in part \textbf{(d)}: a map taking values in the unit circle has vanishing Jacobian
determinant.
\begin{enumerate}[label=\textbf{(\alph*)}]
  \setcounter{enumi}{2}
  \item The identity map $\Psi := \id$ is $C^2$ on $\overline{B_1}$ and agrees with $\Phi$ on
    $\partial B_1$, which is exactly the hypothesis of \textbf{(b)}. Since
    $\Jac(\id) = I_2$ has determinant $1$,
    \[\int_{B_1}\det\Jac\Phi = \int_{B_1}\det\Jac(\id) = \int_{B_1} 1 = \vol_2(B_1) = \pi .\]
    The integral of $\lvert\det\Jac\Phi\rvert$ dominates the absolute value of the integral of
    $\det\Jac\Phi$, so
    \[\int_{B_1}\lvert\det\Jac\Phi\rvert \;\geq\; \left\lvert\int_{B_1}\det\Jac\Phi\right\rvert
      = \pi .\]
    Note what has been proved: $\Phi$ may fold the disk over itself arbitrarily, but it can never
    do so in a way that makes the total unsigned area it sweeps out drop below $\pi$.

  \item Suppose, for contradiction, that $\Phi(\overline{B_1}) \subseteq \partial B_1$, so that
    $\lvert\Phi(x)\rvert^2 = 1$ for every $x \in \overline{B_1}$. Differentiating this identity
    with respect to $x_j$ gives $2\sum_{i=1}^2 \Phi_i\,\partial_j\Phi_i = 0$ for $j = 1,2$, which
    in matrix form reads
    \[\Phi(x)\transp\, \Jac\Phi(x) = 0 \qquad\text{for all } x \in \overline{B_1}.\]
    The row vector $\Phi(x)\transp$ is non-zero, since $\lvert\Phi(x)\rvert = 1$, so
    $\Jac\Phi(x)$ has a non-trivial left kernel and is therefore singular. Hence
    $\det\Jac\Phi \equiv 0$ on $\overline{B_1}$ and $\int_{B_1}\lvert\det\Jac\Phi\rvert = 0$,
    contradicting \textbf{(c)}.

  \item Suppose $0 \notin \Phi(\overline{B_1})$. Then $\lvert\Phi\rvert$ is a continuous, strictly
    positive function on the compact set $\overline{B_1}$, hence bounded below by some $c > 0$, and
    \[\widetilde\Phi := \frac{\Phi}{\lvert\Phi\rvert}\]
    is $C^2$ on $\overline{B_1}$: it is a composition of $\Phi \in C^2$ with the map
    $y \mapsto y/\lvert y\rvert$, which is smooth on $\{\lvert y\rvert \geq c\}$. By construction
    $\lvert\widetilde\Phi\rvert \equiv 1$, so $\widetilde\Phi(\overline{B_1}) \subseteq \partial
    B_1$. Moreover $\widetilde\Phi(x) = x/\lvert x\rvert = x$ for $x \in \partial B_1$, since
    $\Phi(x) = x$ and $\lvert x\rvert = 1$ there. So $\widetilde\Phi$ satisfies every hypothesis
    that \textbf{(d)} places on $\Phi$, and its image \emph{is} contained in $\partial B_1$. That
    is exactly what \textbf{(d)} forbids, so no such $\Phi$ exists and $0 \in \Phi(\overline{B_1})$.

  \item Let $p \in \overline{B_1}$. If $\lvert p\rvert = 1$ then $\Phi(p) = p$ and there is nothing
    to prove, so assume $\lvert p\rvert < 1$ and suppose $p \notin \Phi(\overline{B_1})$.

    Exactly as in \textbf{(e)}, $\lvert\Phi - p\rvert$ is bounded below by some $c > 0$ on the
    compact $\overline{B_1}$, so
    \[G := \frac{\Phi - p}{\lvert\Phi - p\rvert} \ \in\ C^2(\overline{B_1},\mathbb{R}^2),
      \qquad \lvert G\rvert \equiv 1 .\]
    The computation in \textbf{(d)} applies verbatim to $G$ and gives $\det\Jac G \equiv 0$.
    Feeding that into \textbf{(a)} and Green's theorem (\cref{thm:green_theorem}),
    \begin{equation}
    \label{eq:bonus_boundary_integral}
      0 = \int_{B_1}\det\Jac G = \int_{\partial B_1} G_1\nabla G_2 \cdot \tau\,dL .
    \end{equation}
    Now evaluate that boundary integral. On $\partial B_1$ we have $\Phi(x) = x$, so
    $G(x) = (x-p)/\lvert x-p\rvert$. Parametrise $\partial B_1$ by $x(t) := (\cos t, \sin t)$ and
    write $c(t) := x(t) - p$, a circle of radius $1$ centred at $-p$. Since $G(x(t)) = c(t)/\lvert
    c(t)\rvert$ lies on the unit circle, it admits a $C^1$ angle $\varphi$ with
    \[G(x(t)) = \big(\cos\varphi(t),\ \sin\varphi(t)\big).\]
    Then $\tfrac{d}{dt}G_2(x(t)) = \varphi'(t)\cos\varphi(t)$, so the integrand of
    \eqref{eq:bonus_boundary_integral} is $\cos^2\varphi(t)\,\varphi'(t)$ and
    \[\int_{\partial B_1} G_1\nabla G_2\cdot\tau\,dL
      = \int_0^{2\pi}\cos^2\varphi\,\varphi'\,dt
      = \int_{\varphi(0)}^{\varphi(2\pi)}\cos^2\varphi\,d\varphi
      = \left[\frac{\varphi}{2} + \frac{\sin 2\varphi}{4}\right]_{\varphi(0)}^{\varphi(2\pi)} .\]
    The loop closes up, so $\varphi(2\pi) - \varphi(0) = 2\pi N$ for an integer $N$, and
    $\sin 2\varphi$ is $2\pi$-periodic so its two contributions cancel. The boundary integral is
    therefore $\pi N$, and \eqref{eq:bonus_boundary_integral} forces $N = 0$.

    But $N = 1$, and the angle field of \cref{ex:angle_field_true_false} is what shows it. Writing
    $c(t) = \rho(t)\big(\cos\varphi(t), \sin\varphi(t)\big)$ with $\rho := \lvert c\rvert > 0$, and
    taking $Y := (-x_2,\,x_1)/(x_1^2+x_2^2)$, we have $Y(c) = \rho^{-1}(-\sin\varphi,\cos\varphi)$
    and
    \[\langle Y(c),\dot c\rangle
      = \frac{1}{\rho}\Big\langle(-\sin\varphi,\cos\varphi),\
        \dot\rho\,(\cos\varphi,\sin\varphi) + \rho\dot\varphi\,(-\sin\varphi,\cos\varphi)\Big\rangle
      = \dot\varphi,\]
    the $\dot\rho$ term dropping out because the two vectors are orthogonal. Integrating,
    $\int_c Y\cdot dc = \varphi(2\pi)-\varphi(0) = 2\pi N$: the angle field measures exactly the
    total turning. Now $c$ is the unit circle centred at $-p$, and $\lvert -p\rvert < 1$ puts the
    origin strictly inside it, so $\int_c Y\cdot dc = 2\pi$.

  \node[font=\scriptsize, above right] at (0.58,0.36) {$p$};
  \fill (0,0) circle (1.4pt);
  \node[font=\scriptsize, below right] at (0.04,-0.04) {$0$};

  \node[font=\scriptsize, align=center] at (0,-2.35)
    {$\lvert p\rvert < 1$, so the origin lies inside $c$:\\the loop winds once about it, and $N=1$};
\end{tikzpicture}
\end{center}

    To see the value $2\pi$, apply Green's
    theorem to the region between $c$ and a small circle about the origin: $Y$ is closed there, so
    the region contributes nothing and the two circles carry the same integral, and on a circle
    centred at the origin the integrand is identically $1$, as computed in the solution of
    \cref{ex:angle_field_true_false}. Hence $N = 1$, contradicting $N = 0$, and
    $p \in \Phi(\overline{B_1})$ after all.
\end{enumerate}
\begin{remark}[What the chain actually proves]
Read backwards, the four parts are one theorem: a $C^2$ map of the closed disk that fixes the
circle pointwise must hit every point of the disk. It cannot retract the disk onto its own
boundary, which is \textbf{(d)}, and that single fact is what \textbf{(e)} and \textbf{(f)} both
exploit, each time by dividing by a norm to manufacture a map whose image \emph{is} in the circle.
This is Brouwer's fixed point theorem in disguise, in the plane and in its smooth form, and the
whole apparatus is two applications of Green's formula.

The integer $N$ is where the topology finally enters. Everything before it is calculus, and
calculus alone cannot produce a contradiction, since $0$ and $\pi N$ are both perfectly good
numbers. What breaks the argument open is that $N$ is forced to be a \emph{whole number} by the
loop closing up, and forced to be $1$ by where $p$ sits. An integer that must equal both $0$ and
$1$ is the only genuinely non-analytic step in the proof.
\end{remark}
\end{exercisesolution}

\begin{exercisesolution}[Solution of \cref{ex:angle_field_true_false}]
\label{sol:angle_field_true_false}
% Generator: Claude Opus 5 (High)
Begin with the computation every part relies on. Writing $X = (P,Q)$ with $P = -y/(x^2+y^2)$ and
$Q = x/(x^2+y^2)$,
\[\partial_x Q = \frac{(x^2+y^2) - 2x^2}{(x^2+y^2)^2} = \frac{y^2-x^2}{(x^2+y^2)^2},
  \qquad
  \partial_y P = \frac{-(x^2+y^2) + 2y^2}{(x^2+y^2)^2} = \frac{y^2-x^2}{(x^2+y^2)^2},\]
so $\curl X = \partial_x Q - \partial_y P = 0$ throughout $U$. The field is closed. Whether it is
also a gradient is precisely what the domain decides.
\begin{enumerate}[label=\textbf{(\alph*)}]
  \item \textbf{False.} Suppose such a $u$ existed. Then the work of $X$ around any closed loop in
    $U$ would vanish. But along the unit circle $\sigma(t) := (\cos t, \sin t)$, $t \in [0,2\pi]$,
    we have $X(\sigma(t)) = (-\sin t, \cos t) = \dot\sigma(t)$, so the integrand is
    $\lvert\dot\sigma\rvert^2 = 1$ and
\[\int_\sigma X \cdot d\sigma = \int_0^{2\pi} 1 \, dt = 2\pi \neq 0 .\]
    So no potential exists on $U$. Being closed is not enough; $U$ is not simply connected, and this
    field is the standard witness that the Poincaré lemma (\cref{thm:poincare_lemma}) genuinely needs
    that hypothesis.
  \item \textbf{True.} The half-plane $V$ is convex, hence simply connected, so a closed field on it
    is exact (\cref{cor:potentials_simply_connected}). Explicitly, $v(x,y) := \arctan(y/x)$ is $C^1$
    on $V$ --- note $x > 0$ there, so the quotient is defined and smooth --- and
\[\nabla v = \frac{1}{1 + y^2/x^2}\left(-\frac{y}{x^2},\; \frac{1}{x}\right)
   = \frac{1}{x^2+y^2}\,(-y,\, x) = X .\]
    The potential is the polar angle, which is single-valued on the half-plane but not on $U$: that
    is \textbf{(a)} and \textbf{(b)} in one sentence.
  \item \textbf{False.} Injectivity on $[0,1)$ makes $\gamma$ a simple closed curve, but it says
    nothing about \emph{where} that curve runs. Two things can go wrong. First, the loop need not
    enclose the origin at all: the circle of radius $\tfrac12$ about $(0,1)$ is a simple closed curve
    in $U$ through $(0,1)$, and since it bounds a disc on which $X$ is defined and closed, Green's
    theorem (\cref{thm:green_theorem}) gives $\int_\gamma X \cdot d\gamma = 0$. Second, even a loop
    that does enclose the origin may be traversed clockwise, giving $-2\pi$. The correct general
    statement is that the integral equals $2\pi$ times the winding number of $\gamma$ about the
    origin, and \qt{simple closed curve} pins that number down only up to sign, and only once the
    origin is known to be inside.
\end{enumerate}
\begin{remark}
This one field carries most of the chapter's content. It is closed but not exact, so it separates the
two notions; the failure is measured by an integer, the winding number; and the obstruction is a
property of the \emph{domain}, not of the field --- restrict it to any simply connected piece of $U$,
as in \textbf{(b)}, and a potential appears at once.
\end{remark}
\end{exercisesolution}

\begin{exercisesolution}[Solution of \cref{ex:two_fields_two_circles}]

% Generator: Claude Opus 5 (High)

does not depend on $x_2$, so both mixed partials vanish, and the computation for $Y$ is the one
carried out for \cref{ex:angle_field_true_false}, where $Y$ appears under the name $X$. Being
closed is where the two fields stop agreeing, and the six items are really two questions asked
three times over.
\begin{enumerate}[label=\textbf{(\alph*)}]
  \item \textbf{True.} Take $u(x_1,x_2) := -\cos(x_1)$, which is smooth on all of $\mathbb{R}^2$
    and has $\nabla u = (\sin x_1,\, 0) = X$. Restricting it to $U$ gives the required potential.
    The puncture is irrelevant here, since $X$ extends smoothly across the origin.
  \item \textbf{False.} This is $Y$'s defining feature. If such a $v$ existed, the work of $Y$
    around every closed loop in $U$ would vanish, and item \textbf{(d)} exhibits one where it does
    not.
  \item \textbf{True.} $X$ is a gradient on $U$ by \textbf{(a)}, so by
    \cref{prop:work_difference_potential} its work along a path depends only on the endpoints:
    $\int_c X\cdot dc = u(c(b)) - u(c(a))$ for any $C^1$ path $c : [a,b] \to U$. Both $\gamma$ and
    $\sigma$ are closed, so both differences vanish.
  \item \textbf{False.} On the unit circle $Y(\sigma(t)) = (-\sin t,\ \cos t) = \dot\sigma(t)$,

  \item \textbf{True.} The curve $\gamma$ is the unit circle centred at $(10,0)$, so its first
    coordinate satisfies $x_1 = 10+\cos t \geq 9 > 0$. It therefore lies in the open right
    half-plane $V := \{x_1 > 0\}$, which is convex and hence simply connected. On $V$ the closed
    field $Y$ is exact (\cref{cor:potentials_simply_connected}, with potential the polar angle
    $\arctan(x_2/x_1)$), so \textbf{(c)}'s argument applies verbatim and the integral vanishes.
  \item \textbf{True.} Same reason as \textbf{(c)}: $X$ is exact on $U$ and $\sigma$ is closed. It

\begin{exercisesolution}[\cref{ex:jossen_potential_on_disconnected_domain}]
\label{sol:jossen_potential_on_disconnected_domain}
\begin{enumerate}[label=\textbf{(\alph*)}]
  \item $U$ is \textbf{open}: it is the preimage of the open set $\mathbb{R}\setminus\{0\}$ under
    the continuous projection $(x,y,z)\mapsto y$.

    $U$ is \textbf{not connected}. The two sets $U^+ := \{y > 0\}$ and $U^- := \{y < 0\}$ are open,
    non-empty and disjoint, and their union is $U$, which is exactly a disconnection
    (\cref{def:connectedness}).

    $U$ is therefore \textbf{not simply connected} either, since simple connectedness includes
    path-connectedness. It is worth recording what \emph{is} true: each of $U^+$ and $U^-$ is an
    open half-space, hence convex, hence simply connected.

  \item Yes. Writing $E := e^{x+\frac1y}$ for brevity, so that $\partial_1 E = E$ and
    $\partial_2 E = -E/y^2$, we compute
    \begin{align*}
      \partial_2 F_1 &= \partial_y\big((1+x)E\big) = -\frac{(1+x)}{y^2}\,E, \\
      \partial_1 F_2 &= \partial_x\left(-\frac{x}{y^2}E\right)
        = -\frac{1}{y^2}E - \frac{x}{y^2}E = -\frac{(1+x)}{y^2}\,E,
    \end{align*}
    which agree. The remaining pairs are trivial: $F_1$ and $F_2$ do not depend on $z$ and $F_3$
    depends on nothing else, so $\partial_3F_1 = 0 = \partial_1F_3$ and
    $\partial_3F_2 = 0 = \partial_2F_3$. Hence $F$ satisfies the integrability conditions
    (\cref{lem:integrability_conditions}) on all of $U$.

  \item \textbf{Yes}, $F$ is conservative, and the cleanest justification is to produce a potential
    outright, which is \textbf{(d)}. The structural reason is that $U$ has two connected
    components, each of which is convex and therefore simply connected, so
    \cref{cor:potentials_simply_connected} applies \emph{on each component separately} and supplies
    a potential there. Being conservative is a condition checked component by component, since a
    path in $U$ never leaves the component it starts in. Consequently, the two local potentials
    together make $F$ conservative on $U$.

  \item Yes. Take
    \[u(x,y,z) := x\,e^{x+\frac1y} + g(z), \qquad (x,y,z) \in U,\]
    which is well defined because $y \neq 0$ on $U$, and $C^2$ because $g$ is. Then
    \[\partial_x u = e^{x+\frac1y} + x\,e^{x+\frac1y} = (1+x)e^{x+\frac1y} = F_1, \qquad
      \partial_y u = -\frac{x}{y^2}e^{x+\frac1y} = F_2, \qquad
      \partial_z u = g'(z) = F_3,\]
    so $\nabla u = F$ on $U$.

  \item First, $\gamma$ does take values in $U$: its second component is
    $2 + t\sin(2\pi t)$, and $\lvert t\sin(2\pi t)\rvert \le t \le 1$ for $t \in [0,1]$, so that
    component is at least $1 > 0$. The path stays in the component $U^+$ throughout.

    Its endpoints are
    \[\gamma(0) = (0,\,2,\,1), \qquad \gamma(1) = \big(\cos(2\pi),\ 2+\sin(2\pi),\ 1-1+1\big)
      = (1,\,2,\,1).\]
    By \cref{prop:work_difference_potential} the work integral of a conservative field depends only
    on the endpoints, so with the potential from \textbf{(d)},
    \[\int_\gamma F\cdot d\gamma = u(1,2,1) - u(0,2,1)
      = \Big(1\cdot e^{1+\frac12} + g(1)\Big) - \Big(0\cdot e^{0+\frac12} + g(1)\Big)
      = e^{3/2}.\]
\end{enumerate}
\begin{remark}[Not simply connected does not mean not conservative]
Read this exercise against \cref{ex:vortex_field_not_conservative} and the pair says something the
corollary alone does not. There, $U = \mathbb{R}^2\setminus\{0\}$ fails to be simply connected and
the field really is not conservative. Here, $U = \{y\neq0\}$ also fails to be simply connected, and
the field is conservative anyway. \Cref{cor:potentials_simply_connected} is a
\emph{sufficient} condition, so when its hypothesis fails the correct conclusion is that the
theorem is silent, never that the field has no potential. Answering \textbf{(c)} with \qt{no,
because $U$ is not simply connected} is the mistake the problem is built to catch.

The two failures are also of quite different kinds. $\mathbb{R}^2\setminus\{0\}$ is connected but
has a hole, and it is the hole that a loop can wind around and accumulate work. $\{y\neq0\}$ has no
holes at all; it merely falls into two pieces, each as simple as a region can be. Disconnection is
the harmless failure, because no path can exploit it, and that is why the component-by-component
argument in \textbf{(c)} works. The unhelpful thing about the phrase \qt{simply connected} is that
it bundles the two together.

Finally, note that the answer to \textbf{(e)} does not involve $g$ at all. The path returns to the
height $z = 1$ it started at, so the $g(z)$ term of the potential cancels, and no property of $g$
beyond differentiability was ever needed.
\end{remark}
\end{exercisesolution}

\begin{exercisesolution}[Solution of \cref{ex:kobel_line_integral_yz_circle}]
\label{sol:kobel_line_integral_yz_circle}
% Generator: Claude Opus 5 (High)
The answer is \textbf{(c)}, the integral is $0$.

Parametrise the circle. It lies in the plane $x = 0$, has radius $1$ and centre the origin, and
must run counterclockwise as seen from the positive $x$-axis, which for the right-handed frame
$(y,z)$ means increasing angle from the $y$-axis towards the $z$-axis:
\[\gamma(t) = (0,\ \cos t,\ \sin t), \qquad t \in [0,2\pi], \qquad
  \gamma'(t) = (0,\ -\sin t,\ \cos t).\]
Now evaluate the field on the curve. Since $x = 0$ along $\gamma$, the middle component of $V$
vanishes identically there, and $V(\gamma(t)) = (1,\,0,\,-2)$ is \emph{constant}. Hence
\[V(\gamma(t))\cdot\gamma'(t) = 1\cdot 0 + 0\cdot(-\sin t) + (-2)\cos t = -2\cos t,\]
and by \cref{def:line_integral}
\[\int_\gamma V\cdot d\gamma = \int_0^{2\pi} -2\cos t\,dt = \big[-2\sin t\big]_0^{2\pi} = 0.\]
\begin{remark}[Two ways to see the zero without integrating]
Neither requires the parametrisation, and both are worth having.

\textbf{The field is constant on the curve.} A constant field is a gradient field, of the linear
function $\varphi(x,y,z) := x - 2z$, and by \cref{prop:work_difference_potential} the work of a
gradient field around a \emph{closed} path is zero. The only reason $V$ itself is not a gradient
field is its $x$ component in the second slot --- and that component is exactly what the plane
$x=0$ switches off.

\textbf{Stokes' theorem.} We have $\curl V = (0,0,1)\transp$, which is constant. Take the flat unit
disc $D$ in the plane $x=0$ bounded by $\gamma$; its unit normal is $\pm(1,0,0)\transp$, so
$\curl V \cdot \nu = 0$ pointwise on $D$ and \cref{thm:greens_theorem}'s three-dimensional form
gives $\int_\gamma V\cdot d\gamma = \int_D \curl V\cdot\nu = 0$. The curl is non-zero, and the
surface is oriented so as not to see it.

The trap the question sets is to compute $\curl V \ne 0$, conclude \qt{not conservative}, and then
guess a non-zero answer. Non-conservative fields have zero work around plenty of individual loops;
what fails is only that the work is zero around \emph{every} loop.
\end{remark}
\end{exercisesolution}

\begin{exercisesolution}[Solution of \cref{ex:kobel_irrotational_single_choice}]
\label{sol:kobel_irrotational_single_choice}
% Generator: Claude Opus 5 (High)
The correct answer is \textbf{(d)}, the guarded one.
\begin{enumerate}[label=\textbf{(\alph*)}]
  \item \textbf{False.} This is the standing counterexample of this chapter. On
    $G := \mathbb{R}^2\setminus\{0\}$ the angle field of
    \cref{ex:vortex_field_not_conservative} is irrotational, and its work around the unit circle
    is $2\pi \neq 0$. Vanishing curl gives path independence only \emph{locally}, and a closed
    curve can encircle a hole.
  \item \textbf{False}, and it is the option most likely to be chosen. The correct hypothesis is
    \newterm{simply connected}, not connected: \cref{cor:potentials_simply_connected} needs every
    loop to be contractible, and $\mathbb{R}^2\setminus\{0\}$ is connected --- indeed
    path-connected --- while having no potential for the field above. Connectedness only buys
    uniqueness of a potential up to an additive constant, once one exists.
  \item \textbf{False.} Curl and divergence are independent of one another; nothing links them. The
    field $V(x,y) := (x,y)\transp$ has $\curl V = 0$ and $\divg V = 2$.
  \item \textbf{True}, and it is true because it claims almost nothing: \emph{may} be. It is
    satisfied by any irrotational field on a simply connected $G$, for instance $G =
    \mathbb{R}^2$, where \cref{thm:poincare_lemma} produces a potential outright.
\end{enumerate}
\begin{remark}[Why the correct answer is the weakest one]
\label{rem:irrotational_weakest_true_statement}
The question is unusual in that the true option is the one asserting the least, and a reader who
reads \qt{it may be that} as a hedge rather than a claim will pass over it. The design is
deliberate: options \textbf{(a)} and \textbf{(b)} are the two standard over-readings of
$\curl V = 0$ --- that it forces closed line integrals to vanish, and that connectedness is the
missing topological hypothesis --- and \textbf{(c)} tests whether the two differential operators
have been kept apart. Under a hypothesis on $G$ this weak, nothing stronger than \textbf{(d)}
survives.
\end{remark}
\end{exercisesolution}
