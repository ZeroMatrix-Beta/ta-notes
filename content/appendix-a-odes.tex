\section{Turning an \texorpdfstring{$n$}{n}-th order ODE into an \texorpdfstring{$n \times n$}{n x n} system}
\label{sec:nth_order_to_system}

Given an $n$-th order linear ODE, we can rewrite it as a first-order problem in higher dimensions.
Consider the example equation:
\[y(t) + y'(t) + 2y''(t) = f(t).\]
We define $u_1 := y$, which gives:
\[u_1 + u_1' + 2u_1'' = f.\]
Letting $u_2 := u_1'$, we can rewrite the second derivative as $u_1'' = u_2'$. The system becomes:
\begin{align*}
    u_1 + u_2 + 2u_2' &= f \\
    u_2 &= u_1'.
\end{align*}
Solving for the derivatives $u_1'$ and $u_2'$, we get:
\begin{align*}
    u_1' &= u_2 \\
    u_2' &= -\frac{1}{2}u_1 - \frac{1}{2}u_2 + \frac{1}{2}f.
\end{align*}
In matrix notation, this is:
\[\begin{pmatrix} u_1' \\ u_2' \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} 0 & 1 \\ -1/2 & -1/2 \end{pmatrix} \begin{pmatrix} u_1 \\ u_2 \end{pmatrix} + \begin{pmatrix} 0 \\ f/2 \end{pmatrix},\]
or more compactly,
\[u'(t) = A u(t) + F(t).\]
This reduction to a first-order system can be done with any $n$-th order ODE. We therefore focus on solving systems of the form $u'(t) = A u(t) + F(t)$.

\section{Solving linear systems of ODEs}
\label{sec:solving_linear_systems_odes}

\subsection{Diagonalizable matrices}
We first look at the homogeneous case where $F \equiv 0$, so $u'(t) = A u(t)$.
Suppose the matrix $A$ is diagonalizable, meaning we can write $A = P D P^{-1}$ where $D$ is a diagonal matrix and $P$ is invertible:
\[D = \begin{pmatrix} \lambda_1 & & 0 \\ & \ddots & \\ 0 & & \lambda_n \end{pmatrix}.\]
Then we have:
\begin{align*}
    u'(t) &= A u(t) \\
    \iff u'(t) &= P D P^{-1} u(t) \\
    \iff P^{-1} u'(t) &= D P^{-1} u(t) \\
    \iff (P^{-1} u(t))' &= D (P^{-1} u(t)).
\end{align*}
By defining a new variable $v(t) := P^{-1} u(t)$, the system uncouples:
\[v'(t) = D v(t) \iff \begin{pmatrix} v_1'(t) \\ \vdots \\ v_n'(t) \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} \lambda_1 & & 0 \\ & \ddots & \\ 0 & & \lambda_n \end{pmatrix} \begin{pmatrix} v_1(t) \\ \vdots \\ v_n(t) \end{pmatrix}.\]
This yields $n$ independent $1$-dimensional equations $v_i'(t) = \lambda_i v_i(t)$, which have the solutions:
\[v_i(t) = C_i e^{\lambda_i t} \quad \text{for } i \in \{1,\dots,n\}.\]
The solution to the original system is then recovered by $u(t) = P v(t)$. By diagonalizing, we were able to uncouple the $n \times n$ system and solve the scalar equations without a problem.

\subsection{Non-diagonalizable matrices}
What if $A$ is not diagonalizable? Then we have to use the Jordan Normal Form: $A = P J P^{-1}$.
Following the same substitution $v(t) = P^{-1}u(t)$, we obtain $v'(t) = J v(t)$.
We now focus on each Jordan block in $J$. For a block with eigenvalue $\lambda_i$, the system looks like:
\[\begin{pmatrix} v_j'(t) \\ \vdots \\ v_{j+d}'(t) \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} \lambda_i & 1 & & \\ & \lambda_i & \ddots & \\ & & \ddots & 1 \\ & & & \lambda_i \end{pmatrix} \begin{pmatrix} v_j(t) \\ \vdots \\ v_{j+d}(t) \end{pmatrix}.\]
This translates to the equations:
\begin{align*}
    v_j'(t) &= \lambda_i v_j(t) + v_{j+1}(t) \\
    &\vdots \\
    v_{j+d}'(t) &= \lambda_i v_{j+d}(t).
\end{align*}
We first solve for $v_{j+d}(t)$ from the last equation. Then we plug it into the preceding equation to find $v_{j+d-1}(t)$, and continue this process upwards.

Since this back-substitution can be time-consuming, it is useful to use matrix exponentials. For a system $u'(t) = A u(t)$, the formal solution is:
\[u(t) = e^{tA} u_0,\]
where $u_0$ is given by the initial conditions. Evaluating the matrix exponential $e^{tA}$ provides a direct way to solve the system.

% Source: Jérôme Paschoud/Notizen/Woche 14.2 DGL und Picard Lindelof.pdf, p. 2
% Generator: Gemini 3.1 Pro (High)
\begin{remark}[Calculating the matrix exponential in practice]
When computing $\exp(A) = I + \sum_{m=1}^\infty \frac{A^m}{m!}$, the most common strategies are:
\begin{enumerate}[label=\textbf{(\alph*)}]
  \item \textbf{Look for a pattern:} Sometimes $A^2 = A$, or $A^3 = 2A$, or $A$ is nilpotent ($A^n = 0$ for $n \geq k$). In these cases, the infinite series can be simplified directly.
  \item \textbf{Diagonalization:} If $A$ is diagonalizable ($A = B D B^{-1}$), then $\exp(A) = B \exp(D) B^{-1}$, where $\exp(D)$ is the diagonal matrix of exponentials.
  \item \textbf{Jordan Normal Form (JNF):} If $A$ is not diagonalizable, one can use the JNF, though this is often more tedious and less frequently required.
\end{enumerate}
\end{remark}

\begin{example}[Solving a $2 \times 2$ system using matrix exponentials]
\label{ex:ode_matrix_exponential}
Consider the system given by
\begin{align*}
    u_1(t) + 2u_2(t) &= u_1'(t) \\
    2u_1(t) + u_2(t) &= u_2'(t).
\end{align*}
This can be written as $u'(t) = A u(t)$ with $A = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 2 \\ 2 & 1 \end{pmatrix}$.
The eigenvalues of $A$ are found via $\det(A - \lambda I) = (1-\lambda)^2 - 4 = 0$, giving $\lambda_1 = 3$ and $\lambda_2 = -1$.
The corresponding eigenvectors are $w_1 = (1, 1)\transp$ and $w_2 = (1, -1)\transp$.
Since $A$ is diagonalizable, we have $A = P D P^{-1}$ with $P = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 1 \\ 1 & -1 \end{pmatrix}$ and $D = \begin{pmatrix} 3 & 0 \\ 0 & -1 \end{pmatrix}$.
The general solution is then given by:
\[u(t) = \sum_{i=1}^2 C_i e^{\lambda_i t} w_i = C_1 e^{3t} \begin{pmatrix} 1 \\ 1 \end{pmatrix} + C_2 e^{-t} \begin{pmatrix} 1 \\ -1 \end{pmatrix}.\]

Now consider a non-diagonalizable example where the system is already in Jordan normal form, $u'(t) = J u(t)$ with $J = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 1 \\ 0 & 1 \end{pmatrix}$.
Using the matrix exponential, the solution is $u(t) = e^{tJ} u(0)$.
We can split $J = D + N$ where $D = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ 0 & 1 \end{pmatrix}$ is diagonal and $N = \begin{pmatrix} 0 & 1 \\ 0 & 0 \end{pmatrix}$ is nilpotent ($N^2 = 0$).
Since $D$ and $N$ commute, $e^{tJ} = e^{tD} e^{tN}$.
We calculate:
\[e^{tD} = \begin{pmatrix} e^t & 0 \\ 0 & e^t \end{pmatrix}, \qquad e^{tN} = I + tN = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & t \\ 0 & 1 \end{pmatrix}.\]
Multiplying these gives:
\[e^{tJ} = \begin{pmatrix} e^t & t e^t \\ 0 & e^t \end{pmatrix}.\]
Thus, the solution is $u_1(t) = e^t u_1(0) + t e^t u_2(0)$ and $u_2(t) = e^t u_2(0)$.
\end{example}

\begin{example}[Solving an initial value problem]
\label{ex:ode_ivp_system}
% Michael's Bsp. 23.1.4
Consider the initial value problem:
\begin{align*}
    y_1' &= 3y_1 - 2y_2, \\
    y_2' &= -y_1 + 2y_2,
\end{align*}
with initial conditions $y_1(0) = 1$ and $y_2(0) = 0$.
The system is $y' = A y$ with $A = \begin{pmatrix} 3 & -2 \\ -1 & 2 \end{pmatrix}$. We find the eigenvalues:
\[\det(A - \lambda I) = (3-\lambda)(2-\lambda) - 2 = \lambda^2 - 5\lambda + 4 = (\lambda - 4)(\lambda - 1) = 0.\]
The eigenvalues are $\lambda_1 = 4$ and $\lambda_2 = 1$. The corresponding eigenvectors are:
For $\lambda_1 = 4$: $\begin{pmatrix} -1 & -2 \\ -1 & -2 \end{pmatrix} v = 0 \implies v_1 = \begin{pmatrix} -2 \\ 1 \end{pmatrix}$.
For $\lambda_2 = 1$: $\begin{pmatrix} 2 & -2 \\ -1 & 1 \end{pmatrix} v = 0 \implies v_2 = \begin{pmatrix} 1 \\ 1 \end{pmatrix}$.
The general solution is $y(t) = C_1 e^{4t} \begin{pmatrix} -2 \\ 1 \end{pmatrix} + C_2 e^{t} \begin{pmatrix} 1 \\ 1 \end{pmatrix}$.
Using the initial conditions $y(0) = \begin{pmatrix} 1 \\ 0 \end{pmatrix}$:
\begin{align*}
    -2C_1 + C_2 &= 1 \\
    C_1 + C_2 &= 0 \implies C_2 = -C_1.
\end{align*}
Substituting this gives $-3C_1 = 1$, so $C_1 = -1/3$ and $C_2 = 1/3$. The particular solution is:
\[y(t) = -\frac{1}{3} e^{4t} \begin{pmatrix} -2 \\ 1 \end{pmatrix} + \frac{1}{3} e^{t} \begin{pmatrix} 1 \\ 1 \end{pmatrix} = \frac{1}{3} \begin{pmatrix} 2e^{4t} + e^t \\ -e^{4t} + e^t \end{pmatrix}.\]
\end{example}

\subsection{Autonomous systems and phase portraits}
\label{sec:autonomous_systems_examples}

% Transition: Claude Sonnet 5 (High)
A system $u'(t) = F(u(t))$ in which $F$ does not depend on $t$ explicitly is called
\newterm{autonomous} \germanfor{autonom}. Its flow can then be pictured directly as a vector
field on the state space, independent of time: at each point $x$, the arrow $F(x)$ shows the
instantaneous velocity of the trajectory passing through $x$. The three examples below (two
linear, one not) are the standard pictures.

% Supplement: Analysis_II_Script_v1.pdf, p. 182
\begin{example}[A pure rotation]
\label{ex:autonomous_rotation}
The system $u_1'=-u_2$, $u_2'=u_1$ is $u'=Au$ with $A = \left(\begin{smallmatrix}0&-1\\1&0\end{smallmatrix}\right)$. The unique solution with $u(0)=x_0$ is
\[u(t) = e^{tA}x_0 = \begin{pmatrix}\cos t & -\sin t\\ \sin t & \cos t\end{pmatrix}x_0,\]
a rigid rotation of $x_0$ around the origin at unit angular speed: $F$ is the velocity field of a
fluid rotating rigidly counterclockwise, and every trajectory is a circle centred at the origin.
\begin{center}
\begin{tikzpicture}[>=Latex, scale=1.1]
  \draw[gray!50, thick] (0,0) circle (1.4);
  \draw[red!80!black, thick, ->] (1.4,0) arc (0:290:1.4);
  \fill (1.4,0) circle (1.5pt) node[right, font=\footnotesize] {$x_0$};
  \foreach \a in {0,45,...,315} {
    \draw[blue!60!black, ->] ({1.4*cos(\a)},{1.4*sin(\a)})
      -- ++({-0.35*sin(\a)},{0.35*cos(\a)});
  }
  \node[below, font=\small] at (0,-1.7) {$u(t) = e^{tA}x_0$, circular trajectories};
\end{tikzpicture}
\end{center}
\end{example}

\begin{example}[A rotating spiral]
\label{ex:autonomous_spiral}
The system $u_1'=u_1-u_2$, $u_2'=u_1+u_2$ is $u'=Au$ with
$A = \left(\begin{smallmatrix}1&-1\\1&1\end{smallmatrix}\right) = \id + B$, where
$B := \left(\begin{smallmatrix}0&-1\\1&0\end{smallmatrix}\right)$ is the generator of
\cref{ex:autonomous_rotation}. Since $\id$ and $B$ commute, $e^{tA}=e^{t\id}e^{tB}$, so
\[u(t) = e^{tA}x_0 = e^t\begin{pmatrix}\cos t & -\sin t\\ \sin t & \cos t\end{pmatrix}x_0:\]
the same rotation as before, now with an exponentially growing radius. The trajectory is an
outward spiral.

\end{example}

\begin{example}[A nonlinear system with a limit cycle]
\label{ex:autonomous_limit_cycle}
Consider the nonlinear autonomous system
\[u' = u-v-(u^2+v^2)u, \qquad v' = u+v-(u^2+v^2)v,\]
with initial value $(u(0),v(0))=(x_0,y_0)$. If $(x_0,y_0)=(0,0)$, the constant function
$(u,v)\equiv(0,0)$ is a solution, and \cref{thm:picard_lindeloef_local} (the right-hand side is
$C^1$, \cref{ex:c1_implies_locally_lipschitz}) shows it is the \emph{only} one.

For $(x_0,y_0) \neq (0,0)$, switch to polar coordinates $u=r\cos\varphi$, $v=r\sin\varphi$ with
$r^2=u^2+v^2$. Differentiating $r^2=u^2+v^2$ and substituting the system,
\[(r^2)' = 2uu'+2vv' = 2\big(u^2-uv-(u^2+v^2)u^2\big) + 2\big(uv+v^2-(u^2+v^2)v^2\big)
  = 2(r^2-r^4),\]
so, since $(r^2)'=2rr'$, the radius satisfies the autonomous scalar equation
\[r' = r-r^3, \qquad r(0)=\sqrt{x_0^2+y_0^2}.\]
Separating variables and solving gives $r(t) = \exp(t)/\sqrt{A+\exp(2t)}$ for
$A := 1/(x_0^2+y_0^2)-1 \in (-1,\infty)$, and $r(t) \to 1$ as $t \to \infty$
\emph{regardless of the initial value}. Therefore the circle $r=1$ is a \newterm{limit cycle}
\germanfor{Grenzzykel}, attracting every trajectory except the equilibrium at the origin. A
short computation with the angle ($u\varphi'=u-r^2u$ from the system, matched against $u'$, and
symmetrically for $v$) gives $\varphi'\equiv1$ away from the origin, so the motion is a steady
rotation combined with the radial approach to $r=1$: trajectories starting outside the unit
circle spiral in, and those starting inside spiral out, both converging to the same circular
orbit.
\begin{center}
\begin{tikzpicture}[scale=1.3]
  \draw[gray!60, thick, dashed] (0,0) circle (1);
  \node[gray!60!black, font=\footnotesize] at (0.75,0.75) {$r=1$};
  \draw[red!80!black, thick, ->, domain=0:620, samples=140, smooth]
    plot ({(1+0.55*exp(-0.012*\x))*cos(\x)}, {(1+0.55*exp(-0.012*\x))*sin(\x)});
  \draw[blue!70!black, thick, ->, domain=0:620, samples=140, smooth]
    plot ({(1-0.75*exp(-0.012*\x))*cos(\x)}, {(1-0.75*exp(-0.012*\x))*sin(\x)});
  \node[below, font=\small, align=center] at (0,-1.85)
    {qualitative sketch: outside (red) spirals in, inside (blue) spirals out,\\
     both approaching the limit cycle $r=1$};
\end{tikzpicture}
\end{center}
\end{example}

\section{Inhomogeneous linear ODEs}
\label{sec:inhomogeneous_odes}

Given the $n$-th order linear inhomogeneous ODE
\[Ly(x) = b(x), \qquad \text{where } L = \sum_{k=0}^n a_k \frac{d^k}{dx^k},\]
the general solution is always of the form
\[y(x) = y_{\text{hom}}(x) + y_{\text{part}}(x).\]

The homogeneous solution $y_{\text{hom}}(x)$ fulfills $Ly_{\text{hom}}(x) = 0$, or $\sum_{k=0}^n a_k y_{\text{hom}}^{(k)}(x) = 0$. This is solved using the general Ansatz $y_{\text{hom}}(x) = C e^{rx}$, which leads to the characteristic equation:
\[\sum_{k=0}^n a_k C r^k e^{rx} = 0 \implies \sum_{k=0}^n a_k r^k = 0,\]
yielding roots $r_1, \dots, r_n$ (assuming they are all distinct).

The particular solution $y_{\text{part}}(x)$ solves $Ly_{\text{part}}(x) = b(x)$ and is found using a fitting Ansatz, often an \qt{educated guess} based on the form of the inhomogeneous term $b(x)$.

\begin{example}[Solving an inhomogeneous linear ODE]
\label{ex:ode_inhomogeneous}
% Michael's Bsp. 24.2.1
Consider the second-order ODE:
\[y'' - 4y' + 13y = x e^x.\]
First, we solve the homogeneous problem $y'' - 4y' + 13y = 0$. The characteristic polynomial is $\lambda^2 - 4\lambda + 13 = 0$, with roots:
\[\lambda_{1,2} = \frac{4 \pm \sqrt{16 - 52}}{2} = 2 \pm 3i.\]
The homogeneous solution is therefore:
\[y_{\text{hom}}(x) = e^{2x} \big(C_1 \cos(3x) + C_2 \sin(3x)\big).\]
Next, we find a particular solution for the inhomogeneous term $b(x) = x e^x$. According to \cref{tab:ode_ansatz}, we use the Ansatz $y_{\text{part}}(x) = (Ax + B)e^x$. Since $\lambda = 1$ is not a root of the characteristic polynomial, we do not need to multiply by an extra factor of $x$. We compute the derivatives:
\begin{align*}
    y_{\text{part}}'(x) &= A e^x + (Ax + B)e^x = (Ax + A + B)e^x, \\
    y_{\text{part}}''(x) &= A e^x + (Ax + A + B)e^x = (Ax + 2A + B)e^x.
\end{align*}
Plugging these into the original ODE yields:
\begin{align*}
    (Ax + 2A + B)e^x - 4(Ax + A + B)e^x + 13(Ax + B)e^x &= x e^x \\
    \big((A - 4A + 13A)x + (2A + B - 4A - 4B + 13B)\big)e^x &= x e^x \\
    (10Ax - 2A + 10B)e^x &= x e^x.
\end{align*}
Comparing coefficients, we get $10A = 1 \implies A = \frac{1}{10}$, and $-2A + 10B = 0 \implies 10B = \frac{2}{10} \implies B = \frac{1}{50}$.
The particular solution is $y_{\text{part}}(x) = \left(\frac{1}{10}x + \frac{1}{50}\right)e^x$. The general solution is then $y(x) = y_{\text{hom}}(x) + y_{\text{part}}(x)$.
\end{example}

\begin{exercise}[Three linear ODEs]
\label{ex:three_linear_odes}
\begin{enumerate}[label=\textbf{(\alph*)}]
  \item Determine the general solution on $I=\mathbb{R}$ of $y''-4y'+4y=\sin t$.
  \item Determine the solution on $I=\mathbb{R}$ of the initial value problem
    $y''-y=t$, $y(0)=1$, $y'(0)=3$.
  \item Find a solution on $I=(0,\infty)$ of the initial value problem
    $y'-\big(\tfrac4t+1\big)y=t^4$, $y(1)=1$.
\end{enumerate}
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercisesolution}[Proof of \cref{ex:three_linear_odes}]
% Generator: Claude Sonnet 5 (High)
\begin{enumerate}[label=\textbf{(\alph*)}]
  \item The characteristic polynomial $r^2-4r+4=(r-2)^2$ has the double root $r=2$, so
    $y_{\mathrm{hom}}(t) = (C_1+C_2t)e^{2t}$. For the particular solution, try
    $y_{\mathrm{part}}(t) := A\sin t+B\cos t$ (\cref{tab:ode_ansatz}, with $\omega=1$ not a root
    of the characteristic polynomial, so no extra factor of $t$ is needed). Substituting,
    \[y_{\mathrm{part}}''-4y_{\mathrm{part}}'+4y_{\mathrm{part}}
      = (3A+4B)\sin t + (3B-4A)\cos t \overset{!}{=} \sin t,\]
    so $3A+4B=1$ and $-4A+3B=0$, giving $B=\tfrac43A$ and then $A=\tfrac{3}{25}$,
    $B=\tfrac{4}{25}$. The general solution is
    \[y(t) = (C_1+C_2t)e^{2t} + \tfrac{3}{25}\sin t + \tfrac{4}{25}\cos t.\]

  \item The characteristic polynomial $r^2-1$ has roots $r=\pm1$, so
    $y_{\mathrm{hom}}(t) = C_1e^t+C_2e^{-t}$. Since $r=0$ is not a root, try
    $y_{\mathrm{part}}(t) := At+B$; then $y_{\mathrm{part}}''-y_{\mathrm{part}} = -At-B
    \overset{!}{=} t$ gives $A=-1$, $B=0$, so $y_{\mathrm{part}}(t) = -t$. The general solution is
    $y(t) = C_1e^t+C_2e^{-t}-t$, with $y'(t) = C_1e^t-C_2e^{-t}-1$. The initial conditions give
    $C_1+C_2=1$ and $C_1-C_2=4$, so $C_1=\tfrac52$, $C_2=-\tfrac32$, and
    \[y(t) = \tfrac52e^t - \tfrac32e^{-t} - t.\]

  \item This is a first-order linear ODE, $y'+p(t)y=t^4$ with
    $p(t) := -\big(\tfrac4t+1\big)$, so the standard integrating factor is
    $\mu(t) := \exp\!\big(\int p(t)\,dt\big) = \exp\big(-4\ln t - t\big) = t^{-4}e^{-t}$.

    \begin{ainote}
    The script's own hint suggests $u(t):=e^{H(t)}y(t)$ with $H'(t)=\tfrac4t+1$, giving a
    \emph{positive} exponent. Checking it directly, $u' = e^H(H'y+y')$, and substituting
    $y'=H'y+t^4$ (from the ODE) gives $u' = e^H(2H'y+t^4)$, which still contains $y$. The
    substitution therefore does not decouple. The
    sign must be negative, $u(t) := e^{-H(t)}y(t) = t^{-4}e^{-t}y(t)$: then
    $u' = e^{-H}(y'-H'y) = e^{-H}\big(y'-(\tfrac4t+1)y\big) = e^{-H}t^4$ directly from the ODE,
    which \emph{does} decouple. Used below.
    \end{ainote}

    So with $u(t) := t^{-4}e^{-t}y(t)$,
    \[u'(t) = t^{-4}e^{-t}t^4 = e^{-t} \qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad u(t) = -e^{-t}+C\]
    for a constant $C$. Hence $y(t) = t^4e^t\big(C-e^{-t}\big) = Ct^4e^t - t^4$. The initial
    condition $y(1)=1$ gives $Ce-1=1$, i.e.\ $C = 2/e$, so
    \[y(t) = 2t^4e^{t-1} - t^4, \qquad t \in (0,\infty).\]
\end{enumerate}
\end{exercisesolution}

\begin{example}[Pure resonance of a pumped harmonic oscillator]
\label{ex:pure_resonance}
Consider the forced harmonic oscillator $y : [0,\infty) \to \mathbb{R}$,
\[y''(t) + ky(t) = \sin(\omega t) \quad \text{for } t>0, \qquad y(0)=y'(0)=0,\]
with $k>0$. The homogeneous equation $z''+kz=0$ has general solution
$z(t) = C_1\cos(\sqrt kt)+C_2\sin(\sqrt kt)$. For $\omega \neq \sqrt k$, the Ansatz
$y_{\mathrm{part}}(t) := A\sin(\omega t)+B\cos(\omega t)$ gives, on substitution,
$A(k-\omega^2)=1$ and $B(k-\omega^2)=0$, so $B=0$ and $A = 1/(k-\omega^2)$, giving
$y_{\mathrm{part}}(t) = \sin(\omega t)/(k-\omega^2)$. Imposing $y(0)=y'(0)=0$ on the general
solution $y=z+y_{\mathrm{part}}$ fixes $C_1=0$ and $C_2 = -\omega/\big(\sqrt k(k-\omega^2)\big)$,
giving the complete solution, for $\omega \neq \sqrt k$,
\[y(t) = \frac{1}{\sqrt k+\omega}\left(\frac{\sin(\sqrt kt)}{\sqrt k} -
  \frac{\sin(\sqrt kt)-\sin(\omega t)}{\sqrt k-\omega}\right).\]
Taking the limit $\omega \to \sqrt k$ (\newterm{resonance}: the forcing frequency matches the
system's natural frequency) gives
\[y(t) = \frac{1}{2\sqrt k}\left(\frac{\sin(\sqrt kt)}{\sqrt k} - t\cos(\sqrt kt)\right).\]
The term $t\cos(\sqrt kt)$ shows the amplitude of the oscillation now grows \emph{linearly} in
$t$, without bound. This is the phenomenon behind bridges failing under marching troops and,
per popular-science lore not verified here, opera singers shattering glasses.
\end{example}

\begin{remark}
This is the concrete instance of the \qt{special case} noted in the caption of
\cref{tab:ode_ansatz}: the naive Ansatz $A\sin(\omega t)+B\cos(\omega t)$ fails exactly when
$\omega=\sqrt k$, because $\sin(\sqrt kt)$ and $\cos(\sqrt kt)$ already solve the homogeneous
equation. The fix is the same one the table prescribes. Multiplying the Ansatz by $t$ is
visibly what appears in the limiting solution above.
\end{remark}

\subsection{Duhamel's Principle}
\label{sec:duhamels_principle}

For a system of inhomogeneous linear ODEs $u'(t) = A u(t) + f(t)$, the solution can be constructed by combining the homogeneous solution and a particular solution obtained via Duhamel's principle:
\[u(t) = \underbrace{e^{At} u(0)}_{\text{homogeneous solution}} + \underbrace{\int_0^t e^{(t-s)A} f(s) \,ds}_{\text{inhomogeneous part}}.\]
This elegantly generalizes the $1$-dimensional case where the solution to $u'(t) = a u(t) + f(t)$ is given by $u(t) = e^{at} u(0) + \int_0^t e^{a(t-s)} f(s) \,ds$.

\section{The Cauchy--Lipschitz / Picard--Lindelöf theorem}
\label{sec:cauchy_lipschitz}

The Cauchy--Lipschitz theorem (also known as the Picard--Lindelöf theorem) guarantees the existence and uniqueness of solutions to first-order initial value problems, provided the right-hand side is Lipschitz continuous with respect to the state variable.
For an equation of the form $y'(x) = F(x, y(x))$, one first puts the differential equation in normal form to determine if the theorem applies, which requires checking the properties of $F$.

% Source: Jérôme Paschoud/Notizen/Woche 14.2 DGL und Picard Lindelof.pdf, p. 3
% Generator: Gemini 3.1 Pro (High)
\begin{theorem}[Picard--Lindelöf, local existence and uniqueness]
\label{thm:picard_lindeloef_local}
Let $U \subseteq \mathbb{R} \times \mathbb{R}^n$ be an open set, $(t_0, x_0) \in U$, and $F: U \to \mathbb{R}^n$ be continuous. If $F$ is locally Lipschitz continuous in $x$, i.e.,
\begin{align*}
\forall (t_1, x_1) \in U \;\; &\exists \varepsilon > 0, L \geq 0 \;\; \text{such that} \\
&\forall (t, x_2), (t, x_3) \in B_\varepsilon(t_1, x_1) \cap U, \;\; |F(t, x_2) - F(t, x_3)| \leq L |x_2 - x_3|,
\end{align*}
then there exists an interval $(a, b) \subseteq \mathbb{R}$ with $t_0 \in (a, b)$ such that a differentiable function $u: (a, b) \to \mathbb{R}^n$ exists which satisfies $(t, u(t)) \in U$ for all $t \in (a, b)$, and $u$ is a solution to the initial value problem:
\[
\begin{cases}
  u'(t) = F(t, u(t)) \\
  u(t_0) = x_0.
\end{cases}
\]
Furthermore, for any other solution $v: (\tilde{a}, \tilde{b}) \to \mathbb{R}^n$ of the same initial value problem, we have $(\tilde{a}, \tilde{b}) \subseteq (a, b)$ and $u|_{(\tilde{a}, \tilde{b})} = v$. As $t \to a$ or $t \to b$, the limit $\lim_{t \to a} (t, u(t))$ (respectively $\lim_{t \to b} (t, u(t))$) either does not exist or does not lie in $U$.
\end{theorem}

% Source: Jérôme Paschoud/Notizen/Woche 14.2 DGL und Picard Lindelof.pdf, p. 3
% Generator: Gemini 3.1 Pro (High)
A simplified, highly practical formulation is the following:
\begin{corollary}[Picard--Lindelöf, simplified version]
\label{cor:picard_lindeloef_simplified}
Let $I \subseteq \mathbb{R}$ be a compact interval, $\Omega \subseteq \mathbb{R}^n$ an open interval (or open set), $(t_0, y_0) \in I \times \Omega$, and $f: I \times \Omega \to \mathbb{R}^n$ continuous. Suppose $f$ satisfies the Lipschitz condition
\[
|f(t, y_1) - f(t, y_2)| \leq L |y_1 - y_2| \qquad \forall t \in I \text{ and } \forall y_1, y_2 \in \Omega.
\]
Then there exists a $\delta > 0$ such that the initial value problem $u' = f(t, u)$ with $u(t_0) = y_0$ has a unique solution $u: I \cap [t_0-\delta, t_0+\delta] \to \mathbb{R}^n$.
If $\Omega = \mathbb{R}^n$, one can choose the domain to be the entire interval $I$.
\end{corollary}

% Source: Jérôme Paschoud/Notizen/Woche 14.2 DGL und Picard Lindelof.pdf, p. 4
% Generator: Gemini 3.1 Pro (High)
\begin{example}[Applying Picard--Lindelöf]
\label{ex:picard_lindeloef_application}
Let $I = [0, \infty)$ and $\Omega = [0, \infty)$. Consider the function $f: I \times \Omega \to \mathbb{R}$ given by $f(t, y) = \frac{y}{1+t^2}$. We want to prove that the differential equation $u'(t) - \frac{u(t)}{1+t^2} = 0$ has a unique solution for $u: (0, \infty) \to \mathbb{R}$.

We rewrite the ODE as $u'(t) = f(t, u(t))$. To apply the Picard--Lindelöf theorem, we check if $f$ satisfies the Lipschitz condition in $y$:
\[
\lvert f(t, y_1) - f(t, y_2) \rvert = \left\lvert \frac{y_1}{1+t^2} - \frac{y_2}{1+t^2} \right\rvert = \frac{1}{1+t^2} \lvert y_1 - y_2 \rvert \leq 1 \cdot \lvert y_1 - y_2 \rvert.
\]
Since $\frac{1}{1+t^2} \leq 1$ for all $t \in [0, \infty)$, the function $f$ is Lipschitz continuous in $y$ with Lipschitz constant $L=1$. Thus, by Picard--Lindelöf, the ODE possesses a unique solution.
\end{example}

% Supplement: Analysis_II_Script_v1.pdf, p. 192
\begin{exercise}[$C^1$ in space implies locally Lipschitz]
\label{ex:c1_implies_locally_lipschitz}
Let $U \subseteq \mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{R}^n$ be open and $F : U \to \mathbb{R}^n$ be
continuous, such that the partial derivatives $\partial_{x_k}F$ for $k \in \{1,\dots,n\}$ exist
and are continuous on $U$. Show that $F$ is locally Lipschitz in $x$ and thus satisfies the
hypotheses of \cref{thm:picard_lindeloef_local}.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercisesolution}[Proof of \cref{ex:c1_implies_locally_lipschitz}]
% Generator: Claude Sonnet 5 (High)
Fix $(t_1,x_1) \in U$. Since $U$ is open, choose $\varepsilon>0$ with the closed ball
$\overline{B_\varepsilon(t_1,x_1)} \subseteq U$; this closed ball is compact. Each
$\partial_{x_k}F$ is continuous, hence bounded on this compact set, say by $M_k$; set
$L := \sum_{k=1}^n M_k$.

For $(t,x_2),(t,x_3) \in B_\varepsilon(t_1,x_1)$, the straight segment
$s\mapsto x_3+s(x_2-x_3)$, $s\in[0,1]$, stays inside the ball (which is convex), so
$g(s) := F(t,\,x_3+s(x_2-x_3))$ is differentiable on $[0,1]$ with
$g'(s) = \sum_{k=1}^n \partial_{x_k}F(t,x_3+s(x_2-x_3))\,(x_2-x_3)_k$ by the chain rule. Hence
\begin{align*}
|F(t,x_2)-F(t,x_3)| &= |g(1)-g(0)|
  = \left|\int_0^1 g'(s)\,ds\right| \\
  &\leq \int_0^1 \sum_{k=1}^n \lvert\partial_{x_k}F(t,x_3+s(x_2-x_3))\rvert
    \,\lvert(x_2-x_3)_k\rvert\,ds \\
  &\leq \sum_{k=1}^n M_k\,\lvert(x_2-x_3)_k\rvert
  \leq \Big(\sum_{k=1}^n M_k\Big)|x_2-x_3| = L|x_2-x_3|,
\end{align*}
using $\lvert(x_2-x_3)_k\rvert \leq |x_2-x_3|$ in the last step. So $F$ is
$L$-Lipschitz in $x$ on $B_\varepsilon(t_1,x_1)\cap U$, and since $(t_1,x_1) \in U$ was
arbitrary, $F$ is locally Lipschitz in $x$.
\end{exercisesolution}

\begin{remark}
This is the standard shortcut for checking the hypothesis of
\cref{thm:picard_lindeloef_local} in practice: one essentially never verifies the Lipschitz
estimate by hand, but instead checks that $F$ is $C^1$ in the spatial variables. That is
exactly the regularity most vector fields one writes down already have.
\end{remark}

% Supplement: Analysis_II_Script_v1.pdf, p. 173
\begin{lemma}[Grönwall's inequality]
\label{lem:gronwall}
Let $u \in C^1([a,b])$ satisfy $u'(t) \leq \beta(t)u(t)$ for all $t \in [a,b]$, where
$\beta \in C^0([a,b])$. Then
\[u(t) \ \leq\ u(a)\exp\!\left(\int_a^t\beta(s)\,ds\right) \qquad \text{for all } t \in [a,b].\]
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
Let $v(t) := \exp\big(\int_a^t\beta(s)\,ds\big) > 0$, so $v'(t) = \beta(t)v(t)$, and set
$f(t) := u(t)/v(t) \in C^1([a,b])$. Then
\[f'(t) = \frac{u'(t)v(t)-u(t)v'(t)}{v(t)^2}
  \leq \frac{\beta(t)u(t)v(t) - u(t)\beta(t)v(t)}{v(t)^2} = 0,\]
using the hypothesis $u'(t) \leq \beta(t)u(t)$ in the numerator. So $f$ is decreasing on $[a,b]$,
giving $u(t)/v(t) = f(t) \leq f(a) = u(a)/v(a) = u(a)$, i.e.\ $u(t) \leq u(a)v(t)$, which is the
claim.
\end{proof}

\begin{remark}[What Grönwall is for]
Grönwall's inequality is the standard tool for turning a \emph{differential} bound into an
\emph{integral} one. The official script uses it to give a clean uniqueness proof for linear
ODEs: if $z$ solves an order-$m$ linear equation $Lz=0$, the quantity
$u(t) := z(t)^2+z'(t)^2+\dots+z^{(m-1)}(t)^2$ satisfies a bound $u'(t) \leq Cu(t)$ for a constant
$C$ depending only on the coefficients of $L$, and Grönwall with $u(t_0)=0$ (zero initial data)
forces $u\equiv0$, hence $z\equiv0$. This is the \qt{zero in, zero out} principle behind
uniqueness for linear ODEs. The same device, run on $u(t):=|v_1(t)-v_2(t)|^2$ for two solutions
$v_1,v_2$ of a Lipschitz system agreeing at one time, gives an alternative route to the
uniqueness half of \cref{thm:picard_lindeloef_local}.
\end{remark}

\subsection{Picard iteration}
\label{sec:picard_iteration}

The proof of \cref{thm:picard_lindeloef_local} is a Banach fixed-point argument
(\cref{thm:banach_fixed_point}), and fixed-point arguments are \emph{constructive}: the sequence
of approximations built along the way converges to the solution. So Picard--Lindelöf does not
merely assert that a solution exists. It hands over a procedure for computing one.

The starting point is that an initial value problem can be rewritten as an integral equation.
Integrating $y'(s) = F(s,y(s))$ from $t_0$ to $t$ and using $y(t_0) = u_0$,
\[y'(t) = F(t,y(t)),\quad y(t_0) = u_0
  \qquad\Longleftrightarrow\qquad
  y(t) = u_0 + \int_{t_0}^{t} F\big(s,y(s)\big)\,ds.\]
The right-hand side exhibits $y$ as a fixed point of the operator sending a function to
$u_0 + \int_{t_0}^{\cdot}F(s,\cdot)\,ds$, and the Lipschitz hypothesis is exactly what makes that
operator a contraction on a small enough time interval.

\begin{definition}[Picard iteration]
\label{def:picard_iteration}
Starting from the constant function $y_0(t) :\equiv u_0$, the \newterm{Picard iteration}
\germanfor{Picard-Iteration} is the sequence
\[y_n(t) := u_0 + \int_{t_0}^{t} F\big(s,\,y_{n-1}(s)\big)\,ds, \qquad n \geq 1.\]
\end{definition}

Under the hypotheses of \cref{thm:picard_lindeloef_local}, $(y_n)_{n\in\mathbb{N}}$ converges
uniformly to the exact solution on every compact subinterval of the maximal interval of
existence.

% Generator: Claude Opus 5 (High)
\begin{aiexample}[Picard iteration recovers a known solution]
\label{ex:picard_iteration_exponential}
Take $y' = y$ with $y(0) = 1$, so $F(t,y) = y$, $t_0 = 0$ and $u_0 = 1$. Then $y_0 \equiv 1$ and
\begin{align*}
  y_1(t) &= 1 + \int_0^t 1\,ds = 1+t, \\
  y_2(t) &= 1 + \int_0^t (1+s)\,ds = 1+t+\tfrac{t^2}{2}, \\
  y_3(t) &= 1 + \int_0^t \Big(1+s+\tfrac{s^2}{2}\Big)ds = 1+t+\tfrac{t^2}{2}+\tfrac{t^3}{6}.
\end{align*}
An induction gives $y_n(t) = \sum_{k=0}^{n} t^k/k!$, the $n$-th partial sum of the exponential
series. The iteration converges to $e^t$, which is indeed the solution. Pleasingly, it
reconstructs the Taylor series of $e^t$ one term per step, since each integration raises the
degree by exactly one.
\end{aiexample}

% Generator: Claude Opus 5 (High) --- what survives when the Lipschitz hypothesis is dropped.
% Included because the natural student question after Picard--Lindelöf is whether Lipschitz is
% needed for existence or only for uniqueness, and the answer is a clean "only for uniqueness".
\begin{theorem}[Peano existence theorem]
\label{thm:peano_existence_theorem}
Let $I := [t_0-a,\ t_0+a] \subseteq \mathbb{R}$ and $B := \overline{B_b(x_0)} \subseteq
\mathbb{R}^n$, and let $f : I \times B \to \mathbb{R}^n$ be \emph{continuous}, with no
Lipschitz condition in $x$ assumed. Put $M := \max_{I\times B}|f|$, which is finite by
compactness, and $h := \min\{a,\ b/M\}$. Then the initial value problem
\[\dot x(t) = f\big(t, x(t)\big), \qquad x(t_0) = x_0\]
has at least one $C^1$ solution $x : [t_0-h,\ t_0+h] \to B$.
\end{theorem}

\begin{remark}[Peano versus Picard--Lindelöf]
\label{rem:peano_vs_picard}
Dropping the Lipschitz hypothesis costs exactly uniqueness and nothing else. The standard witness
is $\dot x = \sqrt{\lvert x\rvert}$ with $x(0) = 0$: the right-hand side is continuous everywhere
but not Lipschitz at $x = 0$, since $\sqrt{s}/s \to \infty$ as $s \downarrow 0$. Both
\[x(t) \equiv 0 \qquad\text{and}\qquad x(t) = \tfrac14t^2 \ \ (t \geq 0)\]
solve it, as do the infinitely many solutions that wait at $0$ until an arbitrary time $\tau > 0$
and only then take off along $\tfrac14(t-\tau)^2$. Peano's theorem still applies and still
delivers a solution; it simply cannot say which one.

Note also where the two theorems get their machinery. Picard--Lindelöf is a fixed-point
argument. It runs the Banach fixed-point theorem (\cref{thm:banach_fixed_point}) on the integral
operator, and the Lipschitz constant is precisely what makes that operator a contraction. Peano
has no contraction to work with and instead argues by compactness, extracting a convergent
subsequence from a family of approximate solutions. The theorem licensing that extraction
is Ascoli--Arzelà (\cref{thm:ascoli_arzela}), whose equicontinuity hypothesis is supplied by the
bound $\lvert F\rvert \leq C$: it makes all the approximate solutions Lipschitz with the same
constant $C$. That is also why it gives no uniqueness: a compactness argument produces \emph{a}
limit, never \emph{the} limit.
\end{remark}

\subsection{Dependence on the initial condition}
\label{sec:dependence_on_initial_conditions}

Picard--Lindelöf produces, for each starting point, one solution. In applications one rarely has
the starting point exactly: it is measured, and therefore wrong by a little. So the question is
not only whether a solution exists but whether it \emph{moves continuously} when the initial
condition is perturbed, and more sharply whether it is differentiable in that data.

Write $u(t;x_0)$ for the value at time $t$ of the solution of
\[u' = F(t,u), \qquad u(0) = x_0,\]
so that $u(\,\cdot\,;x_0)$ is the trajectory through $x_0$ and $u(t;\,\cdot\,)$ records where
each starting point has been carried by time $t$.

\begin{theorem}[Differentiability in the initial condition]
\label{thm:differentiability_initial_conditions}
Let $\Omega \subseteq \mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{R}^n$ be open and convex and let
$F : \Omega \to \mathbb{R}^n$ be continuous and $C^1$ in its spatial variable. Then
$x_0 \mapsto u(t;x_0)$ is continuously differentiable, on the set of $x_0$ for which the solution
exists up to time $t$.
\end{theorem}

\begin{remark}[Why this is more than a technicality]
The hypothesis is barely stronger than the one Picard--Lindelöf already needs ($C^1$ in space
rather than locally Lipschitz in space), and the conclusion is what makes the whole subject
usable. It says the \emph{flow} $x_0 \mapsto u(t;x_0)$ is a $C^1$ map, so the trajectories of an
ODE do not merely exist individually but fit together smoothly into a family. Two consequences
worth naming:
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Errors stay controlled.} A measurement error $\varepsilon$ in $x_0$ produces an
    error in $u(t;x_0)$ that is $O(\varepsilon)$ for fixed $t$, rather than something
    uncontrolled. The constant does typically grow with $t$. Differentiability at each fixed
    time is not a statement about long-run stability, and chaotic systems are exactly the ones
    where that constant grows fast.
  \item \textbf{The derivative itself solves an ODE.} Differentiating $u' = F(t,u)$ in $x_0$
    gives that $J(t) := \partial u(t;x_0)/\partial x_0$ satisfies the linear system
    $J' = \Jac_uF(t,u(t;x_0))\,J$ with $J(0) = \id_n$, the \newterm{variational equation}. So the
    sensitivity is computable without ever solving the original equation twice.
\end{itemize}
\end{remark}

\subsection{Higher-order linear ODEs and Cauchy--Lipschitz counterexamples}
\label{sec:higher_order_odes_counterexample}

\begin{exercise}[Third-order linear ODE with constant coefficients]
\label{ex:third_order_linear_ode}
For a parameter $\lambda > 0$ and a continuous function $f : [0,\infty) \to \mathbb{R}$, consider the third-order linear differential equation
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:3rd_order_ode}
y^{(3)}(t) + 2\lambda y''(t) + \lambda^2 y'(t) = f(t), \qquad y(0) = y'(0) = y''(0) = 0.
\end{equation}
\begin{enumerate}[label=\textbf{(\alph*)}]
  \item Find the general solution of the associated homogeneous equation $y^{(3)} + 2\lambda y'' + \lambda^2 y' = 0$.
  \item For $\lambda = 1$ and $f(t) = e^{-t}$, find the explicit solution $y(t)$ of \eqref{eq:3rd_order_ode}.
  \item Show that there exists a smooth impulse response function $h : [0,\infty) \to \mathbb{R}$ such that for any continuous $f$, the solution of \eqref{eq:3rd_order_ode} is given by the convolution integral
  \[y(t) = \int_0^t f(s) h(t-s) \, ds,\]
  and determine $h(t)$ explicitly for general $\lambda > 0$.
\end{enumerate}
\exinfo{This exercise is taken from the exam of 13 February 2025, Short Problem 2. Part 3 of the
original, which asks for the explicit solution when $f(t) = \cos(\lambda t)$, is omitted here.}
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercisesolution}[Proof of \cref{ex:third_order_linear_ode}]
% Generator: Gemini 3.6 Flash (High)
\begin{enumerate}[label=\textbf{(\alph*)}]
  \item The characteristic polynomial of the homogeneous equation is $P(r) = r^3 + 2\lambda r^2 + \lambda^2 r = r(r+\lambda)^2 = 0$. Its roots are $r_1 = 0$ (simple) and $r_2 = -\lambda$ (double root). The general homogeneous solution is:
\[y_h(t) = C_1 + C_2 e^{-\lambda t} + C_3 t e^{-\lambda t}, \qquad C_1, C_2, C_3 \in \mathbb{R}.\]

  \item For $\lambda = 1$ and $f(t) = e^{-t}$, the characteristic root $r = -1$ is a double root of $P(r)$, so the trial particular solution is $y_p(t) = A t^2 e^{-t}$.
Differentiating $y_p$:
\begin{align*}
y_p'(t) &= A (2t - t^2) e^{-t}, \\
y_p''(t) &= A (2 - 4t + t^2) e^{-t}, \\
y_p^{(3)}(t) &= A (-6 + 6t - t^2) e^{-t}.
\end{align*}
Plugging into $y_p^{(3)} + 2y_p'' + y_p' = e^{-t}$, the terms in $t$ and $t^2$ cancel and only the
constants survive:
\[A \Big[(-6+6t-t^2) + 2(2-4t+t^2) + (2t-t^2)\Big] e^{-t} = -2A e^{-t}.\]
% Correction: Claude Opus 5 (High) --- the bracket sums to -2, not +2. Flash read the constant term
% as +2 and got A = 1/2, which flipped the sign of the whole answer and contradicted its own part
% (c): convolving f = e^{-t} against the h found there returns 1 - (1+t+t^2/2)e^{-t}, not its
% negative. Verified against Sol: y' = f*h' + f(t)h(0) etc. gives y(0)=y'(0)=y''(0)=0.
Therefore $-2A = 1$, so $A = -\tfrac{1}{2}$ and $y_p(t) = -\tfrac{1}{2} t^2 e^{-t}$.
Setting initial conditions on $y(t) = C_1 + C_2 e^{-t} + C_3 t e^{-t} - \frac{1}{2} t^2 e^{-t}$:
\begin{itemize}
  \item $y(0) = C_1 + C_2 = 0$.
  \item $y'(0) = -C_2 + C_3 = 0$, and therefore $C_3 = C_2$.
  \item $y''(0) = C_2 - 2C_3 - 1 = 0$, which reduces to $-C_2 = 1$, so $C_2 = -1$.
\end{itemize}
Thus $C_1 = 1$, $C_2 = -1$, $C_3 = -1$. The explicit solution is:
\[y(t) = 1 - e^{-t} - t e^{-t} - \frac{1}{2} t^2 e^{-t} = 1 - \left(1 + t + \frac{1}{2} t^2\right) e^{-t}.\]
This is consistent with \textbf{(c)}: for $\lambda = 1$ the impulse response is
$h(t) = 1 - e^{-t} - te^{-t}$, and $\int_0^t e^{-s} h(t-s) \, ds$ reproduces exactly this $y$.

  \item By Duhamel's principle, $h(t)$ is the solution of the homogeneous equation $h^{(3)} + 2\lambda h'' + \lambda^2 h' = 0$ with initial conditions $h(0) = 0, h'(0) = 0, h''(0) = 1$.
Writing $h(t) = A + B e^{-\lambda t} + C t e^{-\lambda t}$:
\begin{itemize}
  \item $h(0) = A + B = 0 \implies B = -A$.
  \item $h'(0) = -\lambda B + C = 0 \implies C = \lambda B = -\lambda A$.
  \item $h''(0) = \lambda^2 B - 2\lambda C = -\lambda^2 A + 2\lambda^2 A = \lambda^2 A = 1 \implies A = \frac{1}{\lambda^2}$.
\end{itemize}
Thus $B = -\frac{1}{\lambda^2}$ and $C = -\frac{1}{\lambda}$.
The impulse response is:
\[h(t) = \frac{1}{\lambda^2} \left(1 - e^{-\lambda t} - \lambda t e^{-\lambda t}\right).\]
By linear ODE theory, $y(t) = \int_0^t f(s) h(t-s) \, ds$ satisfies \eqref{eq:3rd_order_ode}.
\end{enumerate}
\end{exercisesolution}

\begin{exercise}[Cauchy--Lipschitz non-uniqueness counterexample]
\label{ex:cauchy_lipschitz_nonuniqueness_power}
Let $\alpha > 1$. Consider the initial value problem
\[u'(t) = F(t, u(t)), \quad t > 0, \qquad u(0) = 0.\]
Give an explicit continuous function $F \in C^0(\mathbb{R}^2)$ such that both $u(t) \equiv 0$ and $u(t) = t^\alpha$ are solutions for $t \ge 0$, and explain why this does not contradict the Cauchy--Lipschitz Theorem (\cref{thm:picard_lindeloef_local}).
\exinfo{This exercise is taken from the exam of 13 February 2025, Box Problem 4. The request to
explain why Cauchy--Lipschitz is not contradicted is added here; the original asks only for $F$.}
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercisesolution}[Proof of \cref{ex:cauchy_lipschitz_nonuniqueness_power}]
% Generator: Gemini 3.6 Flash (High)
Differentiating $u(t) = t^\alpha$ gives $u'(t) = \alpha t^{\alpha-1} = \alpha (t^\alpha)^{(\alpha-1)/\alpha} = \alpha (u(t))^{(\alpha-1)/\alpha}$.
Thus, define $F(t,u) := \alpha \lvert u\rvert^{(\alpha-1)/\alpha}$.
Since $\alpha > 1$, $\beta := \frac{\alpha-1}{\alpha} \in (0,1)$, so $F(t,u) = \alpha \lvert u\rvert^\beta$ is continuous on $\mathbb{R}^2$.
Both functions do solve the problem:
% Correction: Claude Opus 5 (High) --- was an enumerate with \arabic* labels, which style.md forbids
% for sub-parts and cases; these are two named checks, so itemize with a bold lead-in. Also
% "u_1'(0) = 0" corrected to "u_1'(t) = 0", and the \implies signs replaced by prose.
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{The zero solution:} $u_1(t) \equiv 0$ has $u_1'(t) = 0$, and $F(t,0) = 0$. Hence $u_1$ solves the initial value problem.
  \item \textbf{The power solution:} $u_2(t) = t^\alpha$ has $u_2'(t) = \alpha t^{\alpha-1}$, and $F(t, u_2(t)) = \alpha (t^\alpha)^{(\alpha-1)/\alpha} = \alpha t^{\alpha-1}$ for $t \ge 0$. Since $u_2(0) = 0$, it too solves the initial value problem.
\end{itemize}

Why does this not contradict Cauchy--Lipschitz? The theorem asks for $F$ to be locally Lipschitz in
its second argument, and this $F$ is not. Indeed,
\[\frac{\partial F}{\partial u}(t,u) = \alpha \beta \lvert u\rvert^{\beta-1} = (\alpha-1) \lvert u\rvert^{-1/\alpha} \longrightarrow \infty \quad \text{as } u \to 0.\]
The hypothesis therefore fails at the initial point $(0,0)$, which is exactly where the two
solutions separate. Note that continuity alone still buys existence (Peano); it is only uniqueness
that is lost.
\end{exercisesolution}
