\begin{exercisesolution}[Proof of \cref{ex:polygon_inequality}]
\label{sol:polygon_inequality}
% Generator: Claude Sonnet 5 (High)
For $N=3$ this is the triangle inequality itself. For the inductive step, assume the claim
holds for some $N \geq 3$ and let $x_1,\dots,x_{N+1} \in X$. Applying the triangle inequality to
the triple $(x_1,x_N,x_{N+1})$ and then the inductive hypothesis to $x_1,\dots,x_N$,
\[d(x_1,x_{N+1}) \ \leq\ d(x_1,x_N) + d(x_N,x_{N+1})
  \ \leq\ \sum_{i=1}^{N-1}d(x_i,x_{i+1}) + d(x_N,x_{N+1})
  \ =\ \sum_{i=1}^{N}d(x_i,x_{i+1}),\]
which is the claim for $N+1$.
\end{exercisesolution}

\begin{exercisesolution}[Proof of \cref{ex:reshaping_same_convergence}]
\label{sol:reshaping_same_convergence}
% Generator: Claude Sonnet 5 (High)
% Correction: Claude Opus 5 (Ultracode) --- this direction read "Concave functions on $[0,\infty)$
% are continuous, so in particular $h$ is continuous at $0$", which is false at the endpoint and
% was the only thing carrying the step. Continuity at $0$ is now a hypothesis of the exercise;
% the remark below gives the counterexample that makes it necessary.
Suppose $x_n \to x$ with respect to $d$, so $d(x_n,x) \to 0$. By hypothesis $h$ is continuous at
$0$, and $h(0)=0$, so $h(d(x_n,x)) \to 0$, i.e.\ $x_n \to x$ with respect to $h \circ d$.

Conversely, suppose $x_n \to x$ with respect to $h \circ d$. Fix $\varepsilon > 0$; by hypothesis
\textbf{(a)} of \cref{prop:concave_reshaping_metric}, $\delta := h(\varepsilon) > 0$. Choose $N$
such that $h(d(x_n,x)) < \delta$ for all $n \geq N$. If $d(x_n,x) \geq \varepsilon$ held for some
such $n$, monotonicity \textbf{(b)} would give $h(d(x_n,x)) \geq h(\varepsilon) = \delta$, a
contradiction. So $d(x_n,x) < \varepsilon$ for all $n \geq N$, i.e.\ $x_n \to x$ with respect to
$d$.

\begin{remark}[Why continuity at $0$ has to be assumed separately]
Concavity does not supply that hypothesis, and it is worth seeing why not. A concave function is
automatically continuous on the \emph{interior} of its domain, but $[0,\infty)$ has an endpoint,
and at an endpoint a concave function may jump --- upwards, since concavity is what bounds it
below by its chords. Take
\[h(0) := 0, \qquad h(t) := 1+t \quad \text{for } t>0.\]
All three hypotheses of \cref{prop:concave_reshaping_metric} hold: $h$ vanishes exactly at $0$,
it is non-decreasing, and it is concave, the only case needing a look being a blend of $0$ with
some $t>0$, where the value $1+(1-\lambda)t$ does exceed the chord's $(1-\lambda)(1+t)$. So
$h \circ d$ is a perfectly good metric. It is just a discrete one: $h(d(x,y)) > 1$ whenever
$x \neq y$, so the sequences converging in $h \circ d$ are exactly the eventually constant ones,
and on $\mathbb{R}$ with the standard metric $\tfrac1n \to 0$ is lost.

Note which half of the proof breaks. The converse direction uses only \textbf{(a)} and
\textbf{(b)}, never continuity, and remains true for this $h$ --- an eventually constant sequence
converges in every metric. It is the forward direction that needs the hypothesis.
\end{remark}
\end{exercisesolution}

\begin{exercisesolution}[Proof of \cref{ex:ai_metric_equivalence}]
\label{sol:ai_metric_equivalence}
% Generator: Gemini 3.6 Flash (Medium)
For any $x = (x_1, x_2) \in \mathbb{R}^2$, we have $\max\{|x_1|, |x_2|\} \leq |x_1| + |x_2|$, giving $d_3(x,0) \leq d_1(x,0)$. Conversely, since $|x_1| \leq \max\{|x_1|, |x_2|\}$ and $|x_2| \leq \max\{|x_1|, |x_2|\}$, summing these yields $|x_1| + |x_2| \leq 2 \max\{|x_1|, |x_2|\}$, i.e., $d_1(x,0) \leq 2 \, d_3(x,0)$. Both bounds are sharp: equality on the left holds for $x=(1,0)$, and equality on the right holds for $x=(1,1)$.
\end{exercisesolution}

\begin{exercisesolution}[Solution to \cref{ex:metric_space_examples}]
\label{sol:metric_space_examples}
% Generator: Claude Opus 5 (High)
Throughout, non-negativity and symmetry are immediate in every part and are not belaboured; the
interest is in \textbf{definiteness} and the \textbf{triangle inequality}.
\begin{enumerate}[label=\textbf{(\alph*)}]
  \item \textbf{Yes.} Boundedness of $f$ and $g$ makes the supremum finite, so $d$ really maps
    into $\mathbb{R}$. Definiteness: $d(f,g)=0$ forces $|f(x)-g(x)| = 0$ for every $x$, i.e.\
    $f=g$. For the triangle inequality, estimate pointwise and only then take the supremum:
    \[|f(x)-h(x)| \leq |f(x)-g(x)| + |g(x)-h(x)| \leq d(f,g) + d(g,h) \quad \text{for all } x,\]
    so the right-hand side is an upper bound for $|f-h|$ and hence dominates $d(f,h)$. This is
    \cref{ex:sup_metric_is_metric} verbatim, with $[0,1]$ replaced by an arbitrary set $X$ and
    continuity replaced by boundedness. Note that it was boundedness, and not continuity, that
    the argument there actually used.
  \item \textbf{Yes.} Let $\varphi(x) := 1/x$, an injective map $\mathbb{Q}_+ \to \mathbb{Q}_+$,
    and observe that $d(x,y) = |\varphi(x)-\varphi(y)|$. Symmetry and the triangle inequality
    are inherited from $|\cdot|$ without any work, and definiteness is exactly injectivity of
    $\varphi$: from $d(x,y)=0$ we get $1/x = 1/y$, hence $x=y$. This is the same pullback
    construction as \cref{ex:completeness_not_topological}, and the same warning applies. Pulling
    a metric back along an injection \emph{always} produces a metric, which is why the interesting
    question about such a $d$ is never whether it is one, but whether it is complete.
  \item \textbf{No:} the triangle inequality fails. Ignore the second coordinate, as the
    official hint suggests, and take three collinear points $x := (0,0)$, $y := (1,0)$ and
    $z := (2,0)$:
    \[d(x,z) = 2^2 = 4 \quad > \quad d(x,y) + d(y,z) = 1 + 1 = 2.\]
    Squaring produces the same failure as in
    \cref{ex:metric_axioms_are_needed}\textbf{(c)}: a function growing faster than linearly
    makes one long step more expensive than two short ones.
  \item \textbf{Yes.} A sum of two metrics acting on separate coordinates is a metric, so
    everything reduces to the first coordinate, where the claim is that
    $(s,t) \mapsto |s-t|^{1/2}$ satisfies the triangle inequality on $\mathbb{R}$. That follows
    from \textbf{subadditivity of the square root}, $\sqrt{a+b} \leq \sqrt a + \sqrt b$ for
    $a,b \geq 0$, which holds because squaring the right-hand side gives
    $a+b+2\sqrt{ab} \geq a+b$. Hence
    \[|x_1-z_1|^{1/2} \leq \big(|x_1-y_1| + |y_1-z_1|\big)^{1/2}
      \leq |x_1-y_1|^{1/2} + |y_1-z_1|^{1/2}.\]
    Contrast this with part \textbf{(c)}: $\sqrt{\cdot}$ is concave and vanishes at $0$, hence
    subadditive, while $(\cdot)^2$ is convex, hence super-additive. That single distinction
    settles both parts.
  \item \textbf{Yes.} Write $A := X-Y$. Then
    \[\Tr(A\transp A) = \sum_{i=1}^n (A\transp A)_{ii} = \sum_{i,j=1}^n A_{ji}^2,\]
    so $d(X,Y)$ is nothing but the Euclidean distance between $X$ and $Y$ regarded as vectors of
    $\mathbb{R}^{n^2}$, and it is a metric by
    \cref{prop:induced_norm_and_metric}\textbf{(b)}. The associated norm is the
    \newterm{Frobenius norm} \germanfor{Frobeniusnorm}, also called the Hilbert--Schmidt norm.
  \item \textbf{No}, and here it is \textbf{definiteness} that fails, not the triangle
    inequality. Following the hint, suppose $A := X-Y$ is \emph{anti-symmetric},
    $A\transp = -A$. For any $v$ the number $v\transp Av$ is a $1\times1$ matrix, hence equal to
    its own transpose:
    \[v\transp A v = (v\transp A v)\transp = v\transp A\transp v = -\,v\transp A v,\]
    so $v\transp Av = 0$ for \emph{every} $v$. Taking $n=2$ with
    \[X := \begin{pmatrix} 0 & 1 \\ -1 & 0\end{pmatrix}, \qquad
      Y := \begin{pmatrix} 0 & 0 \\ 0 & 0\end{pmatrix}\]
    gives $X \neq Y$ but $d(X,Y) = 0$. Every other axiom does hold, so $d$ is a
    \newterm{pseudo-metric} \germanfor{Pseudometrik}: it cannot separate two matrices
    differing by an anti-symmetric one. Equivalently, it restricts to a genuine metric on the
    symmetric matrices.
  \item \textbf{Yes.} Three things need checking, and the first is the one that is easy to skip.

    \textit{Well defined.} Replacing $x$ by $x+(m,n)$ and $y$ by $y+(m',n')$ shifts $x-y$ by the
    integer vector $(m-m',\,n-n')$, which merely re-indexes the infimum. So $d([x],[y])$ does
    not depend on the representatives chosen.

    \textit{The infimum is a minimum.} Only finitely many integer vectors $(k,h)$ satisfy
    $|x-y+(k,h)| \leq |x-y|$, because $\mathbb{Z}^2$ is discrete; the
    infimum is therefore taken over a finite non-empty set of candidates and is attained. This
    is what the official hint asks you to settle first, and it is what makes the next step
    legitimate.

    \textit{Definiteness.} If $d([x],[y]) = 0$ then, the minimum being attained,
    $x-y+(k,h) = 0$ for some integers $k,h$, that is $x \sim y$ and $[x] = [y]$. Note exactly
    where \qt{the infimum is a minimum} was needed: an infimum equal to $0$ but not attained
    would say nothing at all.

    \textit{Triangle inequality.} Choose minimisers $(k_1,h_1)$ for the pair $(x,y)$ and
    $(k_2,h_2)$ for $(y,z)$. Their sum is a legitimate candidate for the pair $(x,z)$, so
    \begin{align*}
      d([x],[z]) &\leq \big|x-z+(k_1{+}k_2,\ h_1{+}h_2)\big| \\
      &= \big|\big(x-y+(k_1,h_1)\big) + \big(y-z+(k_2,h_2)\big)\big| \\
      &\leq d([x],[y]) + d([y],[z]).
    \end{align*}
\end{enumerate}
\end{exercisesolution}

\begin{ainote}
Parts \textbf{(c)}, \textbf{(d)} and \textbf{(f)} are the ones worth revisiting, because each
breaks (or rescues) a different axiom and together they cover every way the definition can fail.
Part \textbf{(c)} kills the triangle inequality by growing too fast; part \textbf{(d)} rescues it
by growing slowly enough; part \textbf{(f)} satisfies the triangle inequality perfectly and fails
on definiteness instead. Read alongside
\cref{ex:metric_axioms_are_needed}, they make the case that the three axioms are genuinely
independent.
\end{ainote}

\begin{exercisesolution}[Solution to \cref{ex:product_metric_spaces}]
\label{sol:product_metric_spaces}
% Generator: Claude Opus 5 (High)
Write $s := d_X(x,x')$ and $t := d_Y(y,y')$ throughout, so that the three candidates are
$d_1 = \max\{s,t\}$, $d_2 = s+t$ and $d_3 = \sqrt{s^2+t^2}$. These are precisely the supremum,
Manhattan and standard metrics of \cref{ex:metric_examples}, applied to the pair $(s,t)$. Almost
everything below is that one observation.
\begin{enumerate}[label=\textbf{(\alph*)}]
  \item \textbf{All three are metrics.} Each vanishes exactly when $s = t = 0$, i.e.\ when
    $x=x'$ and $y=y'$, which is definiteness; symmetry is inherited from $d_X$ and $d_Y$. For
    the triangle inequality, set
    \[a := \big(d_X(x,x'),\,d_Y(y,y')\big), \quad
      b := \big(d_X(x',x''),\,d_Y(y',y'')\big), \quad
      c := \big(d_X(x,x''),\,d_Y(y,y'')\big),\]
    all lying in $[0,\infty)^2$. The triangle inequalities in $X$ and in $Y$ say precisely that
    $c \leq a+b$ \emph{componentwise}. Each of $\max$, the sum and $\lVert\cdot\rVert_2$ is
    monotone on $[0,\infty)^2$ and subadditive, so
    \[N(c) \ \leq\ N(a+b) \ \leq\ N(a) + N(b)\]
    for $N \in \{\max,\ \textstyle\sum,\ \lVert\cdot\rVert_2\}$, which is the required inequality
    in all three cases at once.
  \item \textbf{Equivalence.} For all $s,t \geq 0$,
    \[\max\{s,t\} \ \leq\ \sqrt{s^2+t^2} \ \leq\ s+t \ \leq\ 2\max\{s,t\},\]
    i.e.\ $d_1 \leq d_3 \leq d_2 \leq 2d_1$; the middle inequality holds because
    $(s+t)^2 = s^2+t^2+2st \geq s^2+t^2$. Hence $C := 2$ satisfies the chain in the statement:
    $d_1 \leq 2d_2$, then $2d_2 \leq 4d_3$ (since $d_2 \leq \sqrt2\,d_3$ by Cauchy--Schwarz,
    and $\sqrt2 \leq 2$), and finally $4d_3 \leq 8d_1$ (since $d_3 \leq \sqrt2\,d_1$).
  \item \textbf{Convergence is coordinatewise.} From part \textbf{(b)},
    \[\max\big\{d_X(x_n,x),\ d_Y(y_n,y)\big\}
      \ \leq\ d_3\big((x_n,y_n),(x,y)\big)
      \ \leq\ d_X(x_n,x) + d_Y(y_n,y).\]
    If the middle term tends to $0$, the left inequality forces both coordinates to converge; if
    both coordinates converge, the right inequality forces the middle term to $0$. This is
    \cref{prop:Rn_coordinatewise_complete}\textbf{(a)} with $n = 2$ and the two factors allowed
    to be arbitrary metric spaces. By part \textbf{(b)} the same conclusion holds for $d_1$ and
    $d_2$, since equivalent metrics have the same convergent sequences.
\end{enumerate}
\end{exercisesolution}

\begin{exercisesolution}[Proof of \cref{ex:integral_reciprocal_inequality}]
\label{sol:integral_reciprocal_inequality}
% Generator: Claude Opus 5 (High) --- the statement is the examiner's; the proof, the equality
% case and the remark are written for these notes. Probeprfg1 ships no solution key.
Work in $V := C([a,b],\mathbb{R})$ with $\langle g,h\rangle := \int_a^b g h$, and take
\[g := \sqrt{f}, \qquad h := \frac{1}{\sqrt f} .\]
Both are continuous, because $f$ is continuous and strictly positive, so the square root and the
reciprocal are applied only where they are smooth. The three quantities Cauchy--Schwarz relates
are then exactly the three appearing in the problem:
\[\langle g,h\rangle = \int_a^b \sqrt{f}\cdot\frac{1}{\sqrt f} = \int_a^b 1 = b-a, \qquad
  \|g\|^2 = \int_a^b f, \qquad \|h\|^2 = \int_a^b \frac1f .\]
\Cref{thm:cauchy_schwarz} gives $\langle g,h\rangle^2 \leq \|g\|^2\|h\|^2$, which is
\[(b-a)^2 \ \leq\ \int_a^b f(x)\,dx \int_a^b \frac{1}{f(x)}\,dx ,\]
as claimed.

\textbf{Equality.} \Cref{thm:cauchy_schwarz} attains equality exactly when $g$ and $h$ are
linearly dependent, that is when $\sqrt f = \lambda/\sqrt f$ for some $\lambda \in \mathbb{R}$,
which says $f \equiv \lambda$. Since $f > 0$ the constant is positive, and conversely every
positive constant gives equality, both sides being $(b-a)^2$. So equality holds precisely for the
constant functions.

\begin{remark}[What the inner product bought]
Nothing in the statement mentions a vector space, and the inequality is about two ordinary
one-variable integrals. What makes it a two-line proof rather than a calculation is choosing the
right two \emph{vectors} in a space of functions, and the choice is forced once one notices that
the product of the two integrands must be $1$, so that the pairing collapses to the length of the
interval.

This is the first place in these notes where the abstract inner product does something the
Euclidean one could not, and it is worth registering why: \cref{thm:cauchy_schwarz} was proved
for \emph{any} inner product space, so it applies verbatim to $C([a,b],\mathbb{R})$, where the
vectors are functions and $\|g\|^2$ is an integral. The generality was not decoration. The
discrete analogue, obtained the same way from the standard inner product on $\mathbb{R}^n$, is
$\big(\sum_i x_i\big)\big(\sum_i 1/x_i\big) \geq n^2$ for positive $x_i$.
\end{remark}
\end{exercisesolution}

\begin{exercisesolution}[Proof of \cref{ex:cauchy_schwarz_statement}]
\label{sol:cauchy_schwarz_statement}
% Generator: Claude Opus 5 (High)
This is \cref{thm:cauchy_schwarz}, proved in \cref{sec:cauchy_schwarz} above by expanding
$\lVert u - \pi_v(u)\rVert^2 \geq 0$, including the equality case. A second, independent proof, by
minimising $\lambda \mapsto \lVert v-\lambda w\rVert^2$, is
\cref{prop:cauchy_schwarz_geometric_proof}. \Cref{rem:two_proofs_cauchy_schwarz}
explains why the two are the same computation approached from opposite ends.

\begin{remark}[A third route: the discriminant]
The official solution gives a third proof, worth recording because it is the one that
generalises most mechanically. By definiteness, the quadratic polynomial
\[\lambda \ \longmapsto\ \langle \lambda v + w,\ \lambda v + w\rangle
  = \lambda^2\langle v,v\rangle + 2\lambda\langle v,w\rangle + \langle w,w\rangle\]
is non-negative for every real $\lambda$. A real quadratic with positive leading coefficient is
non-negative exactly when its discriminant is $\leq 0$, i.e.
\[\big(2\langle v,w\rangle\big)^2 - 4\langle v,v\rangle\langle w,w\rangle \leq 0,\]
which rearranges to the inequality. Equality forces the discriminant to vanish, so the
polynomial has a real root $\lambda_0$, and definiteness turns
$\langle \lambda_0 v + w, \lambda_0 v + w\rangle = 0$ into $\lambda_0 v + w = 0$, which is the
asserted linear dependence. Note that this is the previous proof with the geometry stripped out.
Here $\lambda_0$ is again the projection coefficient, now arriving as a root rather than as a
minimiser.
\end{remark}
\end{exercisesolution}

\begin{exercisesolution}[Solution to \cref{ex:norm_equivalence_rn}]
\label{sol:norm_equivalence_rn}
% Generator: Claude Opus 5 (High)
Write $e_1,\dots,e_n$ for the standard basis and $|\cdot|$ for the Euclidean norm.
\begin{enumerate}[label=\textbf{(\alph*)}]
  \item Expand $x = \sum_{i=1}^n x_ie_i$ and use the triangle inequality and homogeneity of $f$
    (\cref{def:norm}):
    \[f(x) \ \leq\ \sum_{i=1}^n f(x_ie_i) \ =\ \sum_{i=1}^n |x_i|\,f(e_i)
      \ \leq\ \Big(\max_i f(e_i)\Big)\sum_{i=1}^n|x_i|
      \ \leq\ \Big(\max_i f(e_i)\Big)\sqrt n\,|x|,\]
    the last step by Cauchy--Schwarz applied to $(|x_1|,\dots,|x_n|)$ and $(1,\dots,1)$. So
    $C_1 := \sqrt n \max_i f(e_i)$ works, and $C_1 > 0$ because $e_i \neq 0$ forces
    $f(e_i) > 0$ by definiteness.
  \item The reverse triangle inequality for $f$, namely $f(x) \leq f(y) + f(x-y)$ together with
    its mirror image, gives $|f(x)-f(y)| \leq f(x-y)$. Combined with part \textbf{(a)} this
    yields
    \[|f(x)-f(y)| \ \leq\ f(x-y) \ \leq\ C_1|x-y|.\]
    So $f$ is $C_1$-Lipschitz with respect to the \emph{Euclidean} distance
    (\cref{def:lipschitz_continuous}), hence continuous (\cref{rem:continuity_hierarchy}). Note
    which distance is which here: the claim is continuity of $f$ as a function on the metric
    space $(\mathbb{R}^n, |\cdot|)$, and part \textbf{(a)} is what makes that meaningful.
  \item The unit sphere $S := \{x : |x| = 1\}$ is closed (preimage of $\{1\}$ under the
    continuous $|\cdot|$) and bounded, hence \textbf{compact} by \cref{thm:heine_borel}. By part
    \textbf{(b)} the function $f$ is continuous on $S$, so it attains a minimum there
    (\cref{item:extreme_value_theorem}), say $c_2 := f(x_0)$ with $|x_0| = 1$. Since $x_0 \neq 0$,
    definiteness gives $c_2 = f(x_0) > 0$.
  \item For $x \neq 0$ the vector $x/|x|$ lies in $S$, so $f(x/|x|) \geq c_2$, and homogeneity
    gives
    \[f(x) = |x|\,f\!\left(\frac{x}{|x|}\right) \ \geq\ c_2\,|x|.\]
    For $x = 0$ both sides vanish. Together with part \textbf{(a)},
    $c_2|x| \leq f(x) \leq C_1|x|$.
  \item Apply parts \textbf{(a)} and \textbf{(d)} to both norms: $c_2|x| \leq f(x) \leq C_1|x|$ and
    $\tilde c_2|x| \leq \tilde f(x) \leq \tilde C_1|x|$, all four constants positive. Then
    \[\tilde f(x) \ \leq\ \tilde C_1|x| \ \leq\ \frac{\tilde C_1}{c_2}\,f(x),
      \qquad\qquad
      \tilde f(x) \ \geq\ \tilde c_2|x| \ \geq\ \frac{\tilde c_2}{C_1}\,f(x),\]
    so $C := \max\big\{\tilde C_1/c_2,\ C_1/\tilde c_2\big\}$ satisfies
    $C^{-1}f \leq \tilde f \leq Cf$. Equivalence of norms is thus an equivalence relation, and
    the Euclidean norm serves as the common reference point.
\end{enumerate}

\begin{remark}[Where finite dimension is indispensable]
\label{rem:norm_equivalence_needs_finite_dimension}
The result is false in infinite dimensions, and the proof shows exactly which step breaks: part
\textbf{(a)} expands $x$ in a \emph{finite} basis and part \textbf{(c)} uses compactness of the
unit sphere. In an
infinite-di\-men\-sional normed space the closed unit ball is never compact, and one can write
down inequivalent norms. Take for instance $\lVert\cdot\rVert_\infty$ and
$\int_0^1|\cdot|$ on $C^0([0,1])$, where a tall thin spike has supremum norm $1$ and integral
norm arbitrarily small. Every appearance of \qt{all norms on $\mathbb{R}^n$ are equivalent} later
in the course silently uses $n < \infty$.
\end{remark}
\end{exercisesolution}

\begin{exercisesolution}[Proof of \cref{ex:polynomials_infinite_dimensional}]
\label{sol:polynomials_infinite_dimensional}
% Generator: Claude Sonnet 5 (High)
The monomials $\mathcal{B} := \{1,x,x^2,x^3,\dots\}$ form a basis: every polynomial is by
definition a \emph{finite} linear combination $\sum_{k=0}^n a_kx^k$ of them, so $\mathcal{B}$
spans $\mathbb{R}[x]$; and $\sum_{k=0}^n a_kx^k = 0$ as a polynomial forces every coefficient
$a_k = 0$ (a nonzero polynomial of degree $n$ has at most $n$ roots, so it cannot vanish
identically on all of $\mathbb{R}$), so $\mathcal{B}$ is linearly independent.

To see $\mathbb{R}[x]$ is infinite-dimensional, suppose for contradiction it had finite
dimension $d$. Then $1,x,\dots,x^d$, a set of $d+1$ vectors of $\mathbb{R}[x]$, would have
to be linearly dependent, since any $d+1$ vectors in a $d$-dimensional space are. But they are
linearly independent by the argument above (restricted to $n \leq d$): a contradiction. So no
finite basis exists, and the (necessarily infinite) $\mathcal{B}$ above is one.
\end{exercisesolution}

\begin{exercisesolution}[Proof of \cref{ex:hilbert_schmidt_submultiplicative}]
\label{sol:hilbert_schmidt_submultiplicative}
% Generator: Claude Opus 5 (High)
Write $\Psi^i \in \mathbb{R}^n$ for the $i$-th \emph{row} of $\Psi$ and
$\Phi_j \in \mathbb{R}^n$ for the $j$-th \emph{column} of $\Phi$, so that by the definition of
matrix multiplication
\[(\Psi\Phi)_{ij} = \langle \Psi^i,\ \Phi_j\rangle.\]
Cauchy--Schwarz (\cref{thm:cauchy_schwarz}) applied to this pair of vectors of $\mathbb{R}^n$
gives $\big|(\Psi\Phi)_{ij}\big|^2 \leq \lVert\Psi^i\rVert^2\,\lVert\Phi_j\rVert^2$. Summing over
all entries and factorising the resulting double sum,
\[\lVert\Psi\Phi\rVert^2
  = \sum_{i=1}^m\sum_{j=1}^d \big|(\Psi\Phi)_{ij}\big|^2
  \ \leq\ \sum_{i=1}^m\sum_{j=1}^d \lVert\Psi^i\rVert^2\lVert\Phi_j\rVert^2
  = \left(\sum_{i=1}^m\lVert\Psi^i\rVert^2\right)\left(\sum_{j=1}^d\lVert\Phi_j\rVert^2\right)
  = \lVert\Psi\rVert^2\,\lVert\Phi\rVert^2,\]
using that the Hilbert--Schmidt norm squared is the sum of the squares of all entries, read
row by row for $\Psi$ and column by column for $\Phi$. Taking square roots gives
$\lVert\Psi\Phi\rVert \leq \lVert\Psi\rVert\,\lVert\Phi\rVert$.

% Reclassified from ainote to remark: it introduces terminology with \newterm and states a
% property of the norm, so it is mathematics rather than commentary on the transcription.
\begin{remark}[Submultiplicativity, and what it later buys]
The Hilbert--Schmidt norm is the same object as the Frobenius norm met in
\cref{ex:metric_space_examples} part \textbf{(e)} on the previous sheet, where it was shown to be the Euclidean
norm of the
matrix read as a vector of $\mathbb{R}^{n^2}$. The inequality just proved says that this norm is
\newterm{submultiplicative} \germanfor{submultiplikativ}, which is exactly the property that
makes it useful for estimating compositions of linear maps. It is also what will later let
$\lVert Df\rVert$ be handled like an absolute value in the mean value inequality
(\cref{rem:mvt_fails_vector_valued}).
\end{remark}
\end{exercisesolution}

  \item Let $M := \max_i |x_i|$. Then
    \[
    M \leq |x|_p = M\Big(\sum_{i=1}^{n}\Big(\frac{|x_i|}{M}\Big)^p\Big)^{1/p} \leq M\,n^{1/p}.
    \]
    Since $n^{1/p} \to 1$ as $p \to \infty$, the squeeze theorem gives $|x|_\infty = \lim_{p\to\infty}|x|_p = M = \max_i |x_i|$.
  \item Set $u_i := a_i^{p/2}$ and $v_i := a_i^{(p-2)/2}b_i$, so that $u_iv_i = a_i^{p-1}b_i$,
    $u_i^2 = a_i^p$, and $v_i^2 = a_i^{p-2}b_i^2$. Cauchy--Schwarz in $\mathbb{R}^n$ gives
    \[
    \Big(\sum_{i=1}^n a_i^{p-1}b_i\Big)^2 = \Big(\sum_{i=1}^n u_iv_i\Big)^2 \leq \Big(\sum_{i=1}^n u_i^2\Big)\Big(\sum_{i=1}^n v_i^2\Big) = \Big(\sum_{i=1}^n a_i^p\Big)\Big(\sum_{i=1}^n a_i^{p-2}b_i^2\Big).
    \]
  \item Fix $x, y$ with all coordinates strictly positive, let $d := x - y$, and write
    $z(t) := tx+(1-t)y = y + td$, so $z_i(t) > 0$ for all $t \in [0,1]$. Let $g(t) := f(t)^p =
    \sum_i z_i(t)^p$, so $f = g^{1/p}$. Since $z_i'(t) = d_i$,
    \[
    g'(t) = p\sum_i z_i(t)^{p-1}d_i.
    \]
    Differentiating $f = g^{1/p}$ twice and simplifying,
    \[
    f''(t) = (p-1)\,g(t)^{1/p-2}\left[g(t)\sum_i z_i(t)^{p-2}d_i^2 - \Big(\sum_i z_i(t)^{p-1}d_i\Big)^2\right].
    \]
    Applying the inequality of the previous point with $a_i = z_i(t) > 0$ and $b_i = d_i$ (any
    sign is allowed, since the inequality only uses $b_i^2$ on the right and squares the left)
    shows the bracket is $\geq 0$. Since $g(t) > 0$ and $p \geq 1$, we get $f''(t) \geq 0$ for
    $p > 1$ (for $p=1$, $f$ is affine in $t$ and hence convex). Thus $f$ is convex on $[0,1]$.
  \item Convexity of $f$ at $t = \tfrac12$ gives $f(\tfrac12) \leq \tfrac12(f(0)+f(1))$, i.e.
    \[
    \Big|\frac{x+y}{2}\Big|_p \leq \frac{|x|_p + |y|_p}{2} \quad \Longrightarrow \quad |x+y|_p \leq |x|_p + |y|_p
    \]
    for $x, y$ with strictly positive coordinates. For general $x, y \in \mathbb{R}^n$, write
    $\hat x := (|x_1|,\dots,|x_n|)$, $\hat y := (|y_1|,\dots,|y_n|)$; these have non-negative
    coordinates (the strictly positive case above extends to this case by continuity), and
    $|x+y|_p \leq |\hat x + \hat y|_p \leq |\hat x|_p + |\hat y|_p = |x|_p + |y|_p$.
  \item For $p \in (0,1)$, the triangle inequality fails, so $|\cdot|_p$ is not a norm: taking
    $x = e_1$, $y = e_2$ gives $|x|_p = |y|_p = 1$ but $|x+y|_p = 2^{1/p} > 2 = |x|_p+|y|_p$.
\end{enumerate}
\end{exercisesolution}

\begin{exercisesolution}[Solution to \cref{ex:p_means_monotonicity}]
\label{sol:p_means_monotonicity}
% Generator: Claude Opus 5 (High)
Throughout write $S(t) := \sum_{i=1}^n x_i^t$, so that
$\mu_t(x) = \big(S(t)/n\big)^{1/t}$ for $t \neq 0$, and set
\[g(t) := \log \mu_t(x) = \frac{h(t)}{t}, \qquad h(t) := \log\frac{S(t)}{n}.\]
Note $h(0) = \log(n/n) = 0$; this single fact does most of the work below.
\begin{enumerate}[label=\textbf{(\alph*)}]
  \item Let $M := \max_i x_i$ and $m := \min_i x_i$, and let $k$ be the number of indices
    attaining $M$. Factoring out the largest term,
    \[\mu_t(x) = M\left(\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^n\Big(\frac{x_i}{M}\Big)^{t}\right)^{1/t},\]
    and as $t \to +\infty$ the bracket tends to $k/n$, whose $t$-th root tends to $1$. Hence
    $\mu_{+\infty}(x) = M = \max_i x_i$. Replacing $t$ by $-t$ turns the maximum into the
    minimum, so $\mu_{-\infty}(x) = m = \min_i x_i$.

    For $t \to 0$ the exponent and the logarithm both degenerate, which is exactly the
    $\tfrac00$ that $g = h/t$ resolves: $h$ is differentiable with $h(0) = 0$, so
    \[\lim_{t\to0} g(t) = h'(0) = \frac{S'(0)}{S(0)} = \frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^n \log x_i,\]
    using $S'(t) = \sum_i x_i^t\log x_i$ and $S(0) = n$. Exponentiating,
    \[\mu_0(x) = \big(x_1x_2\cdots x_n\big)^{1/n},\]
    the \newterm{geometric mean} \germanfor{geometrisches Mittel}.
  \item The inequality is Jensen applied to $\varphi(s) := s\log s$, which is convex on
    $(0,\infty)$ because $\varphi''(s) = 1/s > 0$. With the uniform weights $\tfrac1n$,
    \[\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^n a_i\log a_i \ \geq\ \varphi\!\left(\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^n a_i\right)
      = \frac{A}{n}\log\frac{A}{n}, \qquad A := a_1 + \dots + a_n.\]
    Multiplying by $n/A > 0$ gives exactly
    \[\sum_{i=1}^n \frac{a_i\log a_i}{a_1+\dots+a_n} \ \geq\ \log\!\left(\frac{a_1+\dots+a_n}{n}\right).\]
  \item \textit{Continuity.} On $\mathbb{R}\setminus\{0\}$, $g = h/t$ is a quotient of smooth
    functions with non-vanishing denominator. At $t = 0$ the value $g(0) := h'(0)$ computed in
    part \textbf{(a)} is precisely the limit, so $g$, and hence also $f = e^{g}$, is continuous
    everywhere.

    \textit{Monotonicity.} Differentiating $g = h/t$ for $t \neq 0$,
    \[t^2 g'(t) = t\,h'(t) - h(t) = \frac{t\,S'(t)}{S(t)} - \log\frac{S(t)}{n}.\]
    Now substitute $a_i := x_i^{\,t}$. Then $\log a_i = t\log x_i$, so
    $t\,S'(t) = \sum_i a_i\log a_i$ and $S(t) = \sum_i a_i$, and the displayed quantity becomes
    \[\sum_{i=1}^n\frac{a_i\log a_i}{a_1+\dots+a_n} \ -\ \log\!\left(\frac{a_1+\dots+a_n}{n}\right)
      \ \geq\ 0\]
    by part \textbf{(b)}. Hence $g' \geq 0$ on $t \neq 0$, and by continuity $g$ is non-decreasing
    on all of
    $\mathbb{R}$; so is $f = e^{g}$.

    This is why part \textbf{(b)} was placed where it is: it is not a separate exercise but the
    derivative computation of part \textbf{(c)} in disguise.
  \item Both inequalities are instances of $\mu_s(x) \leq \mu_t(x)$ for $s \leq t$, i.e.\ of
    part \textbf{(c)}.
    \begin{itemize}
      \item \textbf{Arithmetic--geometric:} $\mu_0(x) \leq \mu_1(x)$ reads
        $(x_1\cdots x_n)^{1/n} \leq \tfrac{1}{n}(x_1+\dots+x_n)$; multiplying by $n$ gives
        $n(x_1x_2\cdots x_n)^{1/n} \leq x_1 + \dots + x_n$.
      \item \textbf{Arithmetic--quadratic:} $\mu_1(x) \leq \mu_2(x)$ reads
        $\tfrac1n\sum_i x_i \leq \big(\tfrac1n\sum_i x_i^2\big)^{1/2}$. Both sides are positive,
        so squaring preserves the inequality:
        $\tfrac{1}{n^2}\big(\sum_i x_i\big)^2 \leq \tfrac1n\sum_i x_i^2$, and multiplying by
        $n^2$ gives $(x_1+\dots+x_n)^2 \leq n(x_1^2+\dots+x_n^2)$.
    \end{itemize}
  \item \textbf{(*) Yes}, and considerably more than $C^1$. Each $x_i^{\,t} = e^{t\log x_i}$ is
    real-analytic in $t$, hence so is $S$, and $S > 0$ everywhere, so $h = \log(S/n)$ is
    real-analytic too. Because $h(0) = 0$, the quotient $h(t)/t$ has a \emph{removable}
    singularity at the origin: writing $h(t) = \sum_{k\geq1}c_kt^k$ gives
    $g(t) = \sum_{k\geq1}c_kt^{k-1}$, a convergent power series near $0$. So $g$, and therefore
    $f = e^{g}$, is real-analytic on all of $\mathbb{R}$, in particular $C^\infty$, and so
    certainly $C^1$.

    The value at the origin is worth recording. From $g(t) = h'(0) + \tfrac12 h''(0)t + O(t^2)$,
    \[f'(0) = f(0)\,g'(0) = \mu_0(x)\cdot\tfrac12 h''(0)
      = \mu_0(x)\cdot\frac12\left(\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^n(\log x_i)^2
        - \Big(\frac1n\sum_{i=1}^n\log x_i\Big)^{\!2}\right),\]
    using $h'' = (S''S - (S')^2)/S^2$ at $t=0$ with $S(0)=n$, $S'(0)=\sum_i\log x_i$ and
    $S''(0)=\sum_i(\log x_i)^2$. The bracket is the variance of the numbers $\log x_i$, so
    $f'(0) \geq 0$, with equality exactly when all $x_i$ agree. In that case $\mu_t(x)$ is that
    common value for every $t$, and $f$ is constant.
\end{enumerate}
\end{exercisesolution}

\begin{remark}[Why parts \textbf{(b)} and \textbf{(c)} are one statement]
Jensen's inequality, used above without being stated, is available as
\cref{prop:jensen_inequality} (\cref{ch:convexity}). The official hint for 4.4.2 routes through
the concavity of $\log$ together with Cauchy--Schwarz. The solution above instead applies Jensen
once, to the convex function $\varphi(s) = s\log s$. Both arrive at the same inequality, but the
second makes visible why the two are really a single statement: substituting
$a_i := x_i^{\,t}$ turns the inequality of part \textbf{(b)} into the sign of $g'(t)$ in part
\textbf{(c)}. That is
worth seeing, because monotonicity of the power means is the whole point of the problem, and the
classical AM--GM inequality falls out of it as the single comparison $\mu_0 \leq \mu_1$.
\end{remark}
