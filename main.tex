% `aside' --- added 2026-08-11 on the user's instruction.
%
% WHAT IT IS FOR. A fact that is genuinely interesting and genuinely not needed: a piece of
% history, a startling special case, a connection to a subject this course does not reach, a
% "while we are here" observation. The test is the one the reader applies anyway: could this be
% deleted without touching a single later argument? If yes, and it is still worth reading, it is
% an aside.
%
% WHY IT IS NOT ONE OF THE EXISTING BOXES. `remark' is for mathematics that bears on the result
% next to it, and burying a digression there dilutes a label the reader relies on. `ainote' is
% editorial -- flagged errors, notation divergences, why a proof is missing -- and an aside is
% none of those. The `didactic-insight' box rejected in style.md was rejected because its content
% was really three kinds of ordinary remark plus history; an aside is exactly the leftover

% Generator: Claude Opus 5 (High) --- part-level framing prose, new content, not tied to any
% tutor source. Rewritten 2026-08-09 in the fuller register style.md now prescribes for \part.
% Correction: Claude Opus 5 (Extra), 2026-08-13 --- the closing sentence claimed Young's inequality
% was "the engine behind the Hölder and Minkowski inequalities in the next part". Both claims were
% false: Hölder is derived from Young in rem:young_to_hoelder, in this very chapter, and Minkowski

\emph{Analysis II} begins where \emph{Analysis I} stopped, and the first thing to settle is how
much of it carries over. The definitions of convergence, of continuity, and of a set being open
survive the move to several variables essentially verbatim. What does not survive is every
statement that quietly used the ordering of $\mathbb{R}$, and each of those has to be rebuilt on
a new hypothesis. This part draws that line, and then rehearses the one skill every later chapter
assumes without ever teaching it: turning a set-builder description into a picture, and a picture
back into equations. The three families of level sets used for that turn out to be the unit balls
of the three standard metrics, so the drawing done here is the metric-space theory of the next
part in disguise. Young's inequality is proved along the way, together with the Hölder inequality
that follows from it in three lines, because both are wanted as soon as $p$-norms appear.

\part{Metric Spaces and Topology}

% Generator: Claude Opus 5 (High) --- part-level framing prose, new content.
% Revised 2026-08-13 (Claude Opus 5, Extra): the earlier version named only completeness and
% compactness and left connectedness, the seventh chapter of the part, unmentioned; it also missed
% that the part's last compactness theorem (Ascoli--Arzela) is about a family of functions.
Very little of one-variable analysis actually used the arithmetic of $\mathbb{R}$. Convergence,
continuity and compactness need only a notion of distance, and this part takes that observation
seriously by developing all of them for an arbitrary metric space. The payoff is not generality
for its own sake. Function spaces such as $C^0([0,1],\mathbb{R})$ are metric spaces too, so the
same theorems apply to a sequence of functions and to a sequence of points alike, and the last
compactness criterion proved here is one for a family of functions rather than for a set of
points. Three properties then carry the remaining five parts. Completeness manufactures limits
nobody can write down, and through the Banach fixed point theorem it becomes the engine of both
the inverse function theorem and the existence theory for differential equations. Compactness
replaces an infinite problem by a finite one, which is what guarantees that a continuous function
on a closed bounded set attains its maximum at all. Connectedness forbids a space from falling
into pieces, and its refinement to simple connectedness decides, five parts later, whether a
curl-free field has a potential.

\part{Differential Calculus}

% Generator: Claude Opus 5 (High) --- part-level framing prose, new content.
% Revised 2026-08-13 (Claude Opus 5, Extra): the earlier version described the part's plan but
% named neither of the two things a reader is most likely to be caught by, the counterexamples
% separating the three candidate derivatives and the failure of the mean value theorem above m=1.
In one variable the derivative is a number, which makes it tempting to expect the several-variable
derivative to be a list of numbers. It is not. The right object is a linear map, the one that best
approximates $f$ near a point, and the partial derivatives are merely its matrix in the standard
basis. Getting that order of ideas right is what turns the chain rule into a statement about
composing linear maps rather than a formula to be memorised, and it is also what accounts for the
counterexamples running through these chapters, in which a function possesses every directional
derivative at a point and is not even continuous there. The rest of the part asks how good the
linear approximation actually is. The mean value theorem measures its error and is the first
result in the course to degrade on the way up, surviving for vector-valued maps only as an
inequality, and Taylor's theorem then sharpens the approximation to any order and supplies every
quantitative estimate the optimization theory of the next part runs on.

% Revised 2026-08-13 (Claude Opus 5, Extra): added the closing observation. Both the fundamental
% theorem of algebra (sec:fundamental_theorem_of_algebra) and the spectral theorem
% (thm:spectral_theorem_optimization) are proved in this part by optimization, and the earlier
% version of this opener gave a reader no reason to expect either.
Differentiability turns the search for extrema into algebra. An interior extremum forces the
gradient to vanish, so the candidates can be found by solving equations, and the sign behaviour of
the Hessian then decides which kind of point each candidate is. Two complications occupy the rest
of the part. When the variables are constrained to a level set, the gradient need not vanish at an
extremum at all, and the method of Lagrange multipliers supplies the condition that replaces it,
together with a hypothesis on the constraint gradients that is easy to drop and expensive to lose.
When the Hessian test comes out inconclusive, or when the question asked is global rather than
local, convexity is the hypothesis that settles it, because for a convex function a critical point
is not a candidate but an answer. It is worth watching what optimization gets used \emph{for}
along the way. Two of the theorems proved in this part are not optimization problems at all: the
fundamental theorem of algebra, which falls out of minimising $\lvert p\rvert$, and the spectral
theorem, which falls out of maximising a quadratic form on the unit sphere.

\part{Inverse and Implicit Functions; Submanifolds}

% Generator: Claude Opus 5 (High) --- part-level framing prose, new content.
% Correction: Claude Opus 5 (Extra), 2026-08-13 --- the earlier version said the two theorems "turn
% out to be equivalent to each other and are proved here from the contraction principle". Neither
% half held. The document derives no equivalence, and only thm:inverse_function_theorem is proved
% (via lem:lipschitz_perturbation_identity and Banach); thm:implicit_function_theorem is stated.
% The geodesics chapter, a quarter of the part, also went unmentioned.
Two questions run through this part. When is a map invertible near a point, and when can an
equation $f(x,y) = 0$ be solved for $y$ as a function of $x$? Neither has a workable global
answer, but both have clean local ones, and both rest on the same linear-algebra hypothesis, that
a Jacobian, or one square block of it, is invertible. The inverse function theorem is proved here
from the contraction principle of Part II, which is the first place that fixed-point machinery
repays what it cost to set up. The deeper use of both theorems is definitional. A level set on
which the differential has full rank is locally a graph, being locally a graph is precisely what
it means to be a submanifold, and a submanifold is a subset of $\mathbb{R}^n$ that looks like
$\mathbb{R}^m$ from close up, which is the object the last two parts integrate over. Once such a
set carries a tangent space at each point one can ask what a straight line becomes on it, and the
part closes with that question, which takes the first genuine calculus-of-variations argument in
the course.

\part{Integration}

% Generator: Claude Opus 5 (High) --- part-level framing prose, new content.
% Revised 2026-08-13 (Claude Opus 5, Extra): the earlier version jumped straight from Jordan
% measure to Fubini, which left the reader unprepared for the two things chapter 19 actually
% spends its length on (the non-measurable sets, and the Lebesgue digression) and for Feynman's
% trick, a full section of chapter 20.
Integration in several variables raises a question that one variable never had to face. Before
asking what $\int_A f$ is, one has to say which sets $A$ possess a volume at all, and Jordan
measure is the answer this course uses. Most of the work in the first chapter goes into the sets
it fails on, and they all fail for one reason, that a Jordan cover may use only finitely many
cubes of a single size. A short digression sketches the Lebesgue theory, which repairs exactly
that defect and is used nowhere afterwards. With the integral defined, the problem becomes
computing one, and three tools do nearly all of it: Fubini reduces a multiple integral to iterated
one-dimensional ones, the change of variables formula trades an awkward domain for a convenient
one and pays $\lvert\det \Jac\phi\rvert$ for the trade, and differentiation under the integral
sign reaches integrals possessing no elementary antiderivative at all. The final chapter leaves
flat regions behind altogether. The Gram determinant measures how much a parametrization stretches
length, area or volume, and with it one can integrate over a curve or a surface sitting inside
$\mathbb{R}^n$ rather than over a piece of $\mathbb{R}^n$ itself.

\part{Vector Calculus and Differential Forms}

% Generator: Claude Opus 5 (High) --- part-level framing prose, new content.
% Revised 2026-08-13 (Claude Opus 5, Extra): the closing list said "compactness to keep the
% integrals finite", which names no mechanism the part actually uses; what it uses is compactly
% supported partitions of unity (prop:partition_of_unity_finite). Simple connectedness, which is
% what the Poincare lemma turns on, was missing from the list altogether.
The classical integral theorems of vector calculus look at first like three unrelated results.
Green's theorem converts a line integral into a double integral, the divergence theorem converts a
flux through a closed surface into a triple integral, and the classical theorem of Stokes converts
a circulation into a flux. This part proves all three, and then explains why they are one theorem.
The apparatus that achieves this consists of differential forms and the exterior derivative $d$,
a single operator absorbing $\grad$, $\curl$ and $\divg$ at once, which satisfies the one identity
$d \circ d = 0$ in place of the two unrelated-looking ones $\curl\grad = 0$ and $\divg\curl = 0$.
In that language each of the statements above reads $\int_M d\omega = \int_{\partial M}\omega$,
and the classical versions are what it becomes when $M$ is a plane region, a solid, or a surface.
Everything built earlier is spent here. Submanifolds supply what one integrates over, orientation
fixes the sign, compactly supported partitions of unity patch local computations into global ones,
and simple connectedness decides when a field whose curl vanishes is a gradient after all.
