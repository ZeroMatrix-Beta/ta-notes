\begin{exercisesolution}[Solution to \cref{ex:8.2}]
% Generator: Claude Opus 5 (High)
The whole problem turns on one distinction: \textbf{Jordan} outer measure is an infimum over
\emph{finite} covers, \textbf{Lebesgue} outer measure over \emph{countable} ones. Parts
\textbf{(a)}--\textbf{(c)} are that distinction three times; parts \textbf{(d)} and \textbf{(e)}
are the difference between a measure and a merely sub-additive outer measure.
\begin{enumerate}[label=\textbf{(\alph*)}]
  \item \textbf{False.} Take $X := \mathbb{Q}\cap[0,1]$: bounded and countable, yet
    $\mu_{out}(X) = 1$ by part \textbf{(c)}, since $X$ is dense in $[0,1]$. Countability buys
    nothing here, since only finitely many intervals are allowed at a time, and finitely many
    intervals covering a dense set must cover the whole of $[0,1]$.
  \item \textbf{True}, and this is exactly where countable covers do buy something. Enumerate
    $X = \{x_1,x_2,\dots\}$ and, given $\varepsilon > 0$, cover $x_k$ by an interval of length
    $\varepsilon 2^{-k}$. The total length is $\sum_{k\geq1}\varepsilon2^{-k} = \varepsilon$, and
    $\varepsilon$ was arbitrary, so the Lebesgue outer measure is $0$.
  \item \textbf{True.} Monotonicity gives $\mu_{out}(D) \leq \mu_{out}([0,1]) = 1$, so only the
    lower bound needs an argument. Let $I_1,\dots,I_N$ be finitely many intervals covering $D$.
    Enlarging each to its closure changes no length, so we may assume they are closed; then
    $\bigcup_{k=1}^N I_k$ is \emph{closed}, which is where finiteness enters, and contains
    $D$, hence contains $\overline D = [0,1]$. Therefore
    $\sum_{k=1}^N |I_k| \geq \mu([0,1]) = 1$. Taking the infimum over such covers,
    $\mu_{out}(D) \geq 1$.
  \item \textbf{True.} Suppose $X \cap Y = \emptyset$. Jordan measure is additive on disjoint
    Jordan measurable sets, so
    \[\mu(X \cup Y) = \mu(X) + \mu(Y) > \tfrac12 + \tfrac12 = 1.\]
    But $X \cup Y \subseteq [0,1]$, so monotonicity forces $\mu(X\cup Y) \leq 1$, a
    contradiction. Hence $X \cap Y \neq \emptyset$.
  \item \textbf{False}, and this is the official hint's point. Take
    \[X := \mathbb{Q}\cap[0,1], \qquad Y := (\mathbb{R}\setminus\mathbb{Q})\cap[0,1].\]
    Both are dense in $[0,1]$, so part \textbf{(c)} gives $\mu_{out}(X) = \mu_{out}(Y) = 1 >
    \tfrac12$; yet $X \cap Y = \emptyset$ by construction. The argument of part \textbf{(d)}
    breaks at its first step:
    $\mu_{out}$ is only \emph{sub}additive, so $\mu_{out}(X\cup Y) \leq \mu_{out}(X)+\mu_{out}(Y)$,
    an inequality pointing the wrong way to produce a contradiction. Neither set is Jordan
    measurable, which is precisely why part \textbf{(d)} does not apply.
\end{enumerate}

\begin{ainote}
Parts \textbf{(a)} and \textbf{(e)} are the same counterexample used twice, and part \textbf{(c)}
is the lemma both rest on. It is
worth extracting what makes it work: a \emph{finite} union of closed sets is closed, so a finite
cover of $D$ automatically covers $\overline D$. Every difference between Jordan and Lebesgue
theory visible on this sheet traces back to that one sentence.
\end{ainote}
\end{exercisesolution}

\begin{exercisesolution}[Solution to \cref{ex:8.4}]
% Generator: Claude Opus 5 (High)
\textbf{The correct answer is 4: $f$ locally Lipschitz continuous and $m \geq n$.} Both
hypotheses are needed, and dropping either one admits a counterexample.

\textbf{Why 1 and 2 fail:} uniform continuity is not enough. Let $c : [0,1] \to [0,1]$ be the
Cantor function, which is continuous on a compact interval and hence uniformly continuous, and
let $N \subset [0,1]$ be the Cantor set. Then $N$ is Jordan-null (it is covered by $2^k$
intervals of total length $(2/3)^k \to 0$), while $c(N) = [0,1]$ is not null. Here $n = m = 1$, so
this single example kills option 1 \emph{and} option 2. The extra hypothesis $m \geq n$ does
nothing to rescue mere continuity. Intuitively, a uniformly continuous map has no bound on how
much it may stretch, only on how abruptly.

\textbf{Why 3 fails:} $m \geq n$ is not decoration. Let $f : \mathbb{R}^2 \to \mathbb{R}$,
$f(x,y) := y$, which is Lipschitz with constant $1$, and let
$N := \{0\}\times[0,1]$, a segment and so Jordan-null in $\mathbb{R}^2$. Then $f(N) = [0,1]$,
which is not null in $\mathbb{R}^1$. Here $m = 1 < 2 = n$: a projection loses a dimension, and
what was negligible in the source stops being negligible in the smaller target.

\textbf{Why 4 holds.} Let $N$ be Jordan-null. Being Jordan-null it is bounded, and we may work on
a compact neighbourhood $K \subset U$ of $\overline N$ on which $f$ is Lipschitz with some
constant $L$ (local Lipschitz continuity plus compactness gives a single constant). Fix
$\varepsilon > 0$ and cover $N$ by finitely many cubes $Q_1,\dots,Q_M \subset K$ of sides
$s_i \leq 1$ with $\sum_i s_i^{\,n} < \varepsilon$. Each $Q_i$ has diameter $s_i\sqrt n$, so
$f(Q_i \cap N)$ has diameter at most $L s_i\sqrt n$ and therefore fits inside a cube of side
$L s_i \sqrt n$ in $\mathbb{R}^m$, of volume $(L\sqrt n)^m s_i^{\,m}$. Since $s_i \leq 1$ and
$m \geq n$ we have $s_i^{\,m} \leq s_i^{\,n}$, so the images are covered with total volume at
most
\[(L\sqrt n)^m \sum_{i=1}^M s_i^{\,n} \;<\; (L\sqrt n)^m\,\varepsilon.\]
As $\varepsilon$ was arbitrary, $f(N)$ is null.

\begin{ainote}
The proof shows exactly where each hypothesis is spent. \emph{Lipschitz} is what converts a bound
on the side of a cube into a bound on the diameter of its image. Continuity alone gives no
such conversion. \emph{$m \geq n$} is what makes $s_i^{\,m} \leq s_i^{\,n}$, i.e.\ what stops the
volume from being measured with too few powers of $s_i$. The two counterexamples attack precisely
these two steps.
\end{ainote}
\end{exercisesolution}

\begin{exercisesolution}[Solution to \cref{ex:ai_gaussian_two_exhaustions}]
% Generator: Claude Opus 5 (High)
\begin{enumerate}[label=\textbf{(\alph*)}]
  \item Both families are nested and consist of compact sets whose boundaries are a circle
    and the four sides of a square respectively, each a Jordan null set, so both are Jordan
    measurable by \cref{thm:jordan_measurability_boundary_criterion}. For the covering condition,
    $\operatorname{int}(D_\ell) = B_\ell(0)$ and $\operatorname{int}(Q_\ell) = (-\ell,\ell)^2$,
    and every point of $\mathbb{R}^2$ lies in both once $\ell$ exceeds its distance from the
    origin. Hence $\bigcup_\ell\operatorname{int}(D_\ell) =
    \bigcup_\ell\operatorname{int}(Q_\ell) = \mathbb{R}^2$, and
    \cref{lem:improper_integral_as_limit} applies to each.
  \item In polar coordinates, using \cref{thm:change_of_variables} with Jacobian $r$,
    \[\int_{D_\ell} e^{-(x^2+y^2)}\,dx\,dy
      = \int_0^{2\pi}\!\!\int_0^{\ell} e^{-r^2}\,r\,dr\,d\varphi
      = 2\pi\left[-\frac{e^{-r^2}}{2}\right]_0^{\ell} = \pi\big(1 - e^{-\ell^2}\big)
      \ \xrightarrow[\ell\to\infty]{}\ \pi.\]
  \item On a square the integrand factorises, so \cref{thm:fubini} splits it:
    \[\int_{Q_\ell} e^{-(x^2+y^2)}\,dx\,dy
      = \left(\int_{-\ell}^{\ell} e^{-x^2}dx\right)\left(\int_{-\ell}^{\ell} e^{-y^2}dy\right)
      = \left(\int_{-\ell}^{\ell} e^{-x^2}dx\right)^{\!2}
      \ \xrightarrow[\ell\to\infty]{}\ I^2.\]
  \item Both limits compute the same number $\int_{\mathbb{R}^2}f$, by
    \cref{lem:improper_integral_as_limit}, so $I^2 = \pi$ and $I = \sqrt\pi$, the positive root
    since the integrand is positive.
\end{enumerate}

The step that collapses without $f \geq 0$ is the identification in \textbf{(d)}. Non-negativity
is what makes $\ell \mapsto \int_{K_\ell}f$ monotone, and monotonicity is what forces every
exhaustion to the same limit. For a sign-changing integrand the disks and the squares are free to
disagree, and \cref{rem:improper_needs_nonnegativity} gives the standard example of an integral
whose value genuinely depends on how the domain is exhausted.

\begin{remark}[This is the argument \cref{ex:gaussian_integral_polar} was already making]
\label{rem:gaussian_two_exhaustions_retro}
The classical derivation of $\int_{\mathbb{R}}e^{-x^2}dx = \sqrt\pi$ squares the integral, reads
the product as an integral over $\mathbb{R}^2$, and switches to polar coordinates. Written out,
that is precisely the comparison above: the squaring step is the square exhaustion and the polar
step is the disk exhaustion, and the derivation works because the two agree. What
\cref{lem:improper_integral_as_limit} adds is the permission to move between them, which the
classical presentation uses without mentioning.
\end{remark}
\end{exercisesolution}

\begin{exercisesolution}[Proof of \cref{ex:examHS24_TF_improper}]
% Generator: Gemini 3.1 Pro (High)
Both improper integrals can be evaluated by switching to polar coordinates, where $f(r\cos\theta, r\sin\theta) = r^{-2\alpha}$ and the area element is $r\,dr\,d\theta$.
\begin{enumerate}[label=\textbf{(\alph*)}]
  \item \textbf{False:} Over the region $A = \{r \ge 1\}$, the integral becomes $2\pi \int_1^\infty r^{1-2\alpha} \, dr$. The integral of $r^p$ on $[1,\infty)$ converges if and only if $p < -1$. Thus, we need $1-2\alpha < -1$, which simplifies to $2\alpha > 2$, or $\alpha > 1$. The statement claims it converges if and only if $\alpha \le 1$, which is the exact opposite.
  \item \textbf{False:} Over the region $B = \{r \le 1\}$, the integral becomes $2\pi \int_0^1 r^{1-2\alpha} \, dr$. The integral of $r^p$ on $(0,1]$ converges if and only if $p > -1$. This requires $1-2\alpha > -1$, meaning $\alpha < 1$. For $\alpha = 10/9 > 1$, the integral diverges.
  \item \textbf{False:} As established above, for $\alpha < 1$, the integral evaluates to
  \[ \int_B f = 2\pi \left[ \frac{r^{2-2\alpha}}{2-2\alpha} \right]_0^1 = \frac{2\pi}{2-2\alpha} = \frac{\pi}{1-\alpha}. \]
  Taking the limit as $\alpha \to 0^+$, we get $\lim_{\alpha \to 0^+} \frac{\pi}{1-\alpha} = \pi$. The statement incorrectly claims the limit is $\frac{\pi^2}{6}$.
\end{enumerate}
\end{exercisesolution}

\begin{exercisesolution}[Solution of \cref{ex:improper_cos_over_r4}]
% Generator: Claude Opus 5 (High)
The integrand is undefined at the origin, so $\lvert f\rvert$ is a continuous non-negative function
on the open set $\mathbb{R}^2\setminus\{(0,0)\}$ and \cref{def:improper_integral} applies there.
The two boundary circles are Jordan null sets, so it makes no difference whether $A$ and $B$ are
read as closed or open. Throughout, write $r := \sqrt{x^2+y^2}$, so that $\lvert f\rvert =
\lvert\cos(xy)\rvert/r^4$, and recall from \cref{rem:polar_as_shells} that a radial bound
integrates as $2\pi\int g(r)\,r\,dr$.

Nothing here can be computed exactly, because $\cos(xy)$ has no antiderivative in polar
coordinates. Both verdicts come from bounding that numerator, once from above and once from below.
\begin{enumerate}[label=\textbf{(\alph*)}]
  \item \textbf{True.} On all of $\mathbb{R}^2$, $\lvert\cos(xy)\rvert \leq 1$, so on $A$
    \[\int_A \lvert f\rvert \;\leq\; \int_A \frac{1}{r^4}
      \;=\; 2\pi\int_1^\infty \frac{1}{r^4}\,r\,dr
      \;=\; 2\pi\int_1^\infty r^{-3}\,dr
      \;=\; 2\pi\left[\frac{-1}{2r^2}\right]_1^\infty = \pi \;<\; \infty .\]
  \item \textbf{False.} Here the numerator must be bounded \emph{below}, and on $B$ it is bounded
    below by a positive constant. Indeed $\lvert xy\rvert \leq \tfrac12(x^2+y^2) \leq \tfrac12$ for
    $(x,y) \in B$ by the inequality of arithmetic and geometric means, and $\cos$ is positive and
    decreasing on $[0,\tfrac12] \subseteq [0,\pi/2)$, so $\cos(xy) \geq \cos\tfrac12 > 0$
    throughout $B$. Consequently
    \[\int_B \lvert f\rvert \;\geq\; \cos\tfrac12 \int_B \frac{1}{r^4}
      \;=\; 2\pi\cos\tfrac12 \int_0^1 r^{-3}\,dr \;=\; +\infty,\]
    since $\int_0^1 r^p\,dr$ diverges for $p \leq -1$ and here $p = -3$.
  \item \textbf{False.} $B \setminus\{(0,0)\}$ is contained in $\mathbb{R}^2\setminus\{(0,0)\}$, so
    monotonicity of the improper integral in the domain gives
    $\int_{\mathbb{R}^2}\lvert f\rvert \geq \int_B \lvert f\rvert = +\infty$ by \textbf{(b)}. One
    divergent piece is enough; the convergence found in \textbf{(a)} cannot repair it.
\end{enumerate}
\begin{remark}[The singularity is at the origin, not at infinity]
Set against \cref{ex:examHS24_TF_improper}, which asks the same two questions for
$r^{-2\alpha}$, this block is the case $\alpha = 2$: the radial integrand is $r^{-4}\cdot r =
r^{-3}$, which converges at infinity and diverges at $0$, and no exponent does both. That is the
general picture in the plane. On $A$ convergence needs $r^{-2\alpha+1}$ integrable at infinity,
that is $\alpha > 1$; on $B$ it needs the same expression integrable at $0$, that is $\alpha < 1$.
The two conditions are complementary, so $\int_{\mathbb{R}^2}r^{-2\alpha}$ diverges for every
$\alpha$, and the bounded oscillating factor $\cos(xy)$ changes none of it.
\end{remark}
\end{exercisesolution}

\begin{exercisesolution}[\cref{ex:lebesgue_null_sets_properties}]
\begin{enumerate}[label=\textbf{(\alph*)}]
  \item \textbf{No.} Pick $p \in U$ and a closed cube $K \subseteq U$ centred at $p$, of side
    length $\ell > 0$; this exists because $U$ is open. Suppose $U$ were Lebesgue null and cover it
    by countably many cubes $Q_1,Q_2,\dots$ with $\sum_i \mu(Q_i) < \varepsilon$. Enlarge each
    $Q_i$ to an \emph{open} cube $Q_i'\supseteq Q_i$ concentric with it and with
    $\mu(Q_i') \le 2\mu(Q_i)$. The $Q_i'$ form an open cover of the compact set $K$, so finitely
    many of them, say $Q_{i_1}',\dots,Q_{i_r}'$, already cover $K$. Subadditivity of the volume on
    finite unions then gives
\[\ell^n = \mu(K) \le \sum_{j=1}^r \mu(Q_{i_j}') \le 2\sum_{i} \mu(Q_i) < 2\varepsilon.\]
    Choosing $\varepsilon := \ell^n/2$ makes this read $\ell^n < \ell^n$, a contradiction. So $U$
    is not Lebesgue null.

  \item \textbf{No.} The set $E := \{1/k \mid k \in \mathbb{N}_{\ge1}\} \subseteq \mathbb{R}$ is
    bounded and countable, hence Lebesgue null by \textbf{(c)} below applied to its one-point
    subsets. It is not closed: $1/k \to 0$ and $0 \notin E$.

  \item Let $E = \bigcup_{k\ge1} E_k$ with each $E_k$ Lebesgue null, and let $\varepsilon > 0$.
    Applying the definition to $E_k$ with the tolerance $\varepsilon\,2^{-k}$ produces countably
    many cubes $\{Q_{k,j}\}_{j\ge1}$ covering $E_k$ with
    $\sum_{j}\mu(Q_{k,j}) < \varepsilon\,2^{-k}$. The doubly indexed family
    $\{Q_{k,j}\}_{k,j\ge1}$ is a countable union of countable families, hence countable; it covers
    $E$; and its total volume is
\[\sum_{k\ge1}\sum_{j\ge1}\mu(Q_{k,j}) < \sum_{k\ge1}\varepsilon\,2^{-k} = \varepsilon,\]
    all terms being non-negative, so the order of summation is immaterial. Therefore $E$ is
    Lebesgue null.

  \item Let $\varepsilon > 0$. Since $f$ is Riemann integrable it is bounded, and there is a
    partition $0 = t_0 < t_1 < \dots < t_N = 1$ whose upper and lower sums differ by less than
    $\varepsilon/2$:
\[\sum_{i=1}^N (M_i - m_i)\,\Delta t_i < \frac{\varepsilon}{2}, \qquad
  M_i := \sup_{[t_{i-1},t_i]} f, \quad m_i := \inf_{[t_{i-1},t_i]} f, \quad
  \Delta t_i := t_i - t_{i-1}.\]
    For $x \in [t_{i-1},t_i]$ the point $(x,f(x))$ lies in the rectangle
    $R_i := [t_{i-1},t_i]\times[m_i-\delta,\,M_i+\delta]$, where $\delta > 0$ is chosen below.
    Hence $\Gamma_f \subseteq \bigcup_{i=1}^N R_i$ and
\[\sum_{i=1}^N \mu(R_i) = \sum_{i=1}^N (M_i - m_i + 2\delta)\,\Delta t_i
  < \frac{\varepsilon}{2} + 2\delta,\]
    using $\sum_i \Delta t_i = 1$. Taking $\delta := \varepsilon/8$ makes the total less than
    $\varepsilon$. Since $\varepsilon$ was arbitrary and the cover is finite, $\Gamma_f$ is in fact
    a \emph{Jordan} null set, and a Jordan null set is Lebesgue null.
\end{enumerate}
\begin{remark}[Each part is one hypothesis doing one job]
Part \textbf{(a)} is where the enlargement trick belongs. A countable cover cannot be summed
against a volume directly, because volume is only finitely additive here; compactness converts the
countable cover into a finite one, and the factor $2$ is the price of making the cubes open first.
This is the same manoeuvre as in the proof of \cref{lem:compact_jordan_vs_lebesgue_null}, and it is
the standard way to get from Lebesgue-style covers back to Jordan-style ones.

Part \textbf{(d)} answers more than it was asked: the graph is Jordan null, not merely Lebesgue
null, because the cover produced is finite. The integrability of $f$ is exactly what supplies that.
Boundedness alone would not do, since there are bounded $f : [0,1]\to[0,1]$ whose graph is dense in
the unit square, and a dense subset of the square has outer measure $1$. Recall why: a finite cover
by closed rectangles also covers the closure.

Read the four parts together and they say that null sets are large enough to be closed under
countable unions, and small enough never to contain a ball. That pair is what makes \qt{negligible}
a usable notion, and \textbf{(c)} is the half this document uses most often without comment.
\end{remark}
\end{exercisesolution}

\begin{exercisesolution}[Proof of \cref{ex:vanishing_integral_forces_zero_ae}]
% Generator: Claude Opus 5 (High) --- the statement is the examiner's; the proof and the remark
% are written for these notes. Probeprfg3_Lsg.pdf gives the same level-set argument.
The set where the conclusion fails is $\{f > 0\}$, and the whole proof is the observation that it
is a countable union of sets on which $f$ is bounded below. For $t > 0$ put
\[A_t := \{x \in Q \mid f(x) \geq t\}, \qquad\text{so that}\qquad
  \{f > 0\} = \bigcup_{k=1}^\infty A_{1/k}.\]
By \cref{ex:lebesgue_null_sets_properties}\textbf{(c)} it therefore suffices to show that each
$A_t$ is null.

Fix $t > 0$ and $\varepsilon > 0$. Since $f$ is Riemann integrable with integral $0$, there is a
partition $P$ of $Q$ into boxes whose upper sum satisfies $U(f,P) < \varepsilon t$. Let
$\mathcal{R}$ be the collection of boxes of $P$ that meet $A_t$. On any $R \in \mathcal{R}$ there
is a point where $f \geq t$, so $\sup_R f \geq t$; and $f \geq 0$ makes every term of the upper
sum non-negative, so discarding the boxes outside $\mathcal{R}$ only decreases it. Hence
\[t\sum_{R \in \mathcal{R}} \vol_n(R)
  \ \leq\ \sum_{R \in \mathcal{R}} \Big(\sup_R f\Big)\vol_n(R)
  \ \leq\ U(f,P) \ <\ \varepsilon t ,\]
and dividing by $t > 0$ gives $\sum_{R\in\mathcal{R}}\vol_n(R) < \varepsilon$. The boxes of
$\mathcal{R}$ cover $A_t$ by construction, so $A_t$ admits a \emph{finite} cover by boxes of
total volume less than $\varepsilon$. As $\varepsilon$ was arbitrary, $A_t$ is null.

\begin{remark}[Each level set is Jordan null; only the union needs Lebesgue]
The covers produced above are finite, so the argument proves more than was asked: every
$A_t$ with $t>0$ is \emph{Jordan} null, not merely Lebesgue null
(\cref{rem:lebesgue_vs_jordan_null}). The final set $\{f > 0\}$ need not be, because Jordan
content is not countably additive and a countable union of Jordan null sets can fail to be Jordan
null --- $\mathbb{Q}\cap[0,1]$ is the standing example, a countable union of points whose Jordan
outer content is $1$. So the countable union in the first display is exactly the step that forces
the weaker notion, and it is the reason the conclusion is stated \qt{almost everywhere} rather
than on the complement of a Jordan null set.

Note also where the hypotheses are used, since two of them are doing quite different jobs.
Non-negativity is what lets the upper sum be truncated to $\mathcal{R}$; without it the discarded
boxes could contribute an arbitrarily negative amount and the estimate collapses. Riemann
integrability is what makes an upper sum small at all. And the conclusion really cannot be
improved to $f \equiv 0$: the function on $[0,1]$ that is $1$ at each of finitely many points and
$0$ elsewhere is Riemann integrable with integral $0$, and is not identically zero. For
\emph{continuous} $f$ the stronger conclusion does hold, and needs no null sets --- if
$f(p) > 0$, continuity gives a whole box on which $f > f(p)/2$, forcing a positive integral.
\end{remark}
\end{exercisesolution}

\begin{exercisesolution}[\cref{ex:ai_lebesgue_null_countable}]
\begin{enumerate}[label=\textbf{(\alph*)}]
  \item Let $p \in \mathbb{R}^n$ and $\varepsilon > 0$. The single cube of side length
    $\delta := (\varepsilon/2)^{1/n}$ centred at $p$ covers $\{p\}$ and has volume
    $\delta^n = \varepsilon/2 < \varepsilon$. A finite cover is in particular a countable one, so
    $\lambda^*(\{p\}) = 0$.

  \item Let $E = \{p_1,p_2,\dots\}$ be countable and let $\varepsilon > 0$. Cover $p_k$ by a cube
    $Q_k$ of volume $\varepsilon\,2^{-k-1}$, as in \textbf{(a)}. Then $E \subseteq
    \bigcup_k Q_k$ and
    \[\sum_{k\ge1}\vol(Q_k) = \varepsilon\sum_{k\ge1}2^{-k-1} = \frac{\varepsilon}{2} < \varepsilon.\]
    Hence $\lambda^*(E) = 0$. Since $\mathbb{Q}^n$ is a finite product of countable sets it is
    countable, so it is Lebesgue null.

  \item Suppose $E$ is Lebesgue null and had an interior point $p$. Then $E$ contains a cube $K$ of
    some side length $\ell > 0$, and $\lambda^*$ is monotone, so
    $\lambda^*(E) \ge \lambda^*(K) = \ell^n > 0$, contradicting $\lambda^*(E) = 0$. Hence
    $\operatorname{int}(E) = \emptyset$.
\end{enumerate}
\begin{remark}
Part \textbf{(b)} is the whole difference between the two theories in three lines. The geometric
series is what lets infinitely many cubes have finite total volume, and it is available only
because the cubes are allowed different sizes, which is exactly the point of
\cref{rem:jordan_vs_lebesgue_sizes}. Part \textbf{(c)} then says a null set is small in the
topological sense too, though the converse fails badly: the Cantor set
(\cref{ex:cantor_set_jordan_null}) is null and uncountable, and there are nowhere dense sets of
positive measure.
\end{remark}
\end{exercisesolution}

\begin{exercisesolution}[\cref{ex:ai_lebesgue_three_valued}]
\begin{enumerate}[label=\textbf{(\alph*)}]
  \item Split $[0,1]$ into the three sets on which $f$ is constant:
    \[E_1 := \mathbb{Q}\cap[0,1], \qquad
      E_2 := [0,\tfrac12)\setminus\mathbb{Q}, \qquad
      E_3 := [\tfrac12,1]\setminus\mathbb{Q}.\]
    These are disjoint, measurable and cover $[0,1]$, and $f = 1\cdot\chi_{E_1} + 2\cdot\chi_{E_2}
    + 3\cdot\chi_{E_3}$ is a simple function. By
    \cref{ex:ai_lebesgue_null_countable}\textbf{(b)}, $\lambda(E_1) = 0$, so removing it from
    $[0,\tfrac12)$ and $[\tfrac12,1]$ changes nothing: $\lambda(E_2) = \lambda(E_3) = \tfrac12$.
    Therefore
    \[\int_{[0,1]} f\,d\lambda = 1\cdot 0 + 2\cdot\tfrac12 + 3\cdot\tfrac12 = \frac52.\]

  \item $f$ is continuous at \emph{no} point of $[0,1]$. Fix $x$ and any $\delta > 0$. The interval
    $(x-\delta,x+\delta)\cap[0,1]$ contains both rational and irrational points, since each set is
    dense, so $f$ takes the value $1$ there and also a value in $\{2,3\}$. The oscillation of $f$
    on every neighbourhood of $x$ is therefore at least $1$, and $f$ is discontinuous at $x$.

    The discontinuity set is thus all of $[0,1]$, which has measure $1$ and is not Lebesgue null.
    By \cref{thm:lebesgue_criterion_riemann}\textbf{(a)}, $f$ is not Riemann integrable.
\end{enumerate}
\begin{remark}
The number $5/2$ is what one gets by ignoring the rationals entirely, and that is the honest
summary of what the Lebesgue integral does here: it reads $f$ as the step function
$2\cdot\chi_{[0,\frac12)} + 3\cdot\chi_{[\frac12,1]}$, which it is, away from a null set. The
Riemann theory cannot perform that identification, because its upper sums must take the supremum
over whole intervals and every interval meets $\mathbb{Q}$.
\end{remark}
\end{exercisesolution}

\begin{exercisesolution}[\cref{ex:ai_dominated_beats_uniform}]
\begin{enumerate}[label=\textbf{(\alph*)}]
  \item For $0 \le x < 1$ we have $x^k \to 0$, and $1^k = 1$ for every $k$. So the pointwise limit
    is $f = \chi_{\{1\}}$. The convergence is not uniform: taking $x_k := 2^{-1/k} \in (0,1)$ gives
    $f_k(x_k) = \tfrac12$, so
    $\sup_{x\in[0,1]}\lvert f_k(x)-f(x)\rvert \ge \tfrac12$ for every $k$.

  \item Every $f_k$ satisfies $0 \le f_k \le 1$ on $[0,1]$, and the constant $g \equiv 1$ is
    integrable there, with $\int_{[0,1]} g\,d\lambda = 1 < \infty$. Dominated convergence applies
    and gives
    \[\lim_{k\to\infty}\int_{[0,1]} f_k \,d\lambda = \int_{[0,1]} \chi_{\{1\}}\,d\lambda
      = \lambda(\{1\}) = 0.\]

  \item Directly, $\int_0^1 x^k\,dx = \frac{1}{k+1} \to 0$, in agreement.
\end{enumerate}
\begin{remark}
The point is the contrast in hypotheses, not the value. The limit here is a function this course
would rather not integrate at all, and the uniform-convergence theorem does not apply, yet a single
constant bound settles the exchange. The dominating function is almost always this easy to find,
which is why dominated convergence, and not any new set function, is what practitioners actually
use the Lebesgue theory for.
\end{remark}
\end{exercisesolution}

\begin{exercisesolution}[\cref{ex:ai_improper_not_lebesgue}]
\begin{enumerate}[label=\textbf{(\alph*)}]
  \item $f$ is constant on each $[k,k+1)$, an interval of length $1$, so for integer $N \ge 1$
    \[\int_1^N f(x)\,dx = \sum_{k=1}^{N-1} \frac{(-1)^{k+1}}{k}.\]
    These are the partial sums of the alternating harmonic series, which converges to $\log 2$.
    For non-integer upper limits the integral differs from the nearest such partial sum by at most
    $1/\lfloor R\rfloor \to 0$, so the improper integral converges with
    $\int_1^\infty f(x)\,dx = \log 2$.

  \item $\lvert f\rvert = 1/k$ on $[k,k+1)$, hence
    \[\int_1^N \lvert f(x)\rvert\,dx = \sum_{k=1}^{N-1}\frac1k \xrightarrow{\ N\to\infty\ } \infty,\]
    the harmonic series diverging. Since Lebesgue integrability of $f$ means
    $\int\lvert f\rvert\,d\lambda < \infty$, the function $f$ is not Lebesgue integrable.

  \item That theorem compares the two integrals for a \emph{bounded} function on a \emph{box},
    where the Riemann integral is proper. Here the domain $[1,\infty)$ is unbounded and the
    integral exists only as a limit $\lim_{R\to\infty}\int_1^R$, which is a different construction:
    it is allowed to exploit cancellation between the positive and negative humps, and it is that
    cancellation the absolute-value requirement forbids.
\end{enumerate}
\begin{remark}[Neither theory contains the other]
This is the standard corrective to \qt{Lebesgue generalises Riemann}. On a box the Lebesgue
integral really is the stronger notion. Once improper integrals enter, the two constructions
answer different questions, and $\int_0^\infty \sin(x)/x\,dx = \pi/2$ is the famous instance of the
same phenomenon. In practice one keeps both: the Lebesgue integral for limits and for
several-variable arguments, the improper Riemann integral for conditionally convergent ones.
\end{remark}
\end{exercisesolution}

\begin{exercisesolution}[Solution of \cref{ex:jordan_null_products}]
% Generator: Claude Opus 5 (High)
Throughout, unwind the definition in \cref{rem:lebesgue_vs_jordan_null}: $X$ is Jordan null means
that for every $\varepsilon > 0$ there are \emph{finitely many} intervals
$I_1,\dots,I_N \subset \mathbb{R}$ with
\[X \subseteq \bigcup_{k=1}^N I_k \qquad\text{and}\qquad \sum_{k=1}^N \lvert I_k\rvert < \varepsilon.\]
Both parts are proved by covering the product with the products of such a cover; what differs is
only how the total area is estimated.

% Correction: Claude Opus 5 (Extra), 2026-08-13 --- the two parts were run as \textbf{(a)} /
% \textbf{(b)} paragraph openers against a statement that enumerates them. Mirrored properly.
\begin{enumerate}[label=\textbf{(\alph*)}]
  \item Let $\varepsilon > 0$ and put $\delta := \sqrt{\varepsilon}$. Choose intervals
    $I_1,\dots,I_N$ covering $X$ with $\sum_k\lvert I_k\rvert < \delta$. Then
    \[X \times X \ \subseteq\ \bigcup_{j=1}^N\bigcup_{k=1}^N I_j \times I_k,\]
    a cover by $N^2$ rectangles, still finitely many. Their total area factorises:
    \[\sum_{j=1}^N\sum_{k=1}^N \lvert I_j\rvert\,\lvert I_k\rvert
      \ =\ \left(\sum_{j=1}^N \lvert I_j\rvert\right)\!\left(\sum_{k=1}^N \lvert I_k\rvert\right)
      \ <\ \delta^2 = \varepsilon .\]
    Since $\varepsilon$ was arbitrary, $X\times X$ is Jordan null.

  \item Let $\varepsilon > 0$ and choose $I_1,\dots,I_N$ covering $X$ with
    $\sum_k\lvert I_k\rvert < \varepsilon$, with no square root this time. Then
    \[X \times [0,1] \ \subseteq\ \bigcup_{k=1}^N I_k \times [0,1],\]
    again a finite cover by rectangles, of total area
    \[\sum_{k=1}^N \lvert I_k\rvert \cdot 1 \ =\ \sum_{k=1}^N\lvert I_k\rvert \ <\ \varepsilon .\]
\end{enumerate}

\begin{remark}[Where the two parts differ, and what the difference is telling you]
\label{rem:jordan_null_products}
The two estimates are $\delta^2$ and $\delta\cdot1$, and the exponent is the whole story: the
first factor's total length enters \emph{linearly} in both, while the second factor contributes
its own total size as a multiplier.

That is why \textbf{(b)} works with a factor that is emphatically \emph{not} null. Being null in
$\mathbb{R}$ is not required of $[0,1]$; being \emph{bounded} is, and that is all. The same proof
gives $X \times B$ Jordan null for any bounded $B \subseteq \mathbb{R}$, with the total area
bounded by $\varepsilon \cdot \operatorname{diam}(B)$.

Drop boundedness and the statement fails: $X \times \mathbb{R}$ is not Jordan null for any
non-empty $X$, and not for the interesting reason. Jordan null sets are bounded by definition
(a finite cover by intervals is bounded), so $X \times \mathbb{R}$ is disqualified before any
area is computed. Compare \cref{rem:lebesgue_vs_jordan_null}: the Lebesgue notion, allowing
countable covers, does call $\{0\}\times\mathbb{R}$ null, and this is one of the cleanest places
to see the two notions come apart.
\end{remark}
\end{exercisesolution}

\begin{exercisesolution}[Solution of \cref{ex:kobel_ball_minus_lattice}]
% Generator: Claude Opus 5 (High)
Two facts do all the work: $M$ is Jordan measurable, and $f$ is continuous and bounded. The
criterion quoted with \cref{def:riemann_integral_rn} then applies.

\textbf{$M$ is bounded}, being a subset of $\overline{B}_2(0)$.

\textbf{The removed set is finite.} Write $L := \{x \in \mathbb{R}^4 \mid x_i \in \mathbb{Z}\}
= \mathbb{Z}^4$ and $S := L \cap \overline{B}_2(0)$, so that $M = \overline{B}_2(0)\setminus S$. A
lattice point $x \in S$ has $\sum_i x_i^2 \le 4$ with each $x_i$ an integer, so each $|x_i| \le 2$
and $S \subseteq \{-2,-1,0,1,2\}^4$. In particular $S$ is finite.

\textbf{$M$ is Jordan measurable.} A single point is a Jordan null set (cover it by one cube of
side $\varepsilon$), hence Jordan measurable, and a finite union of Jordan measurable sets is
Jordan measurable, so $S$ is. The closed ball is Jordan measurable --- its boundary is the sphere
$\partial B_2(0)$, a Jordan null set, so
\cref{thm:jordan_measurability_boundary_criterion} applies. Then
$M = \overline{B}_2(0)\setminus S$ is Jordan measurable by
\cref{prop:algebra_jordan_measurable}, and since $\mu(S) = 0$ we even get
$\mu(M) = \mu(\overline{B}_2(0))$.

\textbf{$f$ is continuous and bounded on $\overline{M}$.} It is a composition of polynomials, the
square root and $\cos$: the map $x \mapsto |x|^2$ is a polynomial, $t \mapsto t^{3/2}$ is
continuous on $[0,\infty)$, and $\cos$ is continuous on $\mathbb{R}$. So $f$ is continuous on all
of $\mathbb{R}^4$, in particular on $\overline{M} = \overline{B}_2(0)$, and
$\lvert f\rvert \le 1$ everywhere because it is a cosine.

\textbf{Conclusion.} $M$ is Jordan measurable and $f$ is continuous on $\overline M$, so $f$ is
Riemann integrable over $M$.
\begin{remark}[What the exercise is actually testing]
Neither the dimension $4$ nor the elaborate formula for $f$ matters; both are there to discourage
computation. The formula is chosen so that $f$ is manifestly continuous but has no closed-form
integral, and the removed set is chosen to look infinite --- $\mathbb{Z}^4$ is --- while being
finite \emph{after intersection with the ball}. Establishing that is the one step of the problem
that is not bookkeeping.

Note also what the removal does \emph{not} affect. Deleting a Jordan null set changes neither
Jordan measurability nor the value of the integral, so
$\int_M f = \int_{\overline{B}_2(0)} f$. It is precisely this insensitivity that fails for
countable removals: $[0,1]\setminus\mathbb{Q}$ is not Jordan measurable at all
(\cref{rem:lebesgue_vs_jordan_null}).
\end{remark}
\end{exercisesolution}

\begin{exercisesolution}[Solution of \cref{ex:kobel_diffeomorphic_jordan_measure}]
% Generator: Claude Opus 5 (High)
The answer is \textbf{(a)}, $\mu(V) < \infty$.

A diffeomorphism $\Phi$ carrying $U$ onto $V$ is in particular continuous, and $U$ is compact
(closed and bounded in $\mathbb{R}^n$, \cref{thm:heine_borel}). A continuous image of a compact set
is compact, so $V$ is compact, hence bounded, and a bounded Jordan measurable set has finite
measure.

Options \textbf{(b)}, \textbf{(c)} and \textbf{(d)} all assert something about the \emph{size} of
$\mu(V)$, and a diffeomorphism controls nothing of the kind. The change-of-variables formula
(\cref{thm:change_of_variables}) gives
$\mu(V) = \int_U \lvert\det \Jac\Phi\rvert$, and the Jacobian determinant is at the diffeomorphism's
disposal: the dilation $\Phi(x) := \lambda x$ turns $\mu(U)=1$ into $\mu(V) = \lambda^n$, which can
be made equal to, greater than, or less than $1$ at will. So all three fail, and they fail for the
same reason.
\end{exercisesolution}

\begin{exercisesolution}[Solution of \cref{ex:kobel_layer_cake}]
% Generator: Claude Opus 5 (High)
The answer is \textbf{(a)}, the \newterm{layer-cake formula}
\[\int_\Omega f = \int_0^{\max f}\mu\big(\{x \in \Omega \mid f(x) > t\}\big)\,dt .\]
For a positive step function the identity is a rearrangement of a finite sum. Let $f$ take the
distinct positive values $c_1 < \dots < c_N$ on Jordan measurable pieces $A_1,\dots,A_N$
partitioning $\Omega$, so that $\int_\Omega f = \sum_k c_k\,\mu(A_k)$. For $t \in
[c_{k-1},c_k)$ the superlevel set $\{f > t\}$ is exactly $A_k \cup \dots \cup A_N$, whose measure
is $\sum_{j \ge k}\mu(A_j)$. Hence
\[\int_0^{\max f}\mu(\{f>t\})\,dt
  = \sum_{k=1}^{N}(c_k - c_{k-1})\sum_{j\ge k}\mu(A_j)
  = \sum_{j=1}^{N}\mu(A_j)\sum_{k\le j}(c_k-c_{k-1})
  = \sum_{j=1}^N c_j\,\mu(A_j),\]
with $c_0 := 0$, the middle step being an exchange of the order of summation and the last a
telescoping sum. The two sides agree.

Of the alternatives, \textbf{(c)} is zero for all but finitely many $t$ and so integrates to $0$;
\textbf{(b)} and \textbf{(d)} use \emph{sub}level sets, which run the wrong way --- their integral
is $\mu(\Omega)\max f - \int_\Omega f$, not $\int_\Omega f$.
\begin{remark}[Why the layer-cake formula is worth remembering]
It computes an integral by slicing the \emph{range} rather than the domain, which is exactly the
move that separates the Lebesgue integral from the Riemann integral
(\cref{sec:lebesgue_digression}). Here it is proved for step functions only, but that is the
general case in disguise: the Lebesgue integral of a non-negative measurable function is
\emph{defined} by this formula, with $\mu$ the Lebesgue measure and the right-hand side an
improper Riemann integral of a monotone function of $t$.
\end{remark}
\end{exercisesolution}
